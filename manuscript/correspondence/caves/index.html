Earth

Where the mountain climbs toward the sky and belongs to air and fire, the cave is its opposite, the feminine hollow set into the body of the Earth. It is the most fitting form within the landscape for the Earth Mother, signifying the womb and the secret place where life takes shape.

The Womb of the Earth

The cave is a place of gestation, mirroring the alchemist's athanor, the vessel in which spirit takes on form, turns into matter, and at last passes out into a new world. That passage is read through the cave mouth, understood as a vaginal opening or vesica piscis. The same idea echoes in the Arabian Nights, where Ali Baba opens the hidden door with the cry of "Open Sesame!"; the bursting sesame pod stands for both the buried wealth of the Earth and a frank image of fertility.

Places of Power and Refuge

Because caves gather and hold the energy of the Earth, they have always been potent settings for magickal and religious rites. The Hellfire Caves at High Wycombe in England, the old seat of the Dashwood family, are one well-known example of a subterranean home for occult practice. A cave can shelter as a refuge or confine as a prison. It is the secret opposite of the visible mountain, hidden and hard to find, yet the two share a natural kinship, since cave systems so often wind through the heart of the mountains themselves. Both are felt to sit at the center of the world, an idea carried by the omphalos.

Birthplace of Gods

Just as the gods were said to dwell on the highest peaks, they were also born within caves. Zeus came into the world in a cave on Mount Ida in Crete, and when that cave was excavated it was found heaped with offerings to him. Lao Tzu, father of Taoist thought, was likewise born in a cave, as was the pre-Christian sun god Mithras, whose worship was carried out underground in the privacy of caverns. Even the stable of his successor, Jesus, was cut from rock, another divine birth set within stone. Some Native American traditions hold that caves were the very wombs in which the first humans were formed.

Sacred and Secret Places

Caves rank among the earliest sacred sites, and they appear across the world in both myth and fact. Aladdin's cave, holding the lamp and its djinn, casts the cavern as a storehouse of priceless secrets. The painted caves of Lascaux in the Perigord region of France hold treasures of another order: walls covered with horses, cattle, the long-extinct auroch, wild cats, birds, and fish, their pigments still vivid after some seventeen thousand years because no daylight ever reached them. Their very inaccessibility made caves ideal for hidden rites and for concealment, as with the Dead Sea Scrolls found high on a cliff face. Offering privacy, the cave became the forerunner of the temple, and many shrines carved into mountain rock, such as the Ajanta caves of India, are in effect man-made caverns. Pyramids carry the same logic, their imposing shells hiding a burial chamber within.

Tombs and Rebirth

The cave as a place of death and return appears wherever the dead are laid to rest in the Earth. One of the oldest known ritual burials in western Europe, some thirty thousand years old, was uncovered in the Paviland Caves of South Wales: a young man dusted in red ochre, the pigment likely a sign of blood and therefore of life, laid with ivory rods, shells, and the skull of a mammoth, marking him as a chieftain or other person of standing. Burial mounds, tumuli, and barrows the world over repeat the same gesture, sealing the dead inside a womb-like chamber to await rebirth.

Inner Knowledge

Taken together, mountain and cave teach that understanding rises not only from the outer world but from within. Plato pictured the human condition as life in an underground cavern, a single light behind us throwing our shadows onto the wall ahead; wisdom begins when we grasp that those flickering shapes are not the world itself but mere illusion, what Buddhist teaching calls the veil of Maya.
