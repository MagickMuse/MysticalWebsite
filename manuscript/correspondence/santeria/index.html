🎵Santería

Santeria is a rapidly expanding African Diaspora spiritual tradition whose name literally refers to devotion to the saints. The saints of Santeria, however, may or may not be who they superficially appear to be.

Origins

The roots of Santeria lie mainly in Yorubaland, now within the boundaries of modern Nigeria. During the slavery era, vast numbers of Yorubans were kidnapped and transported to the Western Hemisphere to labor on sugar plantations in Cuba alongside slaves from other African nations.

The Yoruba carried their indigenous spirits, known as the orishas, with them. However, in Cuba, they were forbidden to practice their own religion and were instead baptized as Roman Catholics. They received minimal religious instruction, but they were given chromolithographs of saints, whom they were encouraged to venerate.

Some became exclusively and devoutly Catholic; others, seeking to maintain devotion to ancestral spirits, engaged in subterfuge. They looked at chromolithographs and recognized orishas where they were intended to see canonized saints.

Syncretism: The Classic Example

The classic example involves the beloved orisha Shango and the virgin martyr Saint Barbara. Many chromolithographs of Barbara depict her with a lightning-struck tower. Shango is associated with lightning. When his devotees saw images of Barbara, their primary focus was on the lightning, not on the pretty young girl in the picture.

Shango was thus identified with Saint Barbara, and images of the saint were used to represent Shango. On her feast day, Shango's devotees celebrated their orisha in the guise of the saint, referring to Shango as Saint Barbara.

Evolution of Syncretism

This process of identification and syncretism is fairly transparent for the first generation. However, religious and cultural traditions are passed down from parent to child. Because it was not safe to discuss syncretism openly, over the centuries, saints and orishas blended, at least for some people.

Different Perspectives:

  • Some devotees consider the saints merely masks for the orishas whose original identities are not forgotten. The masking of orishas as saints is understood as necessary because of historical circumstances, but the two types of spiritual being are considered distinct.
  • For others, the individual identities of saint and orisha eventually merged, creating one blended spiritual being. Thus Shango and Saint Barbara are understood as being truly one, with various types of explanations and rationales for why this is so.
  • The word "saint" may be used exclusively, instead of orisha. Furthermore, orishas who may have had human incarnations (like Shango, who was once a king of a Yoruban city-state) may be considered saints by any definition of the word.

Catholic Practitioners

Complicating the situation are those who do not perceive themselves as Santerians, but as exclusively Roman Catholic. They deny the validity of the orishas, even though their veneration of saints is tinged with Santeria practices. Thus, those who deny any connection with Santeria may simultaneously favor images of Saint Barbara dressed in red and riding a white horse: associations that derive squarely from Shango's mythos. These devotees are not Santerians, but many Santeria practices have been absorbed into Caribbean folk religion.

Lucumi Movement

There are also devotees of Santeria who perceive that the era of orishas forced to masquerade as Christian saints is over. They choose to discard the masks, preferring African images. Thus the orisha Yemaya, Queen of the Sea, is shown as a black woman or mermaid rather than as the Virgin Mary. These devotees may also reject the name Santeria, with its direct reference to saints, in favor of Lucumi, a Yoruba name.

Brazilian Tradition: Candomble

Vast numbers of enslaved Yorubans were also transported to Portuguese-speaking Brazil, where Candomble, a similar religious tradition, evolved. The Portuguese spelling is orixa (pronounced: or-ee-sha).

Depending on location and tradition, different saints may represent different orishas. Thus, in Brazil, Saint Barbara is usually syncretized to the orisha Oya (Yansan) rather than Shango. The orisha Eshu-Elegbara may be represented by Saint Anthony of Padua, Saint Peter, or the Anima Sola.

The Orishas

Orishas are powerful spirits who rule over various aspects of nature and human experience. Each Orisha corresponds to a Catholic saint: Changó (thunder, passion, justice) with Saint Barbara; Yemayá (ocean, motherhood) with Our Lady of Regla; Oshún (rivers, love, fertility) with Our Lady of Charity; and Obatalá (wisdom, purity) with Our Lady of Mercy. Practitioners develop relationships with specific Orishas based on their life path and spiritual needs.

Initiation and Priesthood

Santería has a structured hierarchy of initiation. Practitioners may receive elekes (sacred necklaces) representing particular Orishas, undergo the ceremony of receiving their Warriors (Elegguá, Ogún, Ochosi, and Osun), or pursue full initiation as a priest or priestess (santero/santera). The deepest level of initiation involves "making ocha," receiving one's ruling Orisha in an intensive week-long ceremony.

Divination Systems

Santería employs sophisticated divination methods, particularly the diloggún (cowrie shell reading) and the Table of Ifá used by babalawos (high priests of Orula). These systems provide guidance, reveal spiritual needs, and prescribe offerings or remedies called ebbó.

Florida Water

This cologne, a blend of citrus, spices, and floral notes, plays an important role in Santería practice. It's used for spiritual cleansing, cooling heated situations, offerings to certain Orishas, and preparing ritual space. Its refreshing scent and purifying properties make it a staple on many altars.

Living Tradition

Santería remains a vibrant, growing tradition practiced throughout the Caribbean, Latin America, and increasingly worldwide. It offers a path of spiritual development through relationship with the Orishas and connection to ancestral wisdom.
