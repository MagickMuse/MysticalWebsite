Etymology

The word Vodou literally means "spirit" in the Fon language of West Africa. When the devotees of these spirits, adherents of traditional, indigenous African religions, were transported to the Western Hemisphere as slaves, they desperately and fervently attempted to maintain their religious practices. However, this was forbidden by law. Instead, slaves were baptized en masse, typically given new names, and ordered to practice a new religion.

Origins and Development

Voudon (also spelled Vodou, Voodoo, or Vodun) emerged in Haiti through the fusion of West African spiritual traditions with elements of Catholicism. Enslaved peoples from various African nations, particularly those practicing Fon, Ewe, and Kongo religions, preserved their ancestral beliefs by associating African spirits with Catholic saints, creating a resilient syncretic tradition.

Typically they were not taught to read or given extensive religious instruction, but they were given holy cards or lithographs of saints. In many cases, devotees recognized their own forbidden spirits in these images. For example, slaves were forbidden to worship the Dahomean snake spirit Damballah. But when they saw an image of Saint Patrick inevitably portrayed with snakes, not knowing Patrick's legend, they recognized the image as simultaneously evoking Damballah. Just as the slave had received a name change, so did Damballah.

In this way, Vodou, a new syncretic religious tradition, was born in Haiti. It was not a secret; authorities were aware that the slaves were evolving new traditions that were heretical from a Christian standpoint. However, up to a point, the practice was ignored and tolerated. Outsiders heard repeated use of the word Vodou, which eventually became the name of the faith. Vodou may also be spelled Vodoun.

The Lwa

Central to Voudon practice are the Lwa (also spelled Loa), spirits who serve as intermediaries between humanity and Bondye, the supreme creator. The Lwa are not gods themselves but powerful spirits, each with distinct personalities, preferences, and domains. Major Lwa include Papa Legba (guardian of crossroads), Erzulie Freda (love and beauty), Baron Samedi (death and resurrection), and Damballah (serpent father of creation).

Divine Possession

Unlike many Western magical traditions, Voudon actively seeks spirit possession. During ceremonies, the Lwa may "mount" or "ride" devotees, temporarily inhabiting their bodies. This is considered a great honor, the possessed person becomes a vessel through which the Lwa can communicate, heal, and interact with the community. Drumming, dancing, and offerings facilitate this sacred communion.

Offerings and Service

Practitioners maintain ongoing relationships with the Lwa through service (sèvis). Each spirit has specific preferences: particular foods, drinks, colors, and ritual items. Altars are maintained, offerings given, and songs sung to honor these relationships. This reciprocal exchange forms the foundation of Voudon practice.

New Orleans Voodoo

Following the Haitian revolution (1791-1804), the Vodou-inspired uprising of slaves who threw off their shackles, many devotees of Vodou migrated to New Orleans. Here, the religious tradition continued to evolve, absorbing other magical, cultural, and spiritual influences and becoming New Orleans Voodoo.

New Orleans Voodoo absorbed influences from:

  • Haitian Vodou practitioners who migrated after the revolution
  • French Catholic traditions strong in Louisiana
  • Native American spiritual practices
  • African traditions from various regions
  • European folk magic

Spelling Distinctions

Practitioners in Haiti resent the spelling "Voodoo," as it evokes offensive Hollywood exploitation movies.

  • Vodou/Voudon: Refers to Haitian traditions
  • Voodoo: Refers specifically to the tradition practiced in New Orleans
  • Hoodoo: A related but distinct system of North American folk magic (not intrinsically religious, born in Protestant regions where saint veneration was discouraged)

Misconceptions

Hollywood has badly distorted Voudon, reducing a rich spiritual tradition to harmful stereotypes about "voodoo dolls" and malevolent sorcery. In reality, Voudon is a community-centered religion focused on healing, guidance, and maintaining harmony between the visible and invisible worlds.
