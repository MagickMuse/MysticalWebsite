Definition

The traditional religious definition of an altar is a structure on which votive offerings are placed. From a magickal perspective, an altar is a tableau or arrangement of specific articles carefully chosen for a purpose. An altar is a designated place for offerings to saints or other spirits, but it also serves as a focal point for worship. Altars can be found in buildings dedicated to worship, like churches, temples, or shrines. Altars can also be constructed at home for private devotions.

Energy Concentration

Crossroads and cemeteries are places of power because they allow energy extra space and opportunity to radiate. An altar achieves the opposite effect: the concentration of energy into a single focal point, the very center of the crossroads. Where thresholds let power disperse, an altar draws it inward and holds it.

Altars generally serve one or both of two core purposes:

  • Spirit communication -- By assembling specific objects, colors, and fragrances, a practitioner sends a targeted signal requesting a spirit's attention. The concentration of themed objects catches the spirit's notice and invites its presence. Because certain items are shared across multiple deities (roses, for example, appeal to Aphrodite, Juno, Kybele, and Maria Padilha, though each prefers different colors, numbers, or presentations), the more specific and detailed the tableau, the more precisely it calls the intended spirit.
  • Spell energy focus -- An altar can serve a spell rather than a spirit. Workings conducted over extended periods, such as nine-day spells, benefit from a concentrated area where the spell remains distinct from its surroundings, its boundaries and thresholds clearly defined. All objects involved in the working are arranged together in formation, reinforcing each other's energy.

Terminology Across Traditions

An altar may also be called an offering table. The word boveda, which literally means "a vault" in Spanish, refers to an altar in the Espiritismo and Santeria traditions. Mesa, which literally means "table" in Spanish, has the additional implication of "offering table" in Espiritismo circles. Mesa blanca, literally "white table," may also be used, referring to the white cloths that often cover such altars. The term mesa has become so popular that it may be used in Spiritualist circles that incorporate no other Spanish words.

What is an Altar?

An altar is your basic workbench for doing magick. It establishes a sanctuary in your home and provides a focal point for casting spells, performing rites and rituals, and meditating. You go there to temporarily leave the ordinary, everyday world behind and enter sacred space.

An altar can be a TV table draped with a beautiful cloth, an ornate antique cabinet, or anything in between. What matters is that it serves as your dedicated space for magickal work.

The Center of Practice

The altar stands at the heart of magickal work. It is not necessarily a religious shrine, though the powers used in magick connect to the core of every religion, offering a key to understanding magick, spirituality, and the nature of Deity. At its simplest, an altar is any flat surface where you can work with herbs and perform magickal rites.

While magick can and should be practiced anywhere as needed (outdoors is often preferred), indoor work benefits from consistency. Returning to the same space builds energetic resonance over time. This dedicated area need be nothing elaborate: a coffee table, a cleared dresser top, or a small table tucked into an inconspicuous corner all serve perfectly well.

Ideally, choose a location where objects can remain undisturbed for several days. Some spells require sustained work over that length of time, and breaking down your setup mid-working disrupts the energy.

Simplicity Over Expense

Many practitioners drape their altars with colored cloth, but this is not required. Expensive censers and gleaming silver candlesticks look impressive, yet they add nothing essential to your practice. A flat, plain surface, preferably wooden, is all you truly need.

If you wish to burn candles in recognition of higher powers, do so. If placing flowers as offerings to the gods (or however you view Deity) feels right, include them. What matters most is that your altar moves you. Magick works best when it is personally satisfying, so create a space that resonates with your spirit rather than following someone else's aesthetic.

Setting Up Your Altar

Many witches include at least one central altar in a sacred space. Some believe it's important to place their altars in the east, others prefer the north, but wherever you choose to put yours is fine, go with what feels right.

For practical purposes, setting the altar in the middle of your circle makes sense, especially if a number of people will join you for rituals. Everyone can gather around it easily, and a central candle placed on the altar can symbolize Spirit as the guiding force.

Permanent vs. Temporary Altars

You can set up a permanent altar if your living circumstances permit. If you don't have enough space or share your home with people who may not respect your altar, you can create a temporary one and dismantle it when you're finished. If you're lucky enough to have a nice outdoor area to work in, you might position an altar there, perhaps dedicating a special large stone to that purpose.

What Goes on Your Altar?

Witches usually set up something depicting the four elements:

  • Earth, Salt, stones, crystals
  • Water, Bowl of water, shells
  • Fire, Candle
  • Air, Incense, feathers

Some magicians display their wands, pentagrams, athames, and chalices openly. If you lay out your tools, be sure to include all four. You might also place:

  • Candles in handsome candleholders
  • Crystals
  • Statues of favorite deities
  • Flowers
  • Other meaningful objects

Many people store their magickal gear in their altars. A cabinet with drawers is perfect for stashing additional candles, incense, herbs, oracles, ritual clothing, and various spell ingredients.

Your altar should bring you a sense of peace, joy, harmony, and personal power. Choose only things that hold positive associations for you.

Attuning Your Altar to Your Purpose

The items and symbols you bring to your altar largely depend on the purpose of your magick and may change regularly:

  • Ancestor work, Include their photographs or personal effects
  • Love spell, Set a vase of roses there
  • Ostara, Spring flowers and painted eggs
  • Lughnasadh, Cornucopia of fruits and vegetables
  • Yule, Evergreen sprigs and pinecones

Cleanse and purify all things you plan to use for magickal workings or place on your altar beforehand. An easy way is to light incense (cedar or sandalwood) and pass the objects through the smoke.

Mobile Altars

You can make a mobile altar to take along when you travel. Transform a small box into a portable altar by decorating it with magickal images or packing a pretty scarf to drape over it. Fill the box with basics, tea light candles, incense cones, small crystals.

You can even put an altar on your smartphone or tablet. Photograph your home altar or choose meaningful images, and look at them whenever you want, wherever you go. Add peaceful music to set the mood.

Quarter Altars

To connect with the four elements and corners of creation, some witches erect four small altars within their sacred spaces. Position one at each compass point:

DirectionElementSuggested Objects
EastAirYellow candle, feathers, incense, wind chimes
SouthFireRed candle, red stones (bloodstone, carnelian, ruby), dragon figurine
WestWaterBlue candle, shells, bowl of water
NorthEarthGreen candle, stones or crystals, potted plants, marble or ceramic statue

An easy way to assign each altar to an element is to cover it with a cloth of the element's color. You might also consider placing a picture of the archangel who presides over each direction: East/Raphael, South/Michael, West/Gabriel, North/Uriel.

Altars for Saints and Spirits

An altar may be lavish or elaborate. Traditional Vodou altars may take up an entire room, with each item carefully chosen. Nothing placed on an altar is random. Altars may also be extremely modest. The simplest, dedicated to ancestral spirits, display only a single candle and an uncovered glass or dish of water.

Altars may be erected temporarily, for instance, for the duration of an appeal to a saint. They may also be left standing permanently. They may be placed out in the open for everyone to see, or be discreetly placed within a cabinet or behind a curtain, screen, or other room partition.

An altar for a saint usually includes an image of that saint. Those uncomfortable with graven images can use a saint's attributes instead. For instance, Joan of Arc may be represented by a sword. Other objects associated with the saint can also be added, creating a tableau. An altar is a place to put offerings to a saint, but the altar itself is an offering.

Altars as Communication

An altar is also a vehicle of communication. When you wish to request a favor from a saint, often the first step is either a journey to a preestablished altar or the construction of a home altar. This altar is the equivalent of a line of communication, a connection between holy being and devotee.

Altars offer limitless opportunities for individual creative and spiritual expression. The goal is to create something spiritually powerful and aesthetically beautiful. (The place in a traditional Russian home where the household altar is located is called "the beautiful corner.")

Sharing Altars

Most saints play well with each other, as well as with sacred beings from other spiritual traditions. A single altar can serve an individual's personal needs. All the saints and spirits a person venerates can be served by that one altar. Statues or other images can be placed side by side or otherwise arranged to suit the individual's spiritual situation.

There are exceptions, however. A few saints demand their own altars or will only share space with very specific saints. Certain saints are traditionally associated with unique altars or may expect certain types of objects to appear on altars.

Altar Forms Beyond the Tabletop

Altars extend far beyond furniture surfaces. A shadow box altar can be maintained discreetly within a cabinet or closed box. Miniature altars fit inside old cigar boxes or matchboxes.

Outdoor altars are equally valid. A garden, window box, or flowerpot can hold a living Earth altar. Roadside shrines erected at the site of fatal accidents are public altars. In the French Caribbean, altars are created in sheltered places within tree trunks where passers-by can leave offerings of flowers and candles. Spells dedicated to sea deities may instruct the practitioner to dig a shallow pit in the sand and burn candles within it. That hole in the sand becomes the altar.

An altar also serves as a method of demarcating sacred space. Spells of petition for the Brazilian spirit Maria Padilha invariably begin with directions to lay black and red cloths on the ground, effectively establishing the spell's boundaries. Similar to feng shui, articles and objects are arranged to create a desired energy transformation.

Ancestor Altars

An ancestor altar is a designated place to reflect on and commune with the ancestors. The ancestors do not dwell in the altar; they dwell more in the practitioner's body than anywhere else. But over time, the altar tends to feel charged with their presence. Pay attention to how you move through the space around it. How does your choreography shift when you walk past it? What happens to your breath?

The Altar as a Mirror

The state of an ancestor altar frequently mirrors the practitioner's inner state. When it is clean and ordered, the mind tends to feel the same. When it is cluttered, confusion and emotional fog often follow. Cleaning and rearranging the altar when something feels off, even if the cause is unclear, is a reliable first step toward clarity.

Physical Practice, Material Results

Practices that are purely mental tend to produce purely mental results. An ancestor veneration practice built only on thought may shift the quality of a practitioner's inner life, but it is unlikely to produce changes in material circumstances. A physical altar grounds the work in the body and the home, giving the ancestors greater influence over the practical matters of daily life.

Altar Placement and Protection

The position of an altar within a home affects how it functions. One traditional placement seats guests directly across from the altar, giving the ancestors an unobstructed view of those who enter. Over time, this arrangement allows the ancestors to observe behavior, assess intentions, and transmit their judgment back to the practitioner as instinct. What might seem like an inexplicable change of feeling toward someone can be the accumulated perception of the ancestors, cutting through social performance and making itself known through the body.

Tradition Rules vs. Intuition

Some traditions have specific rules about ancestor altars: no salt on the altar, no altar in the bedroom, no approach after sex. If a practitioner is working within one of these traditions, especially where lineage ancestors are involved, following those expectations is advisable. But for those working outside a specific tradition, there is no obligation to adopt another lineage's rules. Follow the body instead.

Since the ancestors communicate through physical sensation, bodily response is a reliable guide when building or tending an altar. An object that fills the body with peace and warmth when placed there belongs. An object that produces a faint unease probably does not. The ancestors are generally forgiving of honest mistakes, especially where the relationship is relatively new. They are invested in their descendants' wellbeing and less likely to take offense over a misplaced object than over a long-standing wound in the relationship that has gone unaddressed.

On Supplies: Keep It Lean

In consumer culture, it is easy to conflate acquiring materials with beginning a practice. Purchasing things is neither necessary nor sufficient for a fulfilling ancestor veneration practice. Buying slowly and deliberately, favoring objects made with intention over mass-produced equivalents, and working with things already owned or handmade are all sound approaches. The ideas that follow are starting points, not requirements.

Fire

Fire carries a strong association with prayer across many traditions, including Christianity, Judaism, Islam, Hinduism, Buddhism, and Chinese folk religion. The votive fire can take many forms: candles, oil lamps, and other sources of sustained flame. In espiritismo, the common refrain luz y progreso ("light and progress") names the dual function fire serves at the altar.

Glass-encased seven-day candles are widely available and work well for ancestor altars; plain white is traditional, though any candle can be personalized with images, names, or markings. Oil lamps are used in Orthodox Christianity and offer the advantage of continuous burning: olive oil is added as needed, and materials can be infused into the oil to match the intent of the work. Orris root, dried rosemary, or a Miraculous Medal, for example, can be added to a lamp dedicated specifically to female ancestors.

Olive oil carries particular resonance in this context. The olive tree is notoriously slow to cultivate, with some varieties requiring up to twelve years before bearing fruit. A Sicilian proverb captures this perfectly: Olivari di tò nannu, cèusi di tò patri, vigna tò, "Your grandfather's olive tree, your father's mulberry tree, your grapevine." The olive becomes a symbol of the long-term planning and sacrifice made by those who came before so that those who follow might benefit. When fire is not safe or practical, electric candles are a valid substitute: they consume energy and emit light and warmth just as any other candle does.

Water

If fire symbolizes prayer, water represents the medium through which spiritual energy moves. Water is fundamental to life and to the body: the adult human body is roughly 60 percent water, and wounds heal more effectively when kept moist. Practitioners who are highly spiritually sensitive often find that hydration becomes especially important during periods of increased spiritual contact.

In certain branches of espiritismo, the bóveda (vault or altar) includes glasses of water, typically one, seven, or nine. When multiple glasses are used, the largest represents the practitioner's own consciousness, their Holy Guardian Angel, or God; the remaining glasses represent individual spirits or groups of spirits.

Water on an altar requires maintenance. Over time it will go murky, and murky water disrupts the connection it is meant to conduct. When this happens, the glass should be emptied, cleaned, and refilled with fresh water. The clarity of the water reflects and affects the clarity of the connection.

Cloth

Cloth is used in spiritist traditions worldwide. Espiritistas typically advise placing a white cloth beneath the bóveda. Neapolitan mediums working at Fontanelle Cemetery traditionally offered white embroidery to their spirits as thanksgiving after a favor was granted. In Santería, white cloth and cotton are understood to be cooling, in the same category as cascarilla and shea butter.

Cloth holds a particular resonance for female ancestors. Across preindustrial cultures, textile production was predominantly women's work: spinning, weaving, knitting, and sewing occupied a significant portion of most women's daily lives. Anthropologist Judith Brown observed that cross-culturally, preindustrial communities relied on women for labor that was compatible with the demands of childcare, characterized by tasks that were repetitive, easily interrupted and resumed, non-dangerous to children, and home-based. Elizabeth Wayland Barber, in Women's Work: The First 20,000 Years, elaborated this point, noting that until the industrial age, textiles offered women the primary socially acceptable means of earning income. Crucially, the same conditions that made textile work compatible with childcare, the repetition, the interrupted rhythm, the narrow focus of the hands while the mind floats free, are also the conditions of trance induction. Many female ancestors were likely divining or communing with spirits at the same moment they were nursing children and earning a living.

This is the tradition the spinning Fates inhabit: the Norse Norns, Greek Moirai, Roman Parcae, and several Slavic deities, all depicted spinning the destinies of the living. Placing a cloth on an ancestor altar is a gesture that reaches all the way back to that thread.

Pictures of Ancestors

Photographs of the ancestors are a natural centerpiece for the ancestor altar. Images that show ancestors as young, healthy, and joyous are generally preferable when available. Some practitioners advise against using photographs that also include people who are still living; whether to follow this guidance depends on cultural background and personal discretion.

Holy Images

Images of holy powers can be incorporated into the ancestor altar in two ways: those sacred to the practitioner personally, and those that held significance for specific ancestors. A great-grandparent's patron saint, for instance, is worth including even if the practitioner holds no personal devotion to that being; the ancestors themselves may appreciate the presence. These images can be modest (a holy card, a medal) or elaborate (a statue, a painting).

Holy powers from the ancestors' own spiritual traditions that hold a particular affinity for the dead are also worth including, even without knowing which specific ancestors were devoted to them.

Memento Mori

Memento mori is the practice of reflecting on mortality. In Western art, it commonly appears as skulls and hourglasses, but the category extends to any symbol that speaks personally of death and impermanence, especially those encountered in childhood or that carry strong cultural resonance.

Colonial American gravestones offer a rich visual vocabulary in this tradition. Common carved motifs include:

  • Mortality symbols: winged skulls, bones, scythes, coffins
  • Resurrection imagery: the tree of life, birds with fruit, celestial bodies, angels, hearts
  • Other: urns, trees

Verses inscribed on these stones function as direct addresses from the dead to the living:

Stranger, stop and cast an eye
As you are now, so once was I
As I am now, so you will be
Remember Death and follow me
Death is a debt
To Nature due
That I have paid
And so must you

Any imagery that activates a genuine sense of mortality can serve as memento mori on the altar.

Working the Ancestor Altar

An established ancestor altar is not only a devotional space; it is also a site for active magickal work.

Cleansing

The ancestors are capable of removing unwanted spiritual energies. In espiritismo, a common method involves taking a few splashes of Florida water (or a similar perfume) in the hands and passing them through the air surrounding the body from head to feet, with particular attention to psychically sensitive areas such as the back of the neck. This can be done before prayer or any working at the altar. See the Cleansing entry for additional methods.

Setting Lights

Beyond votive lights kept solely for the benefit of the ancestors, candles dressed with oils and herbs or carved with sacred symbols can be set on the altar for the ancestors to bless and oversee as they burn. Practitioners of candle magick often read the wax residue and smoke patterns left after burning to interpret the likely outcome of the working.

Blessing Oils, Charms, and Tools

Magickal objects, including condition oils, mojo bags, and other prepared tools, can be left on the ancestor altar overnight or for a magically significant number of days for the ancestors to empower. Commercially prepared oils or colognes can be personalized by adding a pinch of frankincense or burning a candle over them while they rest on the altar.

This practice extends naturally beyond objects with obvious magickal applications. New tarot decks, sacred beverages, medicine, legal paperwork, and other items can all be placed on the altar before first use. Earnings can be placed there before spending or depositing, with prayers for continued abundance.

Offerings

Offerings to the ancestors are placed directly on the altar. See the Ancestor Offerings section for a full treatment of what to offer and how.

Spirit Placement Preferences

Different spirits prefer different locations for their altars. Water spirits frequently favor the bathroom as the most watery room in the house. A mermaid shrine belongs there rather than in a space considered more traditionally spiritual. Love spirits prefer the bedroom, where they can supervise and stimulate activity. Intellectual spirits like Oya or Sarasvati prefer altars placed near books.

Objects as Altars

Objects may decorate an altar or serve as the altar itself. Watermelons are sacred to the Yoruba spirit Yemaya. A slice placed on an altar calls her, but a hollowed-out watermelon with candles inserted inside becomes the altar. Sacred woods such as sandalwood or aloes wood function the same way. Sometimes the deity is the wood: Hera was represented by the oak in Greece, while Diana was represented by that wood throughout Europe. A log segment given center stage, dressed with oil and adorned with ornaments and small candles, becomes both altar and offering.

The bed serves as the altar for many love spells. Sheets are sprinkled with powder, power objects are tucked beneath the mattress and pillows, botanicals hang overhead, and candles burn alongside.

The body itself serves as an altar in spells for healing, love, and protection. Oil and powder may be applied to the body in the same manner as to a candle. Henna and other body decoration transforms the body into a living altar. The act of dressing, applying cosmetics, jewelry, and ornaments, can be understood as dressing an altar that communicates with other beings and concentrates personal power.

Journal Prompts: The Altar

  1. Did any members of your family dedicate a special area to images of the divine or pictures of their ancestors? Did you ever think of this area as an altar?
  2. Have you encountered altars outside of churches and museums? When did you encounter them? How did they make you feel? How did you interact with them? If you have experienced many altars, which one stands out most in your memory? Why do you think that one seemed so special?
  3. Do you already have an ancestor altar?
    • If so: How do you feel when you stand or sit in front of it? Do you interact with it regularly? Are you satisfied with it, or does it feel like it could evolve? Is there anything on your altar that doesn't inspire you when you see it?
    • If not: Do you want to build one? Would you prefer one that is temporary or permanent? Simple or elaborate?
  4. How do you feel about the relationship between material items and spiritual realities? What attitudes toward items and shopping have you absorbed from consumer culture? If you have trained in or read deeply about a spiritual tradition, what attitudes toward the material world have you adopted from that tradition?
  5. What is the most beautiful image of death or the dead that you have ever seen?
  6. Do you currently have a magickal practice? Are there any workings you would like to try doing at your ancestor altar?

The Spell as Crossroads

Not every crossroads is a physical location. A formal magick spell is itself a crossroads where the inherent magickal energies of its components converge. The result of the spell is the symbiotic reaction to that convergence.
