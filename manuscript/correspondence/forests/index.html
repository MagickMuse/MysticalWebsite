🌿Nature🌳Trees

Once upon a time, vast primordial forests covered much of Earth. Human settlements were small clearings within forests; reaching another settlement required passing through forests. Forest was ever-present and familiar, not rare.

Witchcraft's Birthplace

Many consider forest witchcraft's birthplace, certainly where craft was nurtured. Dedicated solitary practitioners/spiritual seekers lived alone in forest, discovering powers of botanicals/mushrooms, learning to negotiate with animal powers and navigate Spirit World. Among Earth's first shamans/witches/healers, they shared skills/knowledge with those who ventured into forest. For these practitioners, forest was home, shrine, and medicine cabinet all in one.

Different Visions

The Wild Place: Forest is wild place not under human control, mysterious, uncontrollable realm where wild nature holds sway. Animals, trees, spirits reign supreme, not people. Only way for people to control this forest is to destroy it. Vision fills some with dread, others with awe. Many consider forest holy place, hallowed ground. Deep forests were/remain sacred shrines where people pay obeisance to Spirit World. Some consider eradication of Europe's forests form of spiritual warfare, equivalent of destroying temples/churches/shrines. The hedge divides forest from human settlements, realm of witches who mediate between domesticity and wilderness, human realm and those others not under human control.

The Dangerous Place: Vision commonly found in fairy tales, forest is dangerous place filled with ravenous beasts, evil malicious spirits, wild dangerous people. Murderers, thieves, outlaws live in forest; chief among these are witches. Folktale forests inhabited by ravenous cannibal witches. Some initially seem innocuous/helpful but that's part of trap: witch in Hansel and Gretel epitomizes this. Beyond dangers of getting lost or attacked by wild animals, can't trust humans encountered in wood. Benign little old ladies transform into evil killer witches lurking deep in woods.

The Place of Refuge: Historically people valued forest precisely because they could get lost in it. Sometimes "lost" is safest place to be. Despite tales of Baba Yaga and other cannibal witches, forest traditionally place of refuge. Many preferred taking chances with dangers in forest rather than those outside it.

Forest as Sanctuary

Over centuries many entered forest hoping to evade human persecution. Sometimes no choice: Pagan Nordic legal tradition decreed "outlaws" forbidden to live among people. Anyone free to kill them. Many found refuge in woods, some living solitary lives, others creating outlaw communities (à la Robin Hood). Similarly, lepers once lived in colonies in European woods, forbidden to come out.

Forest offered unique advantages: Not under human control (laws often ignored), great equalizer (inside forest, rank matters less than survival skills).

Historical Examples: Robin Hood's band in Sherwood Forest. The Maroons, escaped African slaves who established free communities in wild, remote, wooded places (Maroon derives from Spanish Cimarron: "wild," "unbroken," "untamed"). The Netotsi ("Men of the Woods"), escaped Romany slaves who found refuge in Carpathian maze of rocks/forests (two hundred thousand enslaved Romany freed in Romania 1855). WWII Partisans, 1941, when Nazis began mass executions of Polish Jews, some fled to Naliboki Forest forming combined refugee/resistance communities (eventually over one thousand people including women/children).

Christian Persecution: Among those seeking forest refuge were those refusing to accept new Christian faith. Some rejected spiritual precepts, preferring ancient ancestral traditions, priestesses/priests/devotees retreated into woods. When Christianity banished spell-casting/magical traditions, some practitioners sought privacy/independence in woods. These people eventually called witches, among those dangerous people fairy tales describe populating woods.

Forest Spirits and Witchcraft

Forests always associated with witchcraft, whether witches live there or visit. Forests where botanical supplies gathered and where one communed with wild forest spirits. Many spirits make home in forest, most famous witch-goddesses: Artemis, Cernunnos, Faunus, Cybele, and Baba Yaga.

Kybele: Mother of Witchcraft

Forest sometimes called mother of witchcraft. According to legend, goddess Kybele herself invented witchcraft in forest. Born unwanted female child, left abandoned/exposed to die in Anatolian woods. Leopards who discovered crying baby raised/nurtured her, feeding her on their milk. Kybele, original Catwoman, grew up in forest away from human contact, finally emerging as strong, smart, competent, independent, magically empowered woman. She discovered all necessary components of witchcraft in forest: botanicals (especially trees/roots), animals, spirits. Became healer, musician (inventing flute/percussion instruments), shaman, and first witch. Finally motivated to leave forest (temporarily!) to teach other women her newfound skills.

Natural Places of Worship

Human beings have always filled the natural world with spirit, sensing a divine life force within its rocks, stones, lakes, pools, valleys, and hills. Some of these sacred spaces lay within hunting territory, places blessed by the Gods as the site of a successful hunt, or spots where magickal herbs that might carry a shaman into the realms of the Gods could be found. The most potent and universal of these holy sites is the sacred grove.

The Sacred Grove

As a living feature of the landscape, the forest has always formed a truly magickal natural shrine, with the tree itself standing as an immediate object of worship. In the first century AD, Seneca observed:

"If you come upon a grove of old trees that have lifted up their crowns above the common height and shut out the light of the sky by the darkness of their interlacing boughs, you feel that there is a spirit in the place, so lofty is the wood, so lone the spot, so wondrous the thick unbroken shade."

Modern tree huggers follow a long line of people of all faiths and nationalities who revere the tree. Woods and forests, along with the cave, were among humanity's first dedicated places of worship. Anyone who has spent time in a forest or stumbled upon a natural grove understands why. As Virgil put it, the Gods seem to favor wild trees unsown by human hand. Such a place shares the features of the cave. The lofty canopy of a grove holds a similarly potent silence and stillness, broken only by an occasional breeze, the call of birds, or the sudden movement of an animal. There is a natural sanctity to these places that is utterly universal, something later builders of temples and cathedrals seem to have tried to emulate. Their buildings rest on columns that imitate tree trunks, rise into high domed ceilings like the forest canopy, and hold a sense of spaciousness within. Just as in a forest grove, the light inside a temple is dim. In a church the subdued glow filtering through stained glass mirrors sunshine gleaming softly through the dappled leaves of the grove.

Observers of the sacred groves and sacred trees of India have noted that they belong to a variety of cultural practices that helped society maintain an ecologically steady relationship with wild living resources.

Perhaps the best known tree and forest worshippers are the Druids. Their very name comes from old Celtic roots meaning both "tree" and "truth," joined with a sense of "vision," so a Druid is effectively described as one who knows the tree, or the truth. For Druids all trees were sacred, but the oak was the most important of all. Their groves were called Nemeton, places distinctly different from an ordinary forest, often fenced within a square enclosure surrounded by a ditch. The presiding deity was the Goddess Nemetona.

Sacred groves existed all across Europe. In the Caucasus mountains each community kept its grove much as a village might keep a church. Some groves were especially sanctified by their great age, and the trees within them were never to be cut down. In Scandinavia, every single tree in the grove at Old Uppsala was sacred. The concept of the Christmas tree most likely has its origins in the sacred groves of the Germanic tribes.

Tragically, many sacred groves were destroyed by adherents of the incoming Christian religion, anxious to show allegiance to their one jealous God against the many deities of the old order in which the Great Mother was prominent. Groves suffered especially because they were dedicated to Goddesses. In the fifth century AD the Emperor Theodosius was particularly zealous, issuing an edict that all sacred groves, some of them thousands of years old, should be cut down unless they could be shown to be compatible with Christianity. This is why churchyards so often hold a yew. The land was holy precisely because this tree of death and rebirth already stood there. The spirit of this mysterious and magickal tree must have reached even the sensibilities of those timber hungry Christians, since some of these yews survive to the present day, now thousands of years old.

Other groves famously dedicated to the Goddess include the celebrated site of Diana Nemorensis, "Diana of the Wood." Situated near the small town of Ariccia, the lake beside the grove was called "Diana's Mirror." A particular tree at the center of the grove was heavily guarded. No timber was to be taken from it except by the occasional runaway slave fortunate enough to reach it unharmed, who would then win the chance to fight for freedom.

The sacred grove of Dodona was the site of the most ancient Hellenistic oracle. The figurehead of the Argo, the ship Jason sailed in Greek myth, held oracular powers because it had been cut from timber at Dodona. This grove held a wealth of predictive tools. The future could be forecast by the sound of wind rustling through the leaves, and the priestesses there had the skill to understand the language of birds. People traveled from far and wide to consult the "doves," which was also the name given to these priestesses.

India is believed to hold somewhere in the region of fourteen thousand sacred groves, which never had to survive the ravages of an invading religion. Though they may not be formally protected, they continue to be used, dedicated to diverse deities and honored by local communities. In Japan the Shinto Torii that marks the boundary between sacred and profane space is as likely to stand before a naturally holy place, such as a pine forest, as before any built temple.
