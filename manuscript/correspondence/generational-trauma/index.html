Othala

Ancestral trauma is universal. Regardless of the privilege or oppression experienced in any given lifetime, every person has ancestors who suffered trauma, including its most extreme forms: war, slavery, genocide, and losses that cannot be fully imagined or named.

Trauma is psychic damage. It can result from a single, severely disturbing event, or from a moderately upsetting event that recurred over a sustained period. Its psychological symptoms include anxiety, depression, denial, and mental confusion. Physical symptoms include insomnia, fatigue, physical pain, and muscle tension. The perceived line between psychological and physical symptoms is, at best, blurry.

❤️
A Note on Scope

Ancestor veneration is not a substitute for professional mental and physical health care. It can, however, work in partnership with professional care to support healing of inherited trauma. Healing often requires a multi-modality approach.

Transgenerational Trauma

Trauma is commonly understood as isolated to the individual who experienced it. But trauma can also be transgenerational: passed from parent to child across generations, even to those who never met the person who first suffered it.

In 2015, Rachel Yehuda, Director of the Traumatic Stress Studies Division at the Mount Sinai School of Medicine, published findings from a study examining the genes of 32 Jewish Holocaust survivors who had been interned in Nazi concentration camps or subjected to torture. Her team also studied the genes of 22 children born to Holocaust survivors after the war.

Prior research had already established that children of Holocaust survivors were at higher risk for PTSD, depression, and anxiety, particularly when their parents showed similar symptoms. Yehuda's study found evidence that this inherited pattern was caused by epigenetics: not the genes themselves, but the biological mechanisms that modify how genes are expressed. This science is still relatively new and continues to be studied, but Yehuda's work has already helped many people in ancestral healing fields name and contextualize patterns they had observed in themselves and others.

Unresolved trauma, whether ancestral or personal, plays out in unconscious behavior: addictions, sleep disorders, and destructive patterns in intimate relationships. There is no universal solution. Gradually becoming aware of recurring patterns in life and relationships is often the first step toward healing.

Spiritual Bypassing

Trauma cannot truly be hidden, and ignoring it does not make it disappear. Some ancestors may have attempted silence out of love, believing they could spare their children suffering by not speaking of it. But the body holds what the mind forgets; the past continues to shape the present even without conscious acknowledgment.

A relevant concept in spiritual communities is spiritual bypassing, a term introduced in the early 1980s by John Welwood, a Buddhist teacher and psychotherapist. He defined it as "a tendency to use spiritual ideas and practices to sidestep or avoid facing unresolved emotional issues, psychological wounds, and unfinished developmental tasks." In practice, spiritual bypassing is deflecting into language of love, light, and positivity whenever engagement with real difficulty is required. The problem is that unaddressed trauma continues to operate regardless of spiritual practice or positive thinking. Spirituality and emotional work are not in opposition; they function best together.

Three Ways to Break the Cycle

There are three approaches available for ensuring that inherited wounds do not continue to pass forward to future generations: facing ancestral trauma, making art, and dying well.

Facing Ancestral Trauma

The first step is acknowledgment without urgency. Sitting with the reality of inherited trauma, without immediately trying to fix or resolve it, is itself a significant act. Rushing to resolution is another form of avoidance. Creating space for older generations to speak about their experiences can be valuable, but their boundaries must be respected. Willingness cannot be forced.

The second step is forgiveness.

Forgiveness is defined by psychologists as a conscious decision to release feelings of resentment toward someone who has caused harm. It has also been described as the decision to no longer expect any past other than the one that actually occurred.

This resonates with the concept of santosha from the Yoga Sutra of Patanjali, a collection of 196 aphorisms describing the theory and practice of yoga, compiled around 400 CE. Santosha, one component of the niyama limb (concerned with virtuous daily habits), is typically translated as "contentment" or "satisfaction." In practice, it means accepting things as they are, and releasing the stories that tell us something is fundamentally lacking, in ourselves, in others, or in life itself.

In ancestor veneration and spiritual practice broadly, life's imperfections can feel like obstacles. The instinct is to wish for better circumstances, better ancestors, a less complicated lineage. But the areas of greatest pain are often the areas of greatest growth. Like the alchemists, the task is to transmute lead into gold: to accept ancestors as they were, flawed in life and continuing to evolve after death, rather than wishing for a different inheritance.

Forgiveness does not require the other person to deserve it. It is for the one doing the forgiving. It creates space for peace, for new possibilities, for energy that has been held in resentment to be redirected.

Forgiveness is also not the same as forgetting, excusing, or reconciling. It does not require pretending harm did not occur, minimizing its effects, or remaining in a damaging relationship. One can forgive while:

  • Remaining estranged from the person who caused harm
  • Continuing to pursue legal accountability
  • Speaking publicly about what happened

When forgiving family of origin, it helps to remember that they inherited the same ancestral wounds, expressed differently. When forgiving ancestors more broadly, it helps to remember that all lineages contain oppressors, those who were oppressed, and those who were both. Hurt people hurt people. Full understanding of why someone acted as they did in life may never be available. Ancestor veneration holds that the soul continues to evolve after death; the ancestor in their current form is not the same as the person they were.

Forgiveness is not passivity in the face of present injustice. On the contrary, releasing the past's hold on the present frees attention and energy for shaping the future. The process of forgiveness often manifests as a renewed commitment to truth-telling, justice, and activism.

Making Memorial Art

Memorial art has a necessary place in ancestral healing, even when sitting with it is painful. Grief is not an obstacle to the work; it is part of the work. On a physical level, crying stimulates endorphin production. On a metaphysical level, it clears stagnant energy. When no other form of spiritual practice feels accessible, grief itself is valid and effective.

Healing is generally easier in community than alone. Memorial art functions as a gathering point for collective grief. When an ancestral line experienced trauma connected to a large-scale historical tragedy, pilgrimage to a related memorial site can be a meaningful practice. Permanent memorials draw others engaged in the same work, creating the conditions for shared acknowledgment.

Notable Examples of Memorial Art

The National Memorial for Peace and Justice, established in 2018 by the Equal Justice Initiative in Montgomery, Alabama, commemorates victims of lynching in the United States. At its center hang 805 steel rectangles, one for each of the 805 counties in the United States where one of more than 4,300 documented lynchings of African Americans took place between 1877 and 1950. Names are inscribed on the structures, which are shaped to resemble coffins. A duplicate set of 805 monuments is available to be claimed by each named county for installation at local lynching sites.

The Stolpersteine ("stumbling stones") project was begun in 1992 by German artist Gunter Demnig. Each Stolperstein is a small concrete cube topped with a brass plate bearing the name and life dates of a victim of Nazi persecution, placed at the last freely chosen residence of that person before they were killed, deported, driven to suicide, or forced to flee. More than 67,000 have been laid across 22 countries in Europe. The inscription reads "Hier wohnte..." ("Here lived..."), placing the atrocity in the exact space the viewer now occupies.

Ghost bikes are bicycles painted white and secured to fences or street signs at sites where cyclists were struck and killed by motorists. The first were created in St. Louis, Missouri, in 2003. More than 630 ghost bikes now exist in over 210 locations worldwide.

All three forms of memorial serve the same essential function: they interrupt the ordinary flow of daily life to insist that something happened here. This may be what the traumatized dead most need from the living: not resolution, but witness. The Stolpersteine catch the light at every sunset for as long as they exist.

The NAMES Project AIDS Memorial Quilt is the largest piece of community folk art in the world, estimated at 54 tons. Each panel celebrates the life of someone who died of AIDS-related causes. At three by six feet, each panel is approximately the size of a grave, a deliberate reference to the fact that many people who died of AIDS-related causes received no funeral at the time, due to social stigma and the refusal of funeral homes and cemeteries to handle their remains. The quilt asserts that these people lived, and demonstrates the ways individuals remain connected across time and through each other.

Personal Memorial Art

Not all memorial work draws from existing projects. Personal memorial art created for one's own ancestors can be equally powerful. One example: in late 2017, magician and Houngan Demetrius LaCroix began collecting manillas, the metal bracelets used as currency in the West African slave trade. The most common prices in that trade were eight manillas for a man and four for a woman. LaCroix set out to acquire twelve manillas as a way of ritually reclaiming his ancestors' freedom. His fundraising effort exceeded that goal, ultimately producing seventy manillas. He interpreted this surplus as confirmation that his ancestors were responding to the work. After offering the manillas to his ancestors, he reported a significant shift in his life, particularly in his relationship with his father's family. He now shares individual manillas with young African Americans in his community who appear disconnected from their spiritual roots, offering them not only the object's history but what it represents: the reality of what their ancestors survived and overcame together.

Dying Well

The third way to prevent inherited wounds from passing forward is committing to dying well. Dying is demanding work. It requires significant effort from both the newly dead and the living community for the transition to go smoothly. This is likely the origin of most funeral rites: a practical, instinctive response to the difficulty of that crossing. It may also explain why so much lore surrounding ghosts, vampires, and other revenants centers on those who did not receive proper burial.

Contemporary culture broadly denies death and orients toward extending life at any cost, including at the cost of its quality. This makes conscious preparation for death feel unnatural, macabre, or unnecessary. But death is an ordinary fact of life. Accepting it is a prerequisite for being fully present now.

Near-Death Experience as Perspective Shift

Proximity to death can clarify what matters. Wayne Coyne, lead singer of the Flaming Lips, described in a 2002 interview how being robbed at gunpoint affected his approach to his work: "I realized I was going to die. And when that gets into your mind... I thought, 'I'm not going to sit here and wait for things to happen, I'm going to make them happen, and if people think I'm an idiot, I don't care.'" Many people who have faced death report similar reorientations. Those who have not had such an experience can work toward the same clarity through deliberate practice.

Practical Death Planning

Preparing for death does not require a crisis. Practical steps include:

  • Deciding which life-extending medical measures are acceptable and which are not
  • Registering as an organ donor (or not, based on personal or spiritual conviction)
  • Specifying how the body should be handled after death
  • Talking with loved ones about end-of-life wishes
  • Writing preferences down and completing the legal documents (advance directive, healthcare proxy, will) to make them binding

This work protects both the dying and the living from unnecessary confusion and trauma at an already difficult time.

Spiritual Practices for Contemplating Death

Maraṇasati is a Buddhist meditation practice that uses visualization and contemplation to engage directly with the nature of death. The Satipatthana Sutta, one of the most widely studied discourses in Theravada Buddhism, includes contemplation of nine stages of bodily decomposition, from consumption by carrion animals through the reduction of bones to dust. The meditator is instructed to reflect: "This body of mine, too, is of the same nature as that body, is going to be like that body, and has not got past the condition of becoming like that body." The practice is intended to cultivate right effort, spiritual urgency, and detachment from worldly distraction.

In India, the Aghori are ascetic Shaiva sadhus whose practice involves frequenting charnel grounds, applying cremation ash to the body, and using bones from human corpses to craft cups and jewelry. Through these rituals of deliberate contact with taboo and pollution, the Aghori work toward the transcendence of duality and access to altered states of consciousness in which the constructed nature of ordinary categories becomes perceptible.

In the medieval Christian West, memento mori (Latin: "remember that you will die") was a practice of regular contemplation of mortality, undertaken to release attachment to worldly concerns and refocus attention on the soul and afterlife. Its iconography includes skulls, hourglasses, wilting flowers, and decaying fruit.

Simpler approaches are equally valid. Reading obituaries daily, pausing at a cemetery on a regular commute, keeping a skull or hourglass on a desk: any practice that makes mortality a conscious and ordinary part of daily awareness serves the same function. The goal is not to produce fear or despair, but to hold the fact of death lightly enough that it stops requiring avoidance.

Journal Prompts

  1. What trauma are you aware of your ancestors experiencing during their lifetimes, either personally or as a group? When and how did you learn about these traumas? How did learning about them shift your understanding of your living family?
  2. What destructive patterns in your living family might be related to these traumas? What survival skills does your living family have that may be related to these traumas?
  3. How do you feel about forgiveness? Is there anything you could benefit from forgiving today?
  4. How do you feel about death? Have you ever come close to dying before? If so, how did it change your perspective on life?
  5. Do you feel prepared to die? What small step could you take today that might help you prepare for that moment?
  6. What would happen if we defined ourselves by our ecstasies rather than by our traumas?
