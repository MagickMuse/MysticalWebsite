Fasting appears in nearly every spiritual tradition. The Bible, the Quran, the Upanishads, and the Mahabharata all commend it, and most faiths fold it into their disciplines. Part of its power is plainly physical: going without food can bring on light-headedness and even hallucination, a state close to religious ecstasy. It is also a shamanic practice, undertaken to help the seeker reach the spirit realms. Some hold that a fast is as good for the body as for the spirit and keep an annual cleansing, much as our ancestors may have valued giving the body a rest from heavy food.

What Counts as a Fast

What does and does not constitute a fast is something of a moveable feast. For some it means nothing but water; for others, abstaining from meat, or eating a single solid meal a day. Eating fish rather than meat on a Friday is itself a kind of fast. The Catholic Church kept an especially severe form, the Black Fast, which allowed only one small meal and forbade all animal products, including dairy.

The Black Fast as a Curse

This same severe discipline could be turned to darker ends. A Black Fast performed against an enemy was believed to work as a curse, and in 1538 one Mabel Brigge was executed for performing such a fast against Henry VIII and the Duke of Norfolk, a striking measure of the power folk belief granted to the simple act of refusing to eat.

Fasting Across Faiths

For some Jews fasting is very strict, with nothing at all permitted to pass the lips, not even toothbrushing or medicine save where life is at risk; the major fasts are Yom Kippur and Tisha B'Av. For Hindus a fast is often tied to the particular deity being honored. Buddhist monks and nuns commonly take no food after their midday meal as an aid to meditation. For Muslims the month of Ramadan brings fasting from sunrise to sunset. In the Christian calendar the great fast is the forty days of Lent before Easter, recalling Christ's fast in the desert, though today it is more often kept by giving up a single indulgence. Fasting can also serve as a form of protest, staking the life of the one who refuses food, often for political ends.
