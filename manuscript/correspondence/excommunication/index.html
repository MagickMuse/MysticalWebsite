🔔Excommunication

◇Type
Concept
≡Also called
Anathema

Excommunication means, quite literally, to be cut off from a community. In particular it names the Catholic rite by which a member is formally removed from the Church, a grave and relatively rare act. A number of offenses were held to bring instant excommunication, among them apostasy (the renunciation of belief), heresy (any practice running counter to Church doctrine), schism (causing division within the Church), desecration of the Eucharist, attempting to absolve an accomplice in adultery, consecrating a bishop without the pope's permission, and procuring an abortion, along with being an accomplice to any of these.

Anathema

The foundation of excommunication is anathema, in plain terms a curse of the Church that denounces a person or a doctrine. The word is rendered in several ways but is generally taken to mean "cursed." Anathema is older than excommunication and a still stricter form of it, which gives the rite its weight as an ecclesiastical malediction.

Bell, Book, and Candle

Excommunication still occurs from time to time, though it had its heyday in the Middle Ages, when it served as a weapon to discredit persons of rank who had fallen out of favor with the Church. In that era the shame was made public through a solemn ceremony in which the condemned was symbolically banished by bell, book, and candle.

Degrees and Famous Cases

Before the rules were softened in 1983 there were two degrees. The first, vitandus, meant the person was to be shunned entirely by the faithful. The lesser, toleratus or tolerated, still allowed Catholics to keep social and business ties with the excommunicant. The Church has since taken a more forgiving view, hoping to draw the excommunicant back into its fold. Famous excommunicants include Napoleon Bonaparte, Martin Luther, Robert the Bruce, and Fidel Castro, the last excommunicated in 1962.
