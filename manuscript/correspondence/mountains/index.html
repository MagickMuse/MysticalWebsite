🛡️Andes Mountain Protection

Sacred Meeting Places

European folklore consistently places witch gatherings atop hills/mountains. Practical origins: before modern navigation, obvious geographic features, crossroads, standing stones, barrows, high points, served as reliable meeting locations.

Strategic Advantages

Mountain peaks offer crucial vantage points. During persecution, those already assembled could observe approaching visitors and potentially escape into surrounding forests/caves if threatened. Many witch-mountains combine these defensive features.

Spiritual Significance

Mountains and hills are sacred as Earth's closest points to Heavens. Biblical texts repeatedly condemn traveling to "high places" to worship "foreign gods", practice continuing today.

Famous witch-mountains like Bald Mountain and The Brocken allegedly drew practitioners from across world for massive festivities, while countless lesser-known peaks served local communities.

The Magic Mountain

In the mountain, the masculine element of fire meets the feminine element of the Earth Mother, and the high places have carried symbolic weight in the human mind for as long as we have looked up at them.

Why We Climb

Ask any devoted mountaineer why they would step so far past comfort as to risk their life, and the classic answer, coined by the Everest pioneer George Mallory, is "because it's there."

Yet there has to be a deeper reason that someone would willingly face scorching sun, cutting wind, freezing ice and snow, avalanche, rockfall, and the real chance of death simply to stand on a summit. The symbolism of mountains runs so deep in the psyche that even today's climbers might struggle to put their fascination into words. Just as the Fulani hold that the upward reach of flame marks fire as belonging to the Heavens, the rising shape of the mountain draws the same parallel with fire. With a little reflection it is easy to see why every country has its holy mountain and why so many myths cling to them, some tied to real peaks such as Mount Sinai and some to mythical ones such as Mount Meru.

Where Heaven and Earth Meet

Mountains are the meeting place of Heaven and Earth, where a person encounters the gods or receives their messages. Sacrifices were made on mountaintops in the belief that these heights sit closest to the deities being honored, and traces of human sacrifice have even been found on high Andean peaks. Where fire was part of the rite, a bonfire raised on the summit carried the offering nearer still to the gods.

Transcendence and the Center of the World

Mountains stand for transcendence, a physical image of our wish to lift body, mind, and spirit. A summit is a place of exposure and at the same time of refuge, since its vantage lets everything below be seen clearly. Seen from above, the peak looks like the center of the world, an emblem not only of the World Axis but of the Omphalos, the navel of the world. Mountains are likewise symbols of the unknown and the uncharted, in both a physical and a spiritual sense.

The Eternal Hills

The eternal hills seem to stand outside the flow of time, holding their place as landmarks for thousands of years while everything around them changes. For this they have come to mean solidity, fortitude, eternity, and stillness, an idea echoed in the Bible:

"I look up unto the hills, from whence cometh my strength."

Mountains Made by Hand

Any built structure that imitates the essential qualities of a mountain, its great height, its pyramidal form, its aspiration and transcendence, becomes a mountain symbol in its own right. Such hand-made imitations include sacred buildings like the Pyramids and ziggurats, the stupa and the pagoda, along with simpler works such as the tump and the cairn. Even the altar in a church or other place of worship is a made symbol of a mountain, since the word altar means high place.

Chomolungma

Chomolungma is a Tibetan name meaning Goddess Mother of the Universe, and in Nepal she is called Sagarmatha, Goddess of the Sky. The mountain that carries her name and spirit rises 8,850 meters on the border between China and Nepal and is the highest in the world. This evocative Tibetan name was set aside in 1865 for the plainer Mount Everest, after Sir George Everest, predecessor to the then British Surveyor General of India, supposedly because no local name could be found for the most significant mountain on Earth. The Goddess is said to take vengeance on those who invade her home. For the people of the region the peak was never meant to be climbed at all, being the dwelling of gods and spirits rather than a place for humankind. When Everest was first summited in 1953 it carried a symbolic weight to rival the Moon landings, for like the Moon it stood for a step into the unknown. Although well over 3,000 climbers have reached the top, more than 200 are known to have died on its slopes. Chomolungma is one of five Goddess sisters, all linked to mountains, and is regarded as a Goddess of wealth. With the tourist trade now booming among those who wish to climb or simply to behold the holy mountain, the Great Mother has indeed brought wealth to her people.

Mount Kailash

Paired with the holy lake Manasarovar, Kailash is a mountain sacred to both Hindus and Buddhists and stands in Tibet. Its name means Treasure of the Snow Mountain. Its form is archetypal, a vast pyramid whose pointed summit reaches 6,600 meters into the Heavens. Seen from the south it gains still greater sacred status, for the vertical and horizontal strata of rock and ice trace out the swastika, an emblem of the Sun and one of the oldest and most widespread symbols in the world. Kailash is held to be a gathering place of many gods and so a favored site of pilgrimage. Buddhists believe that a single circuit of the mountain atones for the sins of an entire lifetime, that ten circuits spare the pilgrim from Hell for 500 reincarnations, and that one hundred circuits raise them to Buddha-like status, a feat for only the most determined, since Kailash is famously hard to reach. Unlike a holy peak such as Kangchenjunga, the third highest mountain, which climbers may ascend all but the final few feet of the summit, Kailash permits no ascents at all. It is often associated with another symbolic but mythical mountain that has likewise never been climbed, Mount Meru.

Mount Meru

The most sacred mountain in Hindu mythology, fabled Mount Meru, also known as Sumeru, is described in vivid and fantastical terms. So tall that the Sun is said to travel around it, Meru rests in a rarefied and perfect atmosphere where all is transcendent. Its base lies in Hell and its summit in the Heavens, and it is ringed by seven golden mountains separated by seven circular oceans. Its slopes glitter with gemstones and exotic trees heavy with ripe fruit, and the whole rises out of a great ocean, with a golden palace at its summit, home to both Indra, king of the Hindu gods, and Shiva. Meru is surrounded by four continents, the southernmost of which, Jambudvipa, answers to the Earth. Further off, somewhere to the north of Jambudvipa, lies the magical land of Shambhala, the gateway between the physical and spiritual realms, where enlightened souls dwell before they finally attain Buddhahood. For centuries explorers have sought Shambhala as a real, earthly place, missing the finer point that both it and Mount Meru are places of aspiration symbolic of the soul. Images of Meru often appear in mandalas and in meditative practice.

Mount Olympus

It is said that a night spent on Mount Olympus brings either madness or communion with the gods, and the one may well lead to the other. Homer pictured Olympus as a place untouched by storms, wrapped in cloudless aether, the pure upper air. At 2,917 meters it is the highest mountain in Greece, and because its base sits at sea level its vertical rise is all the more striking. It likely began, like Mount Meru, as an idealized peak, since the oldest legends speak of the mountain without fixing it to any one spot, and several mountains bore the name Olympus. It is the home of the great gods of the Greek pantheon, and one legend even claims the gods built it for themselves, dwelling in crystal palaces upon it with no fear of the warning about glass houses. First the seat of Gaia Olympia, Mother of the Gods, it became the throne of Zeus and his wife Hera, his sisters Demeter and Hestia, his brothers Poseidon and Hades, and his children Aphrodite, Apollo, Ares, Artemis, Athena, Hephaestus, and Hermes.

Mount Sinai

On the Sinai Peninsula in Egypt stands Mount Sinai, where the Bible tells that Moses received the Ten Commandments from God. Sinai is really a cluster of peaks, and its name means the Mount of God, a title it carried before Moses ever climbed it and perhaps a hint as to where he should seek the wisdom of the Supreme Being. The peak of the biblical story is also called Mount Musa, the Mountain of Moses, and at 2,285 meters it is not the tallest in the region, that honor belonging to Mount Catherine at 2,673 meters. Musa is also said to be the place from which Buraq, the steed of Mohammed, ascended to Heaven, which only deepens its sacred standing.

The Kun Lun Range

The Kun Lun range in China is immense, running some 3,000 kilometers, and its highest peak, fittingly called the Goddess, rises to 7,167 meters. Taoists hold these mountains to be paradise itself, and they would withdraw into them to seek enlightenment and leave the material world behind; the Taoist immortals are called Sien, meaning Mountain People. The Jade Palace of Huang Di, the fabled emperor and founder of Chinese culture, is believed to lie within the range, as does a legendary fairyland known as the Xuangpu.

Kaf

Kaf is an imaginary mountain of Muslim myth, and though it has no place in the ordinary world it remains a powerful image of an ideal state and of attainable perfection, seen as the mother of all other mountains, real or imagined. It stands at the ends of the universe, where the visible and invisible worlds meet. In the old belief that the Earth was a flat disc, Kaf lay apart from it, beyond a vast plain of darkness that took four months to cross, yet the journey was held to be well worth the trouble, for the mountain was made of a sparkling emerald so bright that its light tinted the sky. Many of Kaf's qualities echo the symbolism of all mountains: one tale sets it upon a rock called the Post at the center of the universe and says that Kaf and its base give the stability that keeps the Earth safe from earthquakes, marking it as the World Axis and the Omphalos, the navel of all things. Kaf appears in many stories, among them the Arabian Nights, where an angel tells the traveler Bulukiya that this is the mountain Kaf which encompasses the world, and that beyond it lie forty worlds each many times the size of our own, some of gold, some of silver, and some of carnelian. Its most famous tale is The Conference of the Birds by the Sufi poet Farid ud-Din Attar, in which a flock of birds sets out for Kaf to find the simurgh, a bird as old as time and the very image of God. One by one the birds make excuses and fall away until thirty, led by the hoopoe, complete the journey, only to discover that they themselves together are their own divinity, for simurgh means thirty birds.
