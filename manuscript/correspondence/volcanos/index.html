🌪️Disaster

Like any force of nature whose power to destroy can be felt, the volcano is revered as much as it is feared, and is often raised to the rank of a deity in the hope of appeasing it. The unpredictable life of an active volcano makes it seem peculiarly alive and spirited. It shares much of the mountain's symbolism, but the crater at its heart, pouring out rivers of fire and molten rock, adds a further meaning: that of a goddess who renews the souls of the dead in her magickal cauldron. Pele, the Polynesian and Hawaiian fire goddess, is still honored at the Kilauea volcano in Hawaii, where modern thrill-seeking visitors climb the slopes of her home and show their regard by tossing cheap bottles of whiskey into the crater.

Mount Fuji

A sacred emblem of Japan and a cornerstone of its culture, Mount Fuji, called the Peak of the White Lotus, gathers countless myths and legends, which is no wonder for a mountain half a million years old. Fuji Yama, to give its fuller name, is also known as the Never Dying Mountain and is so revered that it stands for the nation itself. Like holy mountains elsewhere it is held to be the dwelling of many gods, among them the Supreme Being Kunitokotachi, making it the symbolic guardian of the country, its flawless triangular form piercing the sky at 3,776 meters. The name Fuji comes from Fuchi, the Aino goddess of fire, and the mountain is also said to be home to the powerful goddess Sengen, who casts from her slopes any pilgrim who is not pure of mind. Even so, from July through the end of August thousands climb Fuji each year after a rite of purification, part of which is to scatter salt, rice wine, and water into the crater, a more graceful offering than the one made to Pele. Despite its first dedication to a fire goddess, women and girls were barred from the pilgrimage until 1872 and the dawn of the Meiji era, the so-called Period of Enlightened Rule.

Mountains as Sacred Ground in Japan

Since mountainous terrain makes up about eighty percent of Japan's land, it is no surprise that an entire web of belief and religious practice has grown around mountains as holy places, a system called Sanganku Shinko, the mountain creed. Shinto, the native faith of Japan, holds that every part of the land has its own spirit, the Kami, whose lives are bound up with those of human beings and whose presence is strongest in the mountains. Being benevolent, the Kami were thought to come down from the heights to help with the crops in the growing season and to return home once the harvest was in, an echo of the widespread idea that mountains make the weather. Great stone boulders sit at the foot of many Japanese mountains, Fuji among them, where rites are held to welcome the Kami or to see them off. The spiritual pull of the mountains also gave rise to the Yamabushi, ascetics who are part hermit, part magician, and part priest, versed in herbal lore, healing, and charm-making, who withdrew into the heights to deepen their enlightenment and spiritual awareness.
