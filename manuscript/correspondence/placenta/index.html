For many the placenta may seem like nothing more than clinical waste, but for others it has gathered deep symbolic meaning, since it nourishes and protects the unborn child. The placenta is a symbol of life, of spirit, and of the physical manifestation of the human being.

Because it is "born" along with the child, the placenta is treated with respect and ceremony in many parts of the world. The Maori, for example, bury it as they would a human body; indeed, the same word, whenua, means both placenta and land. These rites mark the bond between the newborn, its unformed "twin," and the land, and burying the placenta ties the child to the earth. In Africa and among some Native American peoples the placenta is given a full burial for similar reasons.

The belief that placenta and child are inseparably linked is widespread. In Southeast Asia the word for placenta is the same as the word for "jacket," the placenta being the child's first garment, and it is said that after death the soul seeks out the place where its placenta is buried so the two can be reunited.

In one example of sympathetic magick, Filipino mothers bury the placenta with books, trusting that the act will make for an intelligent child. Animals routinely eat the placenta after giving birth, since it is rich in vitamins and minerals and a great source of nourishment for the new mother.
