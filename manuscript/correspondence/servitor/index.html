👻Creating an Artificial Spirit (Servitor)

Servitors (also called artificial spirits, egregores, fetch, bud-will, artificial elementals, or tulpas) are autonomous thought forms created by practitioners and programmed with specific functions. Unlike helper spirits, familiaries, or deities, servitors are manifestations of the practitioner's will, shaped from energy and given instructions to operate independently.

Definition

A servitor is an artificial spirit made from energy shaped and programmed by the practitioner, similar to an artificial intelligence program. These entities are created for long and short-term use and are known by many names across traditions. In traditional European witchcraft, they are sometimes known as a fetch or bud-will. In ceremonial magic they are often called egregores, or when constructed from one or more of the four elements, artificial elementals. Chaos magic practitioners call them servitors. Tibetan Buddhism uses the term tulpa (roughly translating to "Mind Emanation").

Purpose

Servitors are advanced sigils. With a sigil, you make it once and release it to the world to do its work. Once that work is done, the sigil is used up and gone. Servitors normally last longer than one use. They can perform simple, magickal tasks such as providing magickal protection of a space, warning of danger, guarding thresholds, dealing with intrusive thoughts, maintaining energetic boundaries, or carrying out specific defensive functions.

Historical Examples

The Golem of Prague (1580): Rabbi Loeb created an artificial spirit that inhabited and animated a physical form to protect the Jewish community from false accusations of ritual murder. The golem was given life by writing one of the names of God, EMETH, on its head. The spirit was deactivated by changing EMETH to METH (meaning "dead" in Hebrew) by wiping off the first letter.

Alexandra David Néel's Tulpa (1920s): The French explorer who traveled Tibet described creating an artificial spirit in her book Magic and Mystery in Tibet. She sealed herself in a cave and concentrated on creating a short, good-natured monk. After a few weeks, the tulpa-monk followed her on travels and was seen by other members of her traveling party. Problems arose when the monk began changing outside her control, his appearance shifting from portly and good-natured to gaunt and sinister. She dismantled the spirit only with great effort over several months.

GOTOS of the Fraternitas Saturni: This German magical order regarded an egregore named GOTOS (acronym for Gradus Ordinis Templi Orientis Saturni) as a secret chief. The egregore was fed into by everyone in the order, creating a group mind that could be called upon to advise the order with the weight of its collected wisdom.

Key Considerations

Intention Setting: Servitors require clear, concise programming. Despite their independence, servitors are not intelligent entities. If you are not clear in your intentions, the servitor will not function the way you want. It could even make things worse.

Visualization: Creating a servitor requires the ability to visualize (or otherwise sense) the energy you are shaping. This does not have to be visual. You can create a servitor that manifests as a smell, a feeling, or even a sense of pressure. Use whatever sense you are most comfortable with. The most important part is picking a form that will be easy for you to recognize.

Temporary vs. Permanent: In general, artificial spirits are meant to be temporary and designed to dissipate upon completion of their task or upon a certain date. Permanent servitors can be constructed but must be carefully looked after and fed with energy to keep them in line, lest they begin to take their nourishment and thus their programming from elsewhere, as with Alexandra David Néel's runaway tulpa.

Form Affects Function: The form you give a servitor will influence its behavior. A servitor made to warn of danger could take the shape of a cloud of eyes and ears. A servitor meant to guard a door could take the form of a knight in armor. If you want a servitor to charge into battle, do not make it cute and cuddly. If you are concerned about causing harm, do not make it into the form of a bear.

Related Practices

Servitor work appears in chaos magick, ceremonial magick, traditional witchcraft, and Tibetan Buddhist practices. The construction and use are more or less the same regardless of terminology or tradition.
