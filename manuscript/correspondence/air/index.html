Elemental Attributes

Air governs thought and intellectual pursuits, embodying a sanguine temperament characterized by fun-loving energy and mental acuity. Those drawn to Air are typically intellectual and social, finding joy in communication and the exchange of ideas.

Temperament: Sanguine

Function: Thought

Attribute: Intellectual, fun-loving

Direction: East

Season: Spring

Archangel: Raphael

Elementals: Sylphs

Tarot Suit: Wands

Playing Card Suit: Clubs

When to Work with Air: Use Air if you need encouragement and support.

The Elusive Element

Air is the most elusive of the elements because it is invisible, intangible, and changeable. The ancients believed that the wind is influenced by the direction from which it originates. This idea translated into magickal methods quite nicely.

Directional Wind Work

Wind direction carries specific energies:

  • South wind: Generates passion, warmth, or enthusiasm in spellcraft
  • West wind: Stimulates intuition and imagination
  • Damp wind: Combines water and air to raise dreamy, nourishing energy

Practical Wind Spellwork:

  • Scatter spell components in a wind moving away from you to carry a message or remove a problem
  • Perform magick for new projects with the "wind at your back" for good fortune
  • Open a window to "air out" negative energy when trying to quell anger

Cross-Pollination of Ideas

Just as wind scatters pollen to fertilize plants, the air element describes how words spread far and wide, fertilizing minds and cross-pollinating societies with new ideas. Air can be gentle or fierce, damp or dry, hot or cold, and each of these moods carries slightly different magickal connotations.

Air Personalities

People who have a lot of air energy in their makeup tend to be friendly, curious, fickle, adaptable, idealistic, talkative, and interested in all sorts of ideas. Air energy moves quickly, making it ideal when a spell involves change, movement, or you want things to happen fast. Air energy can also be used to contact spirits or other nonphysical beings.

Air in Spell-casting

Air acts as a carrier and a messenger. In magickal thinking (and in some branches of advanced physics), nothing that exists ever truly vanishes. "Disappearing into thin air" is meant quite literally in the context of spellwork. When a candle burns, it draws on fire's power but also summons air: whatever the flame consumes passes into the atmosphere. Air is tied to astral travel as well; the classic image of witches soaring through the sky reflects voyages taken in dreamwork and visionary states.

Air is a masculine, yang force. Fragrance speaks its language, and word-based charms tap directly into its power.

Air as Spirit and Breath

Paired with fire as the masculine elements set against feminine water and earth, air is often called the primary element: at once the life force and the spirit that every living creature depends upon, and the very thing that allows fire to burn. The Arabic and Hebrew word ruh names air, spirit, and breath all at once. In geometry air is drawn as a circle with a dot at its center, the same figure used for the Sun, and it is sometimes shown as a feather for its lightness and its tie to birds, the creatures that live within it.

Struggling to breathe has at times stood for a deeper failure to take in the universal spirit, an image used in film to suggest a person cut off from the world around them. Correct breathing, or pranayama, is one of the cornerstones of yogic practice, since air is held to be the stuff of life, energy, and spirit. The alchemists believed air carried the vitality of sunlight, a kind of astral gold to mirror the earthly gold they hoped to make from base matter, and St. Martin called air a palpable symbol of invisible life. To the Greeks and Romans it was the Soul of the World, the anima mundi, and the Hindus likewise bound their word for breath to air seen as a single collective breath; when the final breath leaves the body it rejoins that greater whole. If breath is the most personal way of knowing air, the wind is its universal form.
