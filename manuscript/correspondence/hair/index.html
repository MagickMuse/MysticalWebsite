💇Hair

Hair, the crown of the head, has long been believed to hold an essence or life-force inseparably bound to its owner, even once it has been cut away. A single strand is therefore an essential ingredient in spells to gain power over someone. The old belief has a grain of truth: a small clump of hair can show an analyst which vitamins and minerals a person lacks, and hair can be tested for DNA. It is still thought unlucky and even dangerous to let hair fall into the wrong hands, and some people, the Vietnamese in particular, are careful about how they dispose of it.

Hair symbolizes energy, power, and sensuality, and is an important part of identity and personality. To remove it can seem to remove these qualities too. Losing hair is, for many, psychologically distressing, as though part of the self disappears with it. In the Old Testament, the warrior Samson is betrayed by Delilah, who coaxes from him the secret of his strength, his uncut hair, and cuts it off as he sleeps so that his enemies can capture him; his strength returns as his hair grows back.

Locks of hair are kept as a direct link with their owner, and in Victorian times elaborate jewelry holding the hair of the departed became fashionable as part of a wider fascination with death.

Unruly hair signals separation from conventional society, a mark of someone who flouts the rules and whose loose hair is a symbol of freedom from social constraint. This is far older than the long-haired counterculture of the 1960s and 70s. Witches and wizards were traditionally pictured with wild, disheveled hair, living apart from their neighbors, as was the hermit, whose long robe and tangled hair form an archetypal uniform. The "mad professor" poised between genius and madness is given away by hair that stands on end, the sign of someone too absorbed to tend to it.

In Greek and Hindu myth, the Gods and Goddesses with the wildest, most disheveled hair are the most dangerous or the most demonic. Medusa, whose head was a mass of writhing snakes, is a striking example.

What people do with their hair carries meaning that varies from culture to culture. The Romans grew their hair as a sign of sorrow, while the Greeks cut theirs. In China, the same idea of tousled hair belonging to one set apart from society appears in the uncombed hair of mourners.

Cutting the hair carries great significance. A child's first haircut is a rite of passage for child and parents alike, and some societies hold it unlucky to cut a child's hair before its first year, since to do so would strip away some of its life-force and leave it vulnerable.

Because hair is a symbol of individuality, to make someone cut or shave it is to wield power over them. When a person joins the army or is imprisoned, the taming of the hair is among the first things done, a sign of uniformity and discipline. In Roman times short hair was likewise a mark of slavery; Gaul kept its independence and its people their flowing locks, becoming known as the Gallia Comata, the "long-haired Gauls." A ritual shaving of the head signals purity and a fresh start, and many instinctively cut their hair at moments of great change.

Sikhs do not cut their hair, which is honored as a sacred symbol of strength; keeping it long and uncut is a sign of accepting this gift, while keeping it covered beneath a turban guards against vanity. This includes all bodily hair, and the practice applies to both men and women.

Because long, flowing hair is a sign of virility, power, and worldliness, a shorn or shaven head is a sign of renouncing the world. Religious ascetics often shave their hair, and the tonsure of the monk is a mark of spiritual devotion. The custom is not limited to men: some nuns, and particularly observant Jewish women, shave their heads as a symbol of rejecting worldly and sensual concerns. St. Paul advised that women cover their hair in church, since spirits were thought to be drawn to loose, uncovered hair. In eighth-century Bavaria this was taken to an extreme, where the "lewd loosening of the hair" was reckoned as seductive as adultery, and women attending church for the first time after childbirth, in the rite known as "churching," had to veil their hair.

Hairstyles can say a great deal, especially in traditional societies. In India, hair worn in two plaits marks an unmarried woman; in Russia, a single plait was a sign of virginity and a pair of braids the style of a wife.

Finally there are the dreadlocks of the Rastafarians. This tight braiding was adopted just before the Second World War to identify with a style of hair worn in Ethiopia, its lion-like look paying homage to Emperor Haile Selassie, the "Lion of Judah." Dreadlocks in the West began among a small number of people in Jamaica but spread around the world as reggae music grew in popularity.
