The Roman physician Galen called the liver the home of the passions, and many societies share this view. The words "bile" and "gall," the substance the liver secretes, have come to mean bitterness or spite. In the Arab world the liver is linked with courage and strong feeling, and in Chinese medicine it is likewise seen as a source of strength.

This link between the liver and strength comes from its power to regenerate its own damaged tissue. In Greek myth, Prometheus was punished by the Gods for teaching human beings how to make fire: he was bound to a rock where an eagle tore out his liver each day, only for it to grow back overnight so the torment could begin again.

In one example of sympathetic magick, the strength held in the liver was thought to pass to others. In Cambodia, unlucky travelers were captured and their galls removed, and a liquor made from them was used both to fortify generals and to anoint the heads of the elephants ridden into battle.

The liver has also served as a tool of divination. In ancient Rome the haruspex, or "inspector of entrails," was trained to read the future from the entrails of animals, especially the livers of sheep. In Babylonia, hepatoscopy relied on the blood of the liver, each part of the organ tied to a particular deity. Models of such livers, marked with these divisions, still survive; one of the most famous is the Piacenza Liver of northern Italy, a bronze model that assigns a deity to each region marked upon it.

Religious Associations

In the Talmud, the liver is regarded as the seat of anger.
