What a Coven Is

A coven is a group of practitioners who gather regularly for shared magickal work. The word derives from the Latin conventus, meaning a gathering or assembly, the same root that gives us convent, convention, covenant, and convene.

Covens range from the strictly religious to the entirely practical. Some concentrate on healing. Others specialize in necromancy, divination, or the invocation of deities. Some devote themselves to political workings, directing collective power at those in positions of public authority. Others pursue knowledge and power as an end in itself. Many celebrate the eight Sabbats as their primary collective activity, treating the gatherings as occasions of genuine festivity rather than solemn ceremony.

Solo Practice vs. Coven Work

A solo working requires only privacy and a modest degree of soundproofing, the latter serving less as a precaution against noise complaints and more as an aid to the uninhibited release of emotional energy, which is of primary importance in all magickal work. Any corner of a private home is sufficient for solo practice.

Coven work requires a larger space and a compatible group, but the advantages are considerable. A magickal operation performed by a well-aligned group will often succeed spectacularly where a solo effort achieves only moderate success. The principle is straightforward: strength multiplies with numbers when the emotional rapport and shared purpose are genuine.

The Coven Entity

One of the central ideas of coven practice is that a well-sustained group does not merely add individual powers together. If sufficient pyramid power is brought to it and held in place by genuine emotional rapport, practitioners believe that a pool or vortex of collective energy begins to form.

This vortex, sustained by regular ritual, eventually becomes two things at once: an energy bank from which all members can draw to supplement their personal resources, and an artificially constructed collective deep mind, independent in its own right.

This is the magickal rationale behind the secrecy required in most serious occult groups. The rituals and symbols particular to the group, called triggering devices, are kept from outsiders not out of fear of misuse, but because interference by the uninitiated, particularly those of a hostile or sceptical disposition, undermines and disperses all the work invested in building the entity.

Cowans

The uninitiated are traditionally referred to as cowans. The word has roots in Freemasonry, where it originally referred to a stoneworker without formal guild training. In the context of witchcraft, a cowan is simply anyone who has not been brought into the working group, and whose presence risks destabilizing its established energetic structure.

Size and Membership

By tradition, a coven is limited to thirteen members, inclusive of its leaders. The number corresponds to the thirteen full moons of the lunar year and is also, practically speaking, the largest number of people who can work comfortably within a nine-foot circle.

When membership exceeds thirteen, a new coven splits away from the original one. Traditional territory is observed, with at least three miles maintained between the meeting places of distinct covens. This custom combines older notions of covendom, the territorial sphere of a coven's influence, with the pragmatic caution of the persecution era, when small, dispersed groups were simply safer.

In goddess-oriented covens with an emphasis on the sexual aspects of the Craft, membership is ideally composed of six male-female couples plus a leader, making thirteen total. These traditions also require that initiation be conferred from sex to sex (man upon woman and vice versa), with an exception made for parent and child. This rule does not apply in all branches of the Craft.

Esbats and Sabbats

Covens meet informally at gatherings called Esbats, a word derived from the French for "to frolic," reflecting the historically unsolemn, celebratory character of a witches' meeting.

The eight annual Sabbats mark the turning of the seasons and the ebb and flow of elemental tides. In addition to their magickal significance, they have always been occasions of genuine festivity. A coven that treats Sabbats as purely ritualistic obligations misses the point. Sabbats are a time of renewal of friendship and elemental power, and a time of enjoyment.

Requirements for a Functioning Coven

Two conditions are essential:

  1. Members must be like-minded: sympathetic to one another in basic belief and genuinely in emotional rapport.
  2. Members must bring sufficient magickal dedication and intensity of purpose, understood not as fanaticism but as the ability to focus the mind on a problem and release the imagination within appropriate limits.

All members should be fully and practically conversant with the four powers of the Witches' Pyramid. The more magickally developed the individuals, the more potent the coven as a collective entity.

The Seven Points of Forming a Coven

The practical establishment of a coven can be organized under seven headings:

  1. The Nature of Your Coven: the coven's identifying symbols, totems, and general symbolic framework.
  2. Coven Hierarchy: whether or not leaders and degrees of initiation exist, and how they are structured.
  3. Initiation Ritual: one or more rituals using coven symbolism, corresponding to any hierarchy established.
  4. A Binding Oath of Secrecy: imposed upon all members at admission, reinforced by the magickal weight of surrendered object links such as a drop of blood or a lock of hair.
  5. Coven Name: a magickal name taken specifically for coven work, which may be the same as or in addition to any name assumed for solo practice.
  6. Witch Insignia: the physical marker of membership worn by all members: pendant, ring, bracelet, necklace, garter, or similar.
  7. Coven Sabbat Rituals: workings designed for performance at one or more of the eight Sabbats, drawing heavily on the established coven symbolism.

The Nature of Your Coven

The symbolism chosen to represent a coven's orientation should draw from the mythological and magickal tradition the group wishes to align with. Tradition-minded practitioners generally recommend restricting this to European sources, keeping the group in contact with the Western magickal tradition. Others follow Dion Fortune's maxim that all gods are one God, all goddesses are one Goddess, and there is one Initiator, which permits a nonsectarian, broadly inclusive approach.

The source material chosen for coven symbolism should reflect the group's primary orientation:

  • Knowledge and sky power: symbols associated with Herne and the sky powers in all their forms
  • Power and fire: symbols consonant with the Horned One as Lord of Fire and Energy
  • Healing: imagery connected with Mother Hertha
  • Love: orientation toward the Lady Habondia

The coven emblem, or logo, should be designed like a heraldic crest: a single composite image incorporating the chosen symbolism, simple enough to engrave on witch jewels and to visualize clearly in meditation.

Coven Hierarchy

Witch covens, like most secret magickal societies, have organized themselves into hierarchies since the earliest times. The policy of the group is directed by one or more leaders, traditionally held to be wiser, stronger, or simply more experienced than the rest. A leader holds their position until it is either successfully challenged or handed on to a successor.

The Three Leaders

There may be as many as three leaders:

  1. The Magister / High Priest: the male leader, representing masculine power. Also titled: Cernunnos, Dumus, Puck, Hu, Barabbas, Mamilion, Dianus / Janus, Janicot, The Devil (meaning "little god," from early Aryan div, "holy" or "shining"), Lucifer ("the light bearer"), Simon, Herne, Gogmagog, Andros, Adonai / Adonis, Sabaoth, Baphomet.
  2. The Lady / High Priestess: the female leader, representing feminine power. Also titled: Andred, Bensozia, Nocticula, Rhiannon, Arrianrhod, Herodias / Aradia, Habondia / Dame Habonde, Holda / Hulda, Morgan / Morrigan, Brigid / Bride, Astarte / Ishtar, The Virgin / Maiden (Persephone).
  3. The Summoner / Officer / Executor: the second male, acting as the magister's lieutenant and go-between. He handles mundane coven functions: membership, dues, timing and location of Sabbats, what to wear and what to bring. He may lead the coven in the magister's absence. Traditionally wore all black; also titled "the Man in Black." In medieval France sometimes dressed in green and called Verdelet. His badge of office was a blackthorn staff, also called "Black Rod."

The relative authority of the Lady versus the Magister varies by tradition. In knowledge-and-power covens, the lady holds little executive authority and may function as a seer under the magister's direction. In goddess-oriented covens, the high priestess leads, and the high priest is her consort.

Dress and Regalia

The magister may wear furs, a robe, or nothing at all. On his head he may wear a traditional shamanic horned helmet, a full animal-head mask representing a coven totem (goat, ram, horse, cat, or other), or a closed metal helmet (hence the old epithet "Old Brazen Nose"). During Sabbats, his headpiece bears a short candle at the center, lit during proceedings, symbolizing his role as Lord of the Sun, Lucifer the light bearer.

The high priestess may similarly be robed or unrobed. Robe colors vary: white is most common, but black, green, red, or blue have all been used. On her forehead she may bind a silver lunar crescent, horns upward, or wear her hair flowing free. During Sabbat dances she may wear an illuminated coronet of candles. She always wears her full witch jewels, including the mandatory necklace.

Coven members may bring their own robes or work nude. A uniform cowled black robe or tabard is considered ideal for collective working. All members should wear witch jewels and bring their athames and wands. In earlier times, gentry brought swords and peasants brought pitchforks; the wand was disguised as a broomstick for concealment.

On Sacrifice

The notion that coven leaders were once periodically killed as a magickal sacrifice is likely more connected with beliefs fostered by established priesthoods, such as the Druids, than with actual witch coven practice. The concept of guilt, scapegoats, and atonement is absent from witchcraft.

The only rituals currently involving anything of this nature are cakes and wine and libations. The occasional use of a minute quantity of blood in certain operations is not understood as appeasement but as a means of conveying a specific type of magickal energy held to reside in the blood. Many modern witches prefer the white of an egg as a substitute.
