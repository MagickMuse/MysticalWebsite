Guide: Working with the Saints

A saint is the spirit of someone who has died yet remains present to the living, working miracles and offering aid from the other side. This definition captures the essence of what saints truly are. Though the term has taken on broader meanings over time, genuine sainthood requires two things: the person must be dead, and they must have a proven track record of performing miracles posthumously.

Beyond these requirements, saints defy categorization. They emerge from all backgrounds, faiths, cultures, genders, ages, and time periods. Some died young, others lived long lives. Many embodied virtue and selflessness while alive. Others lived questionable lives: thieves, oppressors, even killers have become saints. A virtuous life does not guarantee sainthood, nor does sainthood require prior virtue.

The Problem with Language

Contemporary usage of "saint" to mean "exceptionally good person" muddles the waters. Someone performing charitable acts might be called a saint while still breathing, but authentic sainthood demands death followed by verified miraculous activity. The words "saintly" and "saint" describe different things entirely.

The Catholic canonization process demonstrates this clearly. Candidates must produce at least two confirmed miracles after death before the Church officially recognizes them. Lifetime good deeds alone do not suffice.

The Age of Reason and Its Aftermath

The 18th century Enlightenment brought widespread reluctance to engage with supernatural topics. Reason supplanted faith as the highest value. Skeptics challenged long-held beliefs about the soul, the afterlife, miracles, communication with the dead, and paranormal abilities. All of these concepts are central to sainthood.

The scientific method that emerged from this period validates only repeatable, predictable phenomena. Miracles, by their very nature, are singular events that transcend logic. Serious discussion of saints and their powers runs counter to both scientific standards and social norms.

From the late 1700s through the 1900s, polite society largely avoided supernatural subjects. The 21st century has seen a reversal: a surge of interest in the paranormal and openness to discussing what was once spoken of only in whispers.

What Becomes of the Dead

Thinkers across disciplines have wrestled with the question of what follows death for thousands of years. Three main answers have emerged:

The Eternal Journey: Upon death, the soul departs the body and travels to another realm: Heaven, Hell, Valhalla, Avalon, the Western Paradise, or similar destinations.

The Cycle of Rebirth: The soul moves from body to body across multiple lifetimes, eventually being reborn in a new form after each death.

The End: Death concludes individual existence entirely. The body decays and memory fades, though remarkable individuals may be remembered longer.

Each view has billions of adherents. Perhaps all three are true for different souls. Some may journey onward, some may return in new forms, some may simply cease. This aligns with widespread folk beliefs that the dead interact with the living in varied ways.

Types of Active Dead

The vast majority of the deceased vanish completely. They exist only in the memories of the living and neither help nor harm anyone. These silent souls form the overwhelming majority.

Some souls, however, do not fully depart. They remain present among the living for various reasons, sometimes helpful, sometimes harmful. Traditional folklore recognizes several categories:

  • Ghosts: Spirits tied to places or unresolved matters
  • Vampires: Spirits that prey upon the living
  • Ancestors: Spirits who maintain bonds with their bloodline
  • Spirit Guides: Spirits devoted to guiding and protecting specific individuals
  • Saints: Spirits who perform miracles and answer prayers

Saints hold a distinct position. They are not trapped like ghosts. Their help extends beyond family unlike ancestors. Their intervention typically requires formal requests and offerings unlike spirit guides. Saints serve as allies to anyone who approaches them correctly.

Ancestors, Spirit Guides, and Saints

Despite what horror films suggest, hostile ghosts and vampires are rare. Most spirits who linger are not threats. They are potential sources of great benefit. Ancestors, spirit guides, and saints stay among the living out of love, duty, kinship, or sacred promises made while alive to protect and provide for others.

Ancestors

Ancestral spirits watch over their living descendants, offering guidance and protection while sometimes causing trouble if they feel ignored or disrespected. Ancestor veneration ranks among humanity's oldest religious practices and continues today. Many traditions maintain elaborate rituals for honoring and communicating with ancestors.

  • Ancestors focus exclusively on their own descendants
  • Saints may assist anyone

Since spirits without descendants cannot be ancestors, ancestral spirits benefit from keeping their descendants healthy, prosperous, and fertile.

Spirit Guides

Spirit guides concern themselves with individual spiritual development. Each guide focuses on a single living person. With countless souls passing over daily, every living person could theoretically have their own unique guide, comparable to a personal guardian angel.

These guides may reveal themselves only to the person they protect. No one else may know of their existence. When a spirit guide begins helping people broadly rather than one individual, they may be considered a saint.

Saints as Workers of Miracles

Saints perform miracles while providing both material help and spiritual direction. They are genuine allies operating from beyond death. For countless people throughout history and today (educated and otherwise, from diverse spiritual backgrounds) saints are lived reality. These devotees do not merely believe in saints; they possess firsthand knowledge and direct experience of specific saints.

Visitors to major shrines like Our Lady of Guadalupe in Mexico City or Sainte Anne de Beaupré in Canada encounter countless testimonial plaques. Each represents someone claiming direct intervention from the saint.

Methods of Communication

Saints reach the living through multiple channels:

  • Dreams, visions, and apparitions
  • Meditation, prayer, ritual, and spellwork
  • Symbolic messages or disembodied voices
  • Human intermediaries who feel divinely moved to deliver messages or assistance
  • The miracles themselves that devotees petition for

When conventional solutions fail (incurable illness, impossible problems, inability to find work, love, or children) saints stand ready to help. Finding the right saint and making a direct request is often necessary. While saints occasionally act spontaneously, they more commonly respond to explicit appeals.

Walkers Between Worlds

Saints bridge the living and the dead. As liminal beings, they move through multiple realms. They access the afterlife and its inhabitants. They interact with angels and confront demons. They can approach the Divine to deliver prayers. Having experienced death, saints understand its mysteries. Having once lived, they empathize with human needs in ways that purely spiritual beings cannot.

Across cultures, eras, and vastly different worldviews, people have invoked their saints and attested to miracles received. This continues today.

Word Origins and Related Terms

"Saint" traces back to the Indo-European root sak, with its direct ancestor being the Latin sanctus (hallowed, consecrated). "Saint," "sacred," and "sacrifice" share common roots. The word names a category of sacred beings while also serving as an honorific.

Some traditions encourage casual familiarity. Devotees affectionately call Saint Anthony "Tony" or Saint Joseph "Joe." Others insist on formal address.

Parallel Concepts in World Traditions

Miracle-working spirits of the dead appear across nearly all spiritual paths: African Diaspora traditions, Buddhism, Hinduism, Islam, Judaism, Spiritism, Spiritualism, and Zoroastrianism. "Saint" serves as a neutral umbrella term for the venerated helpful dead regardless of tradition, just as "religion" encompasses many faiths.

Arhat (Sanskrit): Often translated as "Buddhist saint," literally meaning "worthy one" or "destroyer of enemies" (meaning greed and delusion). The Arhats studied directly under the historical Buddha.

Bodhisattva (Sanskrit): One who has achieved enlightenment and earned Nirvana but declines it, vowing instead to remain and relieve the suffering of others.

Kadosh/Kedosh (Hebrew): Literally "holy" or "sacred," a true synonym for saint. Feminine forms include Kadosha and Kedosha.

Sant (Sanskrit): From a verb meaning "to be good" or "to be real." Often used as a title, applicable to both living and dead.

Siddha (Sanskrit): "A perfected one," "one who has achieved their goal," or "one with miraculous powers."

Tzaddik/Zaddik (Hebrew): "Righteous one." Feminine forms include Tzaddika, Zaddika, and Saddika.

Wali (Arabic): From a root meaning "nearness," specifically closeness to God. The wali is God's friend and companion, also implying "guardian" or "intercessor."

Official vs. Unofficial Saints

The concept of an "unofficial" saint only exists in traditions that also recognize "official" or "authorized" saints. Once upon a time, all saints were recognized by popular acclaim alone. This informal process of conferring sainthood still exists in most religious and folk traditions.

The exception is Christianity, which over the centuries has developed a complex bureaucratic system by which saints are officially acknowledged, or not. Thus unofficial saints either are Christian or derive from spiritual traditions whose devotees are heavily influenced by Christianity or perceive themselves as Christian even if the Church disapproves of some of their practices.

An unofficial saint has not received the official stamp of approval for any number of reasons. A Latin American term for them is Santos Caseros, "homemade saints." Many devotees may be unaware that these saints are unofficial, assuming that they possess the same status as canonized saints. Some local churches discreetly incorporate veneration of unofficial saints, although in some cases, aggressive attempts may also be made to suppress this veneration.

Latin American Terminology

Latin America is home to a vast variety of unofficial saints and possesses many terms to express this concept:

  • Almas milagrosas: Miraculous souls
  • Animas: Souls or spirits
  • Animitas: Little souls or little spirits
  • Canonizaciones populares: Popularly canonized
  • Muertos milagrosos: The miraculous dead
  • Santos paganos: Pagan saints
  • Santos populares: Popular saints
  • Santos profanos: Profane saints

Why Unofficial Saints are Beloved

In some cases, unofficial saints are petitioned, venerated, and beloved precisely because it is believed that they will do things that official saints will not, like blessing illegal or unethical enterprises, or severely punishing enemies. Some unofficial saints are perceived as being more empathetic to basic human needs and desires than official saints, especially those who were extremely pious, devout, and ascetic in life.

Many unofficial saints were exceptionally devout Christians. There can be many reasons why a saint has not been canonized, including technicalities. Many assume that the saintly abbesses Hildegard of Bingen and Julian of Norwich are canonized saints, but they are not. Both remain unofficial.

The Canonization Conflict

There is a basic conflict embedded in the process that confers official sainthood:

  • The Church prefers saints who are saintly and scandal free
  • The general populace seeks active and responsive miracle workers

The result is often an attempt at compromise. Hagiographies (legends and histories of individual saints) may be sanitized in an attempt to make miracle workers appear more "saintly." This tends not to be an issue among unofficial saints, especially those not expected to ever be canonized.

Emergence of Folk Saints

Unlike canonized saints, there is no official process or waiting period to mark the emergence of folk saints (a gentler term for unofficial saints). Wide-scale veneration may pop up literally overnight, especially if the miracle worker was a beloved and well-known celebrity.

Because there is a tremendous magical, spiritual, and healing component to music, singers and musicians are also historically elevated to the status of saint. Several popular singers are folk saints in Argentina. In the United States, Elvis Presley has already evolved into an intercessor reputedly responsible for miracles of healing.

Controversy Surrounding Saint Veneration

Saints provoke controversy beyond mere language disputes. Throughout history, saints and their followers have sparked bitter, sometimes violent conflict. Veneration of saints and relics, along with shrine pilgrimages, helped ignite the Protestant Reformation. Reformers viewed these practices as idol worship.

Both Jewish and Muslim traditions contain ongoing debates about sainthood. Like Protestants, many consider saint veneration to be idolatry or dangerously close. Neither the Torah nor Quran explicitly forbids the practice, yet neither tradition has an official system for recognizing saints like Christianity does.

Saint veneration also divides Islam internally. It separates Sunni and Shia practice. The Shia permit and even encourage it; the Sunni generally do not.

Opposition to saint veneration arises from varied sources: genuine theological concerns, embarrassment at practices deemed primitive or outdated. Yet devotion to saints inspires equally fierce passion. People love their saints deeply and will fight (sometimes literally) to preserve their practices and access to shrines.

Stars and Martyrs

Some saints attract veneration from the instant of death, as though their miraculous activity is assumed. Though some emerge unexpectedly, many extraordinary individuals are widely anticipated to become saints. These expectations frequently prove accurate. Various signs can indicate sainthood potential while a person still lives.

Generally, people attain sainthood through how they lived or how they died.

Baraka: Sacred Power

Many future saints radiated intense personal power while alive. Not mere energy or loudness, but something deeper. English lacks a precise word for this quality. "Charisma" comes closest, originally meaning "divine gift" in Greek Orthodox usage, special grace from God. Modern usage has diluted it to mean any magnetic personality.

Other languages capture this better. Baraka, from North African Arabic, describes intense sacred spiritual power. This power exists in the living but does not end or fade at death. It often amplifies dramatically.

Baraka enables healing and spirit contact. It marks divine blessing and favor. It infuses everything its bearer creates or does, and it transfers. Talismans made by someone with baraka carry inherent potency. Those who display this sacred power while living are widely expected to become saints after death.

This power is recognizable without expert confirmation. You know it when you feel strengthened, renewed, and energized in someone's presence. Some manifest it quietly, perhaps unaware of their effect on others.

Others display it dramatically: certain faith healers, psychics, and spiritual leaders. Performers whose work leaves audiences spiritually transformed or even healed may possess it. Often visible during life, it sometimes only fully emerges after death.

Saints carry this sacred power beyond death and beyond life's limitations. Death does not diminish them. Their abilities amplify. This power is the essential requirement for sainthood. A saint's living behavior need not have been admirable, but their posthumous powers are expected to benefit others.

Extraordinary Abilities

Some saints lived quietly, easily overlooked. Others (the luminaries) displayed dramatic abilities now classified as paranormal. Their life stories contain remarkable accounts:

  • Levitation (sometimes sustained), bilocation, or translocation
  • Handling fire without injury
  • Prophecy and clairvoyance
  • Commanding weather: rain, wind, water
  • Stigmata: manifesting Christ's five wounds without physical cause
  • Miraculous healing; some reportedly raised the dead

Such displays have always generated controversy. Spectacular supernatural feats unsettle people, especially authorities. This was true centuries ago as it is now. During witch-hunting eras, those demonstrating such powers risked accusations of witchcraft. Catherine of Siena and Teresa of Avila now rank among Christianity's most honored saints, both designated Doctors of the Church for their lasting influence. Yet both faced witchcraft accusations while alive.

Many who displayed such powers were dismissed as frauds, mocked, or ignored. Imposters have always existed. "Mountebank" now means "charlatan" because medieval traveling healers and fortune-tellers attracted so many fakes that the term itself became tainted. Yet among the many pretenders, some defy explanation. Their feats cannot be debunked. The proof appears after death, when saints return with even greater demonstrations of power.

How Death Shapes Sainthood

Some seem born with baraka. Others first manifest it at death. The circumstances of death significantly influence sainthood potential.

Violent, sudden, or especially terrible deaths (particularly of those in life's prime) tend to produce saints. The victim's life force is perceived as so vital it cannot simply stop but continues radiating. It never had opportunity to gradually diminish. This applies especially to young women, whose creative life-giving potential is cut short. The intense emotions of such deaths may also surge the soul's power.

This same violent death also produces vengeful ghosts. Why some murder victims become wrathful spirits while others become benevolent saints remains one of the great spiritual mysteries.

Christianity's beloved virgin martyrs (Agatha, Barbara, Lucy, and Agnes) died violently, as did the Jewish martyr Lalla Solica.

Traits of Future Saints

Those who become saints often share certain characteristics:

  • Spiritual calling: Sometimes appearing early in life, sometimes late, sometimes triggered by crisis
  • Deep dedication to helping others: Even outlaws may become Robin Hood figures; some sacrifice far beyond what duty requires
  • Empathy: Some saints literally felt others' hunger and cold; many were healers while alive

Beyond Human Saints

Though saints are typically defined as once-living people who now perform miracles, exceptions exist. Not regarding the miracles, but regarding "people."

Christianity venerates three archangels as saints. Though legends describe Michael, Gabriel, and Raphael walking among humans in disguise, nothing suggests they were ever mortal.

Some banned deities (gods and goddesses displaced by newer religions) may persist as saints among devotees unaware of their true identity. Additionally, a personified death spirit and a faithful dog receive veneration as saints.

Broadly speaking, saints are deceased former mortals who display particular powers and behaviors. Their past humanity explains why the living love and rely on them so intensely. It creates the unique bond between saints and devotees. Having once lived, saints comprehend human experience: the hopes, desires, needs, and hungers that define mortality.

Name Saints and Name Days

An ancient belief, shared by many spiritual traditions, suggests that naming a child after an ancestor, saint, or other holy being creates an irrevocable bond between them. The supernatural being is considered obligated to serve as the child's patron, responding to calls for assistance as needed.

How Children Receive Name Saints

  • Children may be named after a parent's favorite saint
  • Children may be named after the saint who facilitated their conception or somehow intervened during pregnancy or childbirth
  • Many people are named after the saint whose feast day coincides with their birthday

The Calendar Roll of Saints

Christian saints are organized according to a "calendar roll of saints." This is literally a calendar, with each day of the year honoring one or more saints. Typically, there are several to choose from: male and female, famous and obscure.

For instance, July 4 is the feast day shared by Saints Isabel of Portugal, Ulrich, Bertha, Aurelian, and Odo the Good. A child born on that day may be named after any of those saints or given a variation of one of their names.

In some countries, at least until recently, only names that appeared on the calendar roll of saints were legally permissible. There is also a Christian folk belief that only those bearing the name of a saint will be permitted entry to Heaven: the name serves as a sort of sacred passport.

Celebrating Name Days

If you are named after a saint or share a saint's name, then that saint is your "name saint," even if you are not born on that saint's feast. Your name saint's feast day is your name day, even if it is not your birthday.

In some parts of South America and Central and Eastern Europe, it is customary to celebrate your name day instead of, or in addition to, your birthday. Gifts are given and parties held. You do not have to be Christian to celebrate a name day. Although the roots of the tradition are Christian, in many places the concept of the name day transcends religion and is now a shared cultural or national phenomenon.

Paths of Saints

In the context of saints, "path" is an esoteric term indicating that a holy being may manifest in distinct and unique ways. The word "road" is sometimes substituted. The Spanish word camino may also refer to the same concept. These are not literal roads or paths upon which you can tread. Instead, "path" refers to an abstract concept.

Understanding Paths

Paths may be understood as facets of one being: different sides of the same entity. Humans also sometimes manifest different sides (some people are consistently grumpy in some situations but friendly and charming in others). When it comes to saints and other holy beings, however, this concept is exponentially more complex.

Sometimes saints behave in very distinct ways, especially in different locations. They may patronize different people, possess different interests, and even use different symbols. Their personalities may be different. These distinct manifestations are known as paths.

Examples

Saint James the Greater has several paths: he may manifest as a humble pilgrim or as an eternally victorious warrior.

Saint Vitus in parts of Germany behaves very differently than this boy saint does elsewhere, resembling instead the Germanic deity Wotan.

Mary, Christianity's Holy Mother has many paths. Some people are especially devoted to Our Lady of Guadalupe, while others adore Our Lady of Mercy, the Stella Maris, or the Mystic Rose.

Why Paths Exist

The existence of paths may indicate:

  • A saint is particularly complex
  • A forbidden spirit was once secretly venerated under the guise of the saint
  • A saint may simply manifest different personalities to suit the needs of distinct constituencies

Some devotees may acknowledge all the paths of their favorite saint, but others may venerate one to the exclusion of others. Not all saints have paths.

Arthurian Saints

The ever-popular legends of King Arthur and his Knights of the Round Table have been interpreted in many ways over the centuries and from many perspectives. The Arthurian saga features powerful Pagan and Christian motifs, leading to profound and passionate debate regarding which motifs are original and which were introduced when. There is little consensus.

First written down by Geoffrey of Monmouth in 1138, the Arthurian saga is based on older Welsh and Breton legends and lore. Arthur allegedly led British resistance to Saxon invaders in the sixth century, following the departure of the Romans from the British Isles. It was a time of political and religious turbulence. Many early Welsh saints are also simultaneously characters in the Arthurian mythos or have close connections with these characters. These saints are known as the Arthurian saints.

Historical Questions

But, you argue, King Arthur didn't exist-he's only a legend. Are these saints historical people or merely legends, too? These are mysteries that have yet to be resolved. Although there is no conclusive evidence that King Arthur did exist, neither is there proof that he didn't. Many scholars believe that there was a historical figure named Arthur Pendragon upon whom these legends are based. Likewise, these saints may very well have once been historical people. Many are credited with numerous miracles to this day-so clearly someone is answering prayers.

The Grail Connection

The Arthurian saga is intertwined with legends of the Grail, which may be interpreted from a Pagan Celtic, a conventional Christian, or an esoteric Christian perspective. Likewise, Arthurian legends tend to be retold with either an intensely Christian or an intensely Pagan perspective.

  • Pagan perspective: Those who prefer this view argue that many of the Arthurian saints may originally have been beloved indigenous deities whose legends have been given a Christian veneer.
  • Christian perspective: Those who prefer this view deeply resent attempts to de-Christianize what are official saints.

Tertiaries

A tertiary is a lay member of a Roman Catholic religious order. They may wear the habit of the order and follow its rules, participating in the order's workings without necessarily living in the monastery or convent. The word derives from Latin tertius ("third"), referring to the three orders: priests (First Order), nuns (Second Order), and tertiaries (Third Order). Some tertiaries went on to become saints, including Catherine of Siena and Rose of Lima, both tertiaries of the Dominican Order.
