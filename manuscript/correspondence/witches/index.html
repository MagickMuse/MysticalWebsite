Defining the Witch

At its core, a witch is a practitioner who works with energy and natural forces to create intentional change. Witchcraft itself is the methodology, the skillset, the actual practice of directing and shaping energy toward a specific outcome. The term "magick" (spelled with a "k") refers to the energetic transformation that results from these practices, distinguishing it from stage illusions and sleight of hand.

Religion and Practice

Witchcraft carries no inherent religious requirements. Practitioners may follow any spiritual path or none at all. Historically, villages relied on "cunning folk" for practical assistance ranging from agricultural guidance to matters of the heart. These practitioners often served as healers, herbalists, and midwives. The absence of formal doctrine does not imply an absence of ethics.

Wicca and Witchcraft

These terms are often confused but represent distinct concepts. Witchcraft refers to the practice itself, a set of techniques for working with energy. Wicca is a specific spiritual tradition with established ethics, rituals, and theology. While many Wiccans practice witchcraft and many Western witches identify as Wiccan, the two are not synonymous. A witch need not be Wiccan.

Wicca's Origins and Etymology

The words Wicca and witch come from the Anglo-Saxon term wicce meaning "to bend or shape." Wicca's tenets reach back to the Old Religion of pre-Christian Europe, especially that of the early Celts. Its roots also dig deeply into prehistoric times and the ancient fertility goddesses worshipped by Paleolithic peoples.

Writer Gerald Gardner is commonly credited for coining the term Wiccan and jump-starting the modern movement in the 1950s. During the 1960s and '70s as feminism emerged, Wicca gained popularity because it offers greater balance and equality than patriarchal religions. It is one of the few faiths that honors a primary feminine deity, though one need not be female or a feminist to pursue a Wiccan path. Today Wicca is among the fastest-growing religious systems in the United States and is recognized by the U.S. military.

The Wiccan Rede and Threefold Law

Although Wiccans observe certain customs, rituals, and practices, the religion is flexible with no dogma, no sacred texts, and no laws save one: Do no harm. Wiccans follow what is known as the Threefold Law. The law states that whatever you do, whatever energies you put out, will return to you threefold in this lifetime or in the next. Therefore, Wiccans attempt to abide by the Wiccan Rede:

Bide the Wiccan law ye must,
In perfect love and perfect trust,
Eight words the Wiccan Rede fulfill:
An' ye harm none, do what ye will.
What ye send forth comes back to thee,
So ever mind the Rule of Three.
Follow this with mind and heart,
And merry ye meet, and merry ye part.

Branches of Wicca

Various branches of Wicca exist, each with somewhat different views. Dianic Wicca has a strong feminist component. Gardnerian Wicca is more formal and hierarchical than some other branches, and its practitioners perform rituals "skyclad" (nude). Like all belief systems, Wicca continues to evolve, and young enthusiasts coming to it today will surely expand its ideas, practices, and forms of expression in the future.

Terminology

Words like wizard (from "wise"), sorcerer (meaning "witch" or "diviner"), and magician apply to practitioners of any gender. Cultural context shapes how these terms are used, though all describe individuals skilled in divinatory, astrological, or spellcasting arts.

Connection with Nature

Witches develop close relationships with the natural world, often viewing themselves as participants in nature rather than separate from it. Many practitioners extend reverence to plants, animals, and the elements, recognizing their interconnected role in the web of life. This relationship moves beyond appreciation into active dialogue, treating the earth as a living partner in magical work.

Animism and Pantheism

Many witches, green witches, shamans, and other magicians believe that all things in nature possess a type of consciousness. The term animism refers to this belief. The consciousness within even seemingly inanimate objects enables witches to use them in spellworking.

The term pantheism is sometimes used to describe Neopagan paths. Often misunderstood as meaning the worship of nature, it actually means to recognize the Divine in all places, or to identify the Divine with the universe. The root word "pan" means "everywhere," and earth-honoring people believe that their deities are accessible in all places, at all times.

This does not mean witches worship trees, rivers, and stones any more than astrologers worship the sun, moon, and planets. It means they believe that the entire world, material and nonmaterial, possesses sacred energy, and that each of us holds a spark of divine energy within us.

Signs and Omens

Many practitioners pay attention to omens, unexpected events, or natural phenomena that carry symbolic meaning. The appearance of certain animals, unusual weather patterns, or seemingly coincidental occurrences may be interpreted as messages or guidance. This awareness of signs reflects the witch's attunement to the subtle communications woven through the natural world.

Views on the Divine

Witches, Wiccans, and Neopagans often disagree about the nature of the Divine. Some follow a specific faith and worship one or more gods and goddesses; some are not religious at all. Wiccans honor a Goddess and a God as her consort, while Neopagans often recognize many deities. Many witches consider all spiritual paths equally valid and leading to the same place. Who or what you believe in-if anything-is entirely up to you.

Monotheism means a belief in a single supreme being. Dualism means a belief in two deities; in a Wiccan context, this would be God and Goddess. Polytheism is the belief in many separate gods. Henotheism means believing in one god without denying the existence of others.

Facets of the Divine

As cultures interacted through war, trade, and migration, conceptions of the divine realm evolved. Some spirits fell out of favor as people learned more about the physical world. Minor tribal deities merged with or gave way to deities with greater powers. Some deities went by different names in different countries-Venus in Rome, Aphrodite in Greece-although their attributes were essentially the same.

A popular metaphor describes divine energy as a gemstone, with every facet representing a different manifestation of the core energy. These manifestations present themselves differently, but they all come from the same divine source. Some witches naturally relate to gods and goddesses that are part of their personal heritage: Scandinavians might gravitate to Freya, Greeks to Sophia, Irish to Brigid. Santeríans combine Catholicism with African Paganism, honoring deities from both traditions.

You Are Already a Magician

The word magician derives from the Latin magi, meaning wise men or women. Every culture reaching back before written history has had its practitioners: medicine folk, cunning people, kahunas, Druids, witches, and shamans. By choosing a magical path, you follow in the footsteps of ancient seers and healers who knew how to shape universal forces with their intentions. You have likely already performed magick without realizing it, forming objectives in your mind and fueling them with willpower.

What Magick Can Offer

Magick serves as a tool to give you power over your circumstances rather than being subject to them. Spells and workings can improve relationships, attract prosperity, enhance health, protect home and loved ones, open opportunities, strengthen intuition, and ward off obstacles. Nothing strange or sinister exists in this practice. Magick is a natural ability you were born with, a talent that can be developed like any other skill.

Ancient Origins

Witchcraft's origins are hidden in antiquity. People around the world have practiced magick in some form since the beginning of time. Anthropologists speculate that Stonehenge may have been a sacred site where magick rituals were performed thousands of years ago. The famous paintings on the Trois Frères cave walls in France, dating back 15,000 years, may represent an early form of sympathetic magick, where Paleolithic peoples sought the aid of spirit animals to help them succeed at hunting.

The Old Religion

At the dawn of the human race, when people first came to understand cause and effect, they began trying to explain the mysteries of earth and the heavens. If a wind blew down a tree and hurt someone, the wind might be thought of as angry or considered a spirit needing appeasement. People began to anthropomorphize aspects of nature, imagining that gods and goddesses, spirits and demons, and all sorts of fantastic creatures lived in the unseen realms, where they governed everything that happened on earth. From this, magickal thinking was born.

The Interconnected Web

One of the foundational ideas of magick holds that the universe is a huge web made up of invisible interlocking strands. Everything is connected to everything else. If humans could learn to influence one of these connections, they could affect the whole web. Early attempts to influence the world were simple: one action to produce one result. The action usually corresponded symbolically to the desired result. Someone wanting to bind an angry spirit might tie a knot in a piece of rope, imagining the spirit caught in that knot. If the action worked, it was used again, and eventually a tradition developed.

Wise Men and Women

Over time, attempts at guiding fate became more elaborate. Communities delegated the tasks of influencing the universe to a few wise individuals, elevating them to positions of authority. These wise men and women were called shamans, priests and priestesses, magi, or witches. Their job included performing spells and rituals to coerce the ancestors, powerful spirits, or deities into doing their bidding. Although they performed the same basic functions across cultures, including healing the sick, encouraging crops to grow, and predicting the future, how they went about it depended on their culture and era. In early Celtic communities, the Druids served as seers, healers, advisors, astrologers, and spiritual leaders. An ancient Norse text called the Poetic Edda uses the term völva to describe a wise woman who did prophecies, cast spells, and performed healing.

European Witchcraft

Historians disagree about the evolution of witchcraft in Europe. Some believe it developed out of old fertility cults worshipping a mother goddess. Others think the idea of witchcraft was superstition, used to explain unpleasant events. Still others say witchcraft stemmed from Paganism, Hebrew mysticism, Celtic tradition, and ancient Greek folklore. As people traveled, they influenced the beliefs and practices of native cultures. When Vikings and Romans invaded the British Isles, their legends, gods, and goddesses mixed with those of the indigenous people. Because most people in earlier centuries could not read or write, magickal traditions were handed down through oral teaching, making it difficult to separate truth from fantasy.

Criminalizing Witchcraft

During the eighth and ninth centuries, authorities began laying down laws against witchcraft. As the Christian Church gained power, it attacked the old religion based in nature and folk traditions. In 743, the Synod of Rome declared it a crime to leave offerings for spirits. In 829, the Synod of Paris passed a decree against reciting incantations and idolatry. By 900, Christian scholars were promoting the idea that the devil was leading women astray. Between the 1100s and 1300s, the Church continued its assault. Witchcraft became a crime against God and the Church. In 1317, Pope John XXII authorized the Inquisition to pursue anyone believed to have made a pact with the devil. Thousands of trials proceeded. In 1484, the Papal Bull of Innocent VIII unleashed the full power of the Inquisition. The publication of the Malleus Maleficarum in 1486 laid the groundwork for a reign of terror that held Europe in its grip into the seventeenth century.

The Burning Times

The witch-hunt craze picked up speed in the sixteenth century during the Reformation. Legal sanctions became harsher and tortures grew crueler. Inquisitors used the rack, thumbscrews, boots, and hot coals to force confessions. Those found guilty were burned at the stake. During the Burning Times, which lasted from the fourteenth to the eighteenth centuries, tens of thousands and possibly millions of people were executed as witches, most of them women and girls. After Germany's witch trials of 1585, two villages in the Bishopric of Trier were left with only one woman surviving in each. Some individuals profited from the witch hunts through payments, confiscated property, or elimination of competitors. The hysteria finally subsided as people grew weary of violence. In England, the hunts declined after the early 1700s when the witch statute was repealed. The last recorded execution occurred in Germany in 1775.

Witchcraft in the New World

In the Americas, witchcraft evolved as a patchwork of beliefs and practices. Medicine men and women of indigenous tribes had engaged in witchcraft and shamanism for centuries, tapping the plant kingdom for healing and communing with spirits. When European settlers arrived, they brought their customs with them, including some who followed the Old Religion and sought freedom in a new land. The slave trade introduced African traditions including voudon, Santería, and macumba, which continue to flourish today in the Caribbean and southern United States.

The Salem Witch Trials

In 1692, when a village doctor in Salem, Massachusetts could not heal the ailing daughter and niece of Reverend Samuel Parris, he claimed the girls had been bewitched. Soon girls throughout Salem and surrounding communities were crying out the names of supposed witches. Between June and October, nineteen men and women were hung and another man was crushed to death for the crime of witchcraft. More than 150 others were thrown into prison. Religious and political factors combined to create the craze. A recent smallpox epidemic and attacks by Indian tribes had left the community fearful. Economic interests also played a role, as the convicted had their assets confiscated. Many of the accused women owned property and were not governed by husbands or male relatives, which did not sit well with the male-dominated society. One theory suggests the strange behavior exhibited by the afflicted may have been caused by ergot, a hallucinogenic fungus that grows on rye bread.

Different Paths of Witchcraft

Just as musicians approach their craft in personal ways-some classically trained and performing complex pieces, others unable to read music but gifted at improvisation-witches may choose paths ranging from simple to incredibly complex. Some practitioners prefer uncomplicated, natural magick while others perform elaborate rituals drawing from mystical traditions. The path you choose depends on your personality, preferences, and talents.

Kitchen and Hedge Witches

Kitchen and hedge witches generally practice uncomplicated, natural magick. They typically work alone rather than in covens, though they may join other witches for special purposes. As solitary practitioners, they rely on self-study, insight, creativity, and intuition as guideposts. Their practice often includes plant and herbal magick, frequently for healing purposes. These witches feel at home working with familiar, everyday materials and tapping into the magic of domestic life and the natural world immediately around them.

In some circles, these terms connote a person who engages in shamanic practice involving spirit journeys or trancing, often with the aid of herbal knowledge. In earlier times, wise women, cunning men, and other such practitioners were sometimes called green witches or hedge witches. They worked to heal individuals, communities, and any malaise in the natural world.

Home as Sanctuary

Hearth and home occupy a central place in the kitchen witch's spiritual and magickal work. The home becomes a temple and sanctuary, tended to keep energy flowing smoothly and freely. Kitchen witches work out of their own homes, making those spaces places of healing energy and knowledge that provide shelter and nourishment for both body and spirit. They seek to support, nurture, and nourish family and extended community, both spiritually and physically.

Hedge witches are often solitary practitioners who work alone rather than with a coven. They may be self-dedicated but are rarely publicly initiated into the field. They are more likely to have learned the Craft at a grandmother's side or eased into it as an extension of growing herbs in a backyard garden.

Home-Based Spirituality

The concept of the home as a spiritual center appears across many cultures and eras. The home, and in particular the hearth, has often served as a point of connection between deities and humankind. In China, the Kitchen God is viewed as an important domestic deity, and families hang paper images of the god near their stoves.

In the West, kitchen witches use two symbols as joint keystones: the cauldron and the flame. The cauldron traditionally represents abundance and hospitality. In magick, it also symbolizes rebirth, mystery, creation, fertility, transformation, and feminine power. The flame is a symbol of life, activity, the Divine, purification, inspiration, and masculine power, making it an excellent partner for the cauldron.

The Broom's True Purpose

The kitchen witch bases magickal practice in everyday household activities. Cooking, cleaning, and baking all form the basis for the work. For example, sweeping the floor free of dust and dirt may simultaneously cleanse the space of negative energy. This is the real reason witches use brooms, not to fly across the sky.

The Hedge as Barrier

Although we usually think of hedges as surrounding property, for witches the hedge is more than a physical barrier. It symbolizes spiritual protection from the stresses of the outside world. It can also be seen as a barrier between the world of humans and the spirit realm.

Ceremonial and Ritualistic Witchcraft

Other practitioners perform magick with more ritualistic overtones, drawing inspiration from mystical movements such as the Qabalah and other spiritual traditions. These witches view every aspect of a spell or ritual as part of a larger picture where each piece must be in the right place for everything to work properly. The astrological phase of the moon should align with the task. Special clothing may be worn and movement may follow choreographed patterns. Every element of the working is designed to build energy toward the desired outcome. Those with a flair for the dramatic often find ceremonial magick appealing.

Origins of the Freemasons

During the Middle Ages, certain tradesmen became members of secret, mystical guilds. Because they possessed special knowledge of magick and symbolism alongside skills in carpentry, masonry, or glasswork, these artisans were hired to work on Europe's great cathedrals. The famous rose windows they created were said to have unique healing properties due to a type of magick not fully understood even today. These early guilds eventually spawned the Freemasons.

Green Witches

Green witches walk the path of the naturalist, the herbalist, the wise woman, and the healer. Earth serves as their primer and the natural world as their classroom. With the resurgence of nature-based practices and environmental awareness, green witches are once again emerging as guardians of nature and humanity's relationship with the planet.

Green witches use nature's gifts to improve physical well-being, nourish spirit and soul, and care for the environment. In earlier times, midwives, herbalists, shamans, and healers knew the powers of plants-both medicinal and magickal-and their very lives depended on existing in harmony with nature and its cycles.

The Modern Green Witch

Today's green witches honor the earth and all its inhabitants-rocks, plants, and animals. They utilize ingredients from nature in spellcasting, particularly herbs and crystals. They work to protect the environment and live in harmony with creation, sometimes interacting with devas, elementals, or nature spirits.

Historically, green witches lived apart from their communities, and those needing their services traveled to find them. Modern green witches are more likely to live in cities or suburbs with small gardens or container plants. They understand that restoring nature's balance requires bringing their knowledge into the wider world. Cities, highways, and damaged areas need the green witch's healing powers.

Living the Green Path

What makes a green witch is not where they live or how they earn income, but their relationship to the world, their ethics, and their affinity with nature. The green path combines aspects of both witchcraft and shamanism while being wholly neither. It integrates personal ability, preferences, local climate, and interaction with environmental energy.

Healing, harmony, and balance are key to the green witch's practice, embodying three distinct focuses: the earth (local environment and planet), humanity (community and relationships), and oneself. Green witchcraft is not a formal tradition passed through careful training but a personal adaptation of an ideal. The green witch's power comes from participating in the miracle of life and attuning to environmental energies rather than striving to amass power.

Choosing Your Path

Where you live may influence your path. In the American Southwest, shamanism influenced by Indigenous traditions is common; in Salem, Massachusetts, many Wiccans practice openly. Consider your temperament: Do you enjoy group activities and working toward common goals? A coven might suit you. Are you a loner and homebody? You may be a natural hedge witch. Do you feel a strong connection to the earth and love the outdoors? Green witchcraft might be your calling. Whatever draws you, the path of the witch allows room for personal expression and growth.
