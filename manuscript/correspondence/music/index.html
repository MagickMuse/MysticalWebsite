Symbolism

Music is truly divine, and legends the world over, across every faith, agree that it was a gift of the gods. Pythagoras, like the Chinese before him, understood that music carries the harmony of the cosmos, holding within it the vibrational values of numbers, shapes, and sounds. He called this the "Music of the Spheres," a universal symphony of interconnectedness. If sound is the great unifying factor, music is the most sublime expression of that connection, for the same mathematical rules that underpin sacred geometry govern the harmonious or discordant vibrations of strings sounded together.

Music can mirror our mood or transform it. In myth it brings enchanted sleep as readily as it rouses soldiers to battle. It has much in common with perfume, being invisible yet all-pervading, able to be felt by many people at once. Like the scented smoke of incense, music plays a central role in sacred and magickal ritual, a unifying thread in orthodox and pagan ceremony alike.

The Devil's Chord

Just as music can herald a shift of mood or scene, it can also signal the presence of evil. The Devil's Chord, or "Diabolus in Musica," is a dissonant, unsettling interval such as the augmented fourth, so charged with menace that it was said to have been banned from Church music in the Middle Ages.

The Stones of Rosslyn

Rosslyn Chapel in Scotland, long a site of mystical conjecture, has yielded another secret. A series of carved stone cubes in the chapel each bear a particular etched pattern, and these correspond to Chladni patterns. When sand, salt, or another fine powder is scattered on a taut surface, such as a drumhead, and that surface is met by a musical tone, the vibrations gather the powder into symmetrical shapes: the diamonds, rhomboids, and flower-like forms that appear on the cubes. After some twenty years of research, the notes thought to be encoded in the stones were arranged into a piece of music.
