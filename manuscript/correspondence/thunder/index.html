It is natural to imagine that the rolling of thunder in the Heavens comes from the gods raging across the sky, provoked by some offense and readying their vengeance. For this reason the great thunder gods are shown gripping the distinctive triple-headed trident shape of the thunderbolt, which resembles a sheaf of barbed arrows, a mark of their power and their dominion over one of the most fearsome phenomena of the sky. At times the thunderbolt is pictured instead as a tool, a hammer or an axe, such as Thor's magickal hammer Mjolnir, the Labrys or double-headed axe of Zeus, or the Vajra of the Hindu god Indra. Taranis, the Celtic god whose very name means thunder, is known by his fierce expression, by the rather phallic thunderbolt scepter in one hand, and by the great wheel he holds in the other. The African god Faro carries his thunderbolts as whips that also stand for lightning.

The double edge of both the Labrys and the Vajra points to the dual nature of the thunderbolt. It destroys, but it also creates, for it embodies the transforming power of the Heavens upon the Earth. Dangerous though it is, the thunderbolt comes hand in hand with the rain that replenishes the land. Anyone or anywhere struck by a thunderbolt was thought to be made holy on the instant. Animals felled by one were held sacrosanct, the chosen of the gods, and Siberian peoples would raise such creatures up on platforms as an offering back to the powers that struck them down.

The Vajra carries a further meaning, that of divine inspiration that strikes in a figurative rather than a physical way. In this sense it stands for the power of the word and of the mind.
