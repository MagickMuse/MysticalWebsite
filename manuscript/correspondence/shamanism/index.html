The Oldest Path

Shamanism represents humanity's oldest spiritual technology, with roots stretching back tens of thousands of years. Found across every inhabited continent, shamanic practices share common features while expressing through diverse cultural forms. The word itself comes from the Tungus people of Siberia, but the role exists worldwide under many names.

The Shaman's Role

Traditionally, shamans serve as intermediaries between the human community and the spirit world. They heal illness (often understood as soul loss or spiritual intrusion), divine the future, guide the dead, communicate with ancestors and nature spirits, and maintain balance between seen and unseen realms. This role typically comes through calling, often marked by illness, near-death experience, or unmistakable spiritual encounters.

Altered States

Shamanic work requires entering non-ordinary states of consciousness to journey into spirit realms. Traditional methods include rhythmic drumming, rattling, chanting, fasting, sensory deprivation, and in some cultures, plant medicines. In these states, the shaman's soul travels to upper, middle, and lower worlds to gather information, retrieve lost soul parts, or negotiate with spirits.

Spirit Allies

Shamans work closely with helping spirits who guide, protect, and empower their work. These may include power animals (animal spirits who lend their qualities and protection), ancestor spirits, nature spirits of place, and various other beings. Developing and maintaining these relationships is central to shamanic practice.

Core Techniques

Modern practitioners often work with "core shamanism," techniques distilled from cross-cultural patterns by researchers like Michael Harner. These include the shamanic journey (using drumming to enter trance), power animal retrieval, soul retrieval, extraction healing, and psychopomp work (guiding spirits of the dead).

Ethical Considerations

When approaching shamanism from outside indigenous cultures, practitioners must navigate questions of cultural appropriation thoughtfully. Many choose to work with their own ancestral traditions, seek training from legitimate teachers, and approach indigenous practices with respect rather than entitlement.
