Egyptian magic is built on the belief that the spoken word, physical gesture, and material object must work together to create change. Understanding this framework is essential before working with Egyptian entities.

Djed Medu: The Words to Be Said

The phrase djed medu ("the words to be said") marks the beginning of spells in Egyptian magic. It signals that what follows must be spoken aloud. Egyptian magic required three components working in harmony:

  1. Speech act: The incantation spoken with proper intonation
  2. Physical gesture or action: The ritual movement or manipulation
  3. Material object: The physical anchor that binds speech and action

Writing alone was not enough. The spoken performance activated the magic. Priests recited over objects, spoke over the dead, and vocalized the names written on temple walls to keep their power alive. How something was said mattered as much as what was said: tone shifts, hissing, growling, tongue clicks, and lip pops may have been part of proper incantation.

Heka: The Force of Magic

Heka is the Egyptian word for magic itself, a cosmic force older than the gods. In the Pyramid Texts, heaven groans and earth trembles in its presence. Heka is not something borrowed from the divine; it predates divinity. The gods themselves need it and seek it.

Isis gained her title Weret Hekau ("Great Lady of Magic") by tricking Ra into revealing his secret name. She shaped a clay snake, brought it to life with her magic, and let it bite the aging sun god. Only by surrendering his secret name could he be healed. That name, whatever it was, passed directly from Ra's body into hers, granting her supreme magical authority.

The concept of heka was so central that some Egyptians carried it in their personal names: Hekawy, Hekaf, Hekaemsaf ("Magic is his protection").

The Power of Names

Names in Egyptian thought captured something essential about a person or being. Gods accumulated many names and epithets because more names meant more power. Knowing a name gave you leverage over it, which is why Ra guarded his secret name so fiercely.

The cartouche, an elongated shen symbol, encircled royal names to protect them. The dead were addressed as "the Osiris so-and-so" to link them with the god of the afterlife. Speaking or writing a name with the proper formulas could bless or curse.

This is why Egyptian curse magic (execration) worked by writing enemy names on clay figures or pots, then smashing, burning, or burying them while reciting destructive formulas. The act of destroying the name was believed to harm or destroy the person.

The Mythological Framework

The cycle of Osiris, Isis, Horus, and Seth forms the backbone of Egyptian magical practice.

Osiris ruled Egypt in the time of the gods until his brother Seth murdered him, dismembering his body and scattering the pieces. Isis, both wife and sister to Osiris, gathered the pieces and used her magic to reassemble him. Anubis mummified the body, creating the template for funerary rites. Though Osiris could not return to the living world, he became Lord of the Dead.

Isis hid her son Horus in the Delta marshes, protecting him with her magic until he was old enough to challenge Seth. After battles and a divine tribunal, Horus was declared rightful heir to Egypt's throne.

This cycle connects:

  • Death and resurrection (Osiris)
  • Protective and restorative magic (Isis)
  • Funerary rites and mummification (Anubis, Osiris)
  • Righteous vengeance and kingship (Horus)
  • Chaos and disruption (Seth)

When you invoke Egyptian entities, you step into this mythic framework.

Core Egyptian Symbols

Several hieroglyphic symbols carried amuletic power and appear frequently in Egyptian magical practice:

  • Ankh: Life. Gods hold it; kings are "given life" through it. Also the word for mirror.
  • Djed: Stability. Associated with Osiris's spine or the tree that housed his body.
  • Tyet: The "Knot of Isis." Blood-red, shaped like an ankh with folded arms. Combines with djed to represent male and female creative forces.
  • Shen: Eternity and protection through encircling. The cartouche is an elongated shen.
  • Sa: Protection. Shaped like a folded reed bundle, possibly an ancient life preserver.

Knotting and Amulet Magic

The Egyptian word for knot, tjes, connects to a widespread practice: binding incantations into physical form. A magician would tie knots in strips of linen (always pure, undyed linen) while reciting spells. Each knot "locked" the words into the fabric. The knotted cloth became a wearable amulet, tied around the neck or applied to the affected body part.

Papyrus amulets worked similarly. Spells or protective images were written on fresh papyrus, folded tightly, and tied with knotted cord. The combination of written word, spoken formula, and binding knot created layered protection.

Materials mattered:

  • Linen: Plant-based, connected to agricultural cycles and purity. Used in mummification and temple rites.
  • Fresh papyrus: Never reused. The writing surface had to be untouched.
  • Red and black ink: Red marked instructions and ingredients; black marked the words to be spoken.

Execration Magic

Execration (formal cursing) was state-sanctioned Egyptian magic aimed at enemies of cosmic order (maat). Figures of bound prisoners, made from clay, wax, or stone, were inscribed with enemy names, then ritually smashed, trampled, burned, or buried.

One excavated ritual pit at Mirgissa (circa 1800 BCE) contained fragments of over 700 intentionally smashed pots, melted wax figures tinted red, and a decapitated human skull. This was a complex, multi-stage working requiring significant preparation.

Execration could also be personal. Individual names, including women's names, appear on curse figures found in cemeteries. The practice shows that Egyptian magic could harm as effectively as it could heal.

Healing Magic and Medicine

Magic was the first line of defense against diseases in ancient Egypt, as well as the best chance of a cure. Most people relied on a combination of magical charms and simple practices such as cleaning a wound, applying an ointment, or ingesting whatever concoction their local magician offered. Recovery was proof that the magic had worked, while for those who never recovered, that was proof that the demons who caused disease were very powerful indeed.

Healing spells were collected together on papyrus scrolls, sometimes organized by ailment (snake bites, eye problems, "female problems"), and sometimes mixed in with other magical invocations. "Very effective! It's worked a thousand times!" was often written at the end of a healing spell, like a quality mark for wizardry.

Priests of Sekhmet as Physicians

Priests in the service of the lion-goddess Sekhmet were the nearest equivalent to modern doctors. Other magicians offered healing services as well. Amulet-makers, scorpion-charmers, and priests of Sekhmet were usually happy to make home visits.

Identification with the Gods

An important principle of medical magic was identification with the gods. The magician pronouncing a spell took on the identity of a healing god (Isis, Thoth), while the afflicted person was identified with the ailing child (Horus). We are all vulnerable, like a child, when suffering and unwell. By establishing a divine precedent for recovery, these healing formulas offered reassurance that the cure would work.

The Magical Child: Horus-on-the-Crocodiles

The story of Horus's childhood was a source of healing inspiration. For a magical child living under divine protection, he ran into an awful lot of trouble, and his mother Isis constantly had to use her powers to heal him or save him from threats.

A common healing motif shows young Horus standing on crocodiles, grasping lions, antelopes, scorpions, and snakes in his hands, representing harmful forces he has conquered. The protective face of Bes appears overhead. This image appears on cippi (healing stelae) and portable amulets throughout Egypt.

The Metternich Stela

The most famous healing monument is the Metternich Stela, set up in a temple around 360-343 BCE. Standing around 84 centimeters high, it was incised on every surface with magical figures and spells offering healing and protection. Water poured over the stela would absorb the words, and those who drank the water took that magic into their own bodies.

The word for a remedy was pekher, meaning "to encircle" or "to go around," the same as the word for magical enchantment. Walking around the stela may have been part of the cure. Circular motions helped activate the magic.

Portable Healing Magic

Carved versions of the Horus-on-the-crocodiles motif that fit in the hand allowed healing power to reach more people. Many wearable Horus objects and amulets either lack inscribed spells, or have such cramped or imitation inscriptions that the image itself must have contained adequate magical power, together with the words the healer recited over it.

A magical text or drawing made on papyrus and folded up tightly worked just as well when it came from the hands of a magician, accompanied by his incantations. Such amulets often appear at the throats of small children in mummy portraits from Roman Egypt.

Knotting Magic for Healing

Knotting magic was used for healing too. The knots locked in the words a magician spoke during a healing rite. In one spell against headache, the magician assumed the role of Seth and the suffering person became Horus. The magician recited healing words while tying knots into a cord, which was then placed around the foot of the patient.

Egyptian Herbal Medicine

Most magical remedies were based on plant sources. Ground into a paste or powder, plant ingredients were combined with water, honey, milk, animal fat, or vegetable oil. Depending on the complaint, the mixture might be swallowed, applied as a poultice, ointment, or eye wash. Egyptian healers were fond of pessaries and suppositories, and they also burned ingredients to fumigate a space.

One memorable remedy for headaches called for rubbing the head with oil in which the head of a catfish had been fried, offering either relief or at least a little something to snack on.

Common healing ingredients included:

  • Wormwood: Effectiveness against intestinal worms; mixed with juniper berries, honey, and beer for rectal complaints
  • Acacia leaves: Astringent properties for open sores and broken bones
  • Willow and Christ-thorn: Pain-relieving effects
  • Myrtle leaves: Antiseptic properties
  • Marshmallow: Leaves, flowers, and seeds used in various remedies
  • Carob tree: Pods and gum; mixed with beer, milk, honey, tiger nuts, and wine as a laxative
  • Celery: Mixed with beer as a chew-and-spit substance for tooth pain
  • Linseeds: Soothing poultice for skin wounds
  • Aloe: Soothing burns and skin inflammations; also used as a purgative
  • Castor oil and figs: Laxative qualities
  • Dill, fennel, cumin, coriander: Dill for pain-soothing drinks, fennel as a diuretic, cumin in cough remedies and tooth powders, coriander for stomach ailments

Medicine that had to be ingested often included wine or beer in the mixture.

Disguised Ingredients

Strange or stomach-churning ingredient names helped amplify and mystify the magical power of more mundane ingredients. A bilingual Greek and Egyptian papyrus contains a glossary: a snake's head was actually a leech, its blood was red ochre, and an ibis bone was the buckthorn plant. Baboon blood could be retrieved from a spotted gecko, while baboon tears and hair-of-baboon were simply dill. Human bile? Just turnip sap. Crocodile dung was Ethiopian soil.

Magic works by obscuring its methods. Giving substances repulsive labels made the cure seem terribly complicated and mysterious.

Imhotep and Amenhotep: Divine Healers

Some men became so famous for their healing powers that they were honoured as gods. The priest Imhotep lived in the reign of the 3rd Dynasty King Djoser (c. 2600 BCE) and has been credited as an architect, physician, and polymath. Originally based at Saqqara, the worship of Imhotep became widespread throughout Egypt, and thousands of bronze statuettes representing him survive.

Amenhotep, son of Hapu, lived during the late 18th Dynasty and directed building projects for King Amenhotep III. The king allowed Amenhotep to erect statues of himself in the temple of Amun at Karnak. These statues stood outside temple gateways for centuries, inviting local people to pray to him. The stone surface wore away over time as thousands of people touched the statues.

In the Ptolemaic Period, Imhotep and Amenhotep formed a healing pair in several temples around Thebes. Temples honoured both men as priests, magicians, and medical men. Their sanctuary at Deir el-Bahri may have offered sleeping spaces where the faithful could spend the night, hoping for a healing message from the gods in their dreams.

Working with Egyptian Magic

When approaching Egyptian entities or practices:

  1. Speak your workings aloud. Silent intention is not enough in this system.
  2. Combine word, action, and object. All three components should be present.
  3. Respect the power of names. Use proper names and epithets when addressing entities.
  4. Understand the mythic context. Know where an entity fits in the Osiris-Isis-Horus-Seth framework.
  5. Use appropriate materials. Linen, papyrus, and specific inks carry traditional resonance.

The Duat: The Egyptian Underworld

The Duat was the Egyptian realm of the dead, an otherworld that mirrored the land of the living in distorted form. Maps, images, and texts describe a landscape of caverns, deserts, treacherous rivers, and lakes of fire. The Duat was dark and cold, or blazingly hot. The ground trembled. Strange sounds split the silence. Obstacles and traps made navigation perilous.

The Duat encompassed the entire cosmic landscape through which Ra traveled each night. His solar vessel passed through twelve hours of darkness, confronting the chaos serpent Apophis before emerging reborn at dawn. This journey formed the subject of the "Underworld Books" found on royal tomb walls during the New Kingdom.

Ra's regeneration occurred at the Duat's center, where Osiris lay buried in a secret canyon. The union of these two gods restored Ra's strength as dawn approached, resetting the cosmic cycle for another day.

The dead needed amulets and spells to navigate the Duat safely. Demons dwelling there reversed normal behavior: they ate excrement, moved backward, and had body parts in wrong positions. These hostile forces required magical countermeasures.

Spirit Types: Akhu and Mwtw

Not all spirits of the dead were equal. The Egyptians distinguished between beneficial and harmful ghosts.

Akhu (the "shining" or "illuminated" ones) were the blessed dead who had passed through judgment successfully. Becoming an akh was the ultimate goal of funerary rituals. These spirits could protect and bless the living, particularly their descendants. Families kept memorial shrines in their homes, sometimes with sculpted busts representing revered ancestors. Offerings and prayers maintained good relations with the akhu.

Mwtw (rhymes with "tutu") were spirits with hostile intentions. They caused illness, mental anguish, and nightmares. Some may have died violent or untimely deaths. Others failed judgment and were denied peaceful eternity. Even formerly benevolent relatives could turn into mwtw if neglected or offended.

Surviving spells show how magicians banished these malevolent spirits:

"Oh male adversary or female adversary, male akh or female akh, be far away from So-and-so! Oh male mwt or female mwt, you will not come!"

The uraeus, the rearing cobra on royal and divine foreheads, offered protection against such spirits. Magicians placed clay cobra figures with flames in their mouths in the four corners of sleeping rooms.

Letters to the Dead

When troubles plagued a household, Egyptians sometimes suspected a deceased relative was responsible. The solution was to write the ghost a letter. A scribe would compose the message on a pottery bowl or vase, which the family filled with offerings and left in the tomb chapel. Letters could also be written on papyrus, potsherds, or stelae.

One widower wrote to his deceased wife:

"How are you? Is the West taking care of you as you wish? Now, since I am your beloved on earth, fight on my behalf and intercede on behalf of my name. Remove the infirmity from my body! Please become an akh-spirit for me so that I may see you, fighting on my behalf, in a dream."

Some writers were blunter. One son chastised his dead parents: "The two of you are there, only looking after your own interests." Another husband protested to his dead wife that he had never divorced her when he became successful. These letters reveal that relationships with the dead required ongoing maintenance, negotiation, and sometimes conflict.

Mummification as Magic

Mummification was not merely physical preservation. It was a religious ritual intimately connected to magic and mythology.

The process took seventy days, corresponding to the time certain constellations (the decans) took to travel across the sky. This number linked the dead person's transformation to cosmic cycles of renewal.

A reading-priest (khery-heb) recited ritual formulas throughout the embalming and wrapping process. The circular action of wrapping the body, finger by finger and limb by limb, embedded protection and wholeness into the mummy. Amulets placed at various stages added magical insurance:

  • Tyet (Knot of Isis) in red carnelian
  • Djed pillar in blue-green faience
  • Heart scarab inscribed with a spell imploring it not to testify against the deceased at judgment
  • Four Sons of Horus amulets over the abdomen, each protecting a different organ

The Four Sons of Horus guarded the viscera removed during embalming:

  • Imsety (human head): liver
  • Duamutef (jackal head): stomach
  • Hapi (baboon head): lungs
  • Qebehsenuef (falcon head): intestines

Tomb paintings, inscribed stelae, and models of food production could magically provide perpetual offerings to the spirit. Representing ideal offerings in words and images activated them through magic, ensuring the dead remained well-nourished forever.

A Magical Menagerie: Animals in Egyptian Magic

Animals both real and imagined were vital to Egyptian magical practice. Human figures of different ages, genders, and body shapes were combined with parts of animals, birds, and reptiles. This was not monster worship. Instead, this diversity enabled Egyptian artists and magicians to harness the potential of an otherwise invisible supernatural world.

Any image made to represent a powerful animal, a semi-divine spirit, or a god had the potential to channel supernatural energy. The image's potential dangers thus needed to be constrained through ritual.

Faience Hippopotamus Figures

Archaeologists have found shiny blue hippopotamus figures in Middle Kingdom burials. These hippos share two characteristics:

  1. Made of faience: The glazed paste was so tricky to produce that it was believed to have magical qualities. Bodies are adorned with lotus leaves, buds, and blossoms, as if the animal merged with the plants of its marshy home.
  2. Deliberately broken: Each had a foot broken off or damaged when placed in the tomb. Some were broken in two.

The breakage is all that remains of the magic ritual that was their entire reason for being. Reduced to manageable size in magic-imbued faience form, these hippopotami helped the priest-magician control the destructive power of the animal and all it symbolized. Once hobbled and tamed by magic words and actions, the figure could also protect the tomb and the dead from harm.

The hippo's habit of moving between land and water echoed Egyptian ideas about creation. But its dangerous side encouraged its association with Seth, god of disorder. A conquered hippopotamus symbolized triumph over the forces of disorder, exactly the metaphor a magician would want for rites aimed at righting wrongs, healing the sick, or averting disaster.

Objects made of hippopotamus ivory had special magical potency, including wands used for health and protection during childbirth and care of small children.

The Seth Animal

The "Seth animal" was a four-legged creature with a down-turned snout, long ears with squared-off ends, and a tail that sometimes took the form of an arrow, as if pinning this weird creature down to stop it from causing havoc.

This inconceivable animal represented Seth, god of disorder on an almost inconceivable scale, threatening no less than the destruction of the natural order itself. The beast was not all bad: in the Delta during the New Kingdom, Seth had a thriving cult. He is honored in the names of two 19th Dynasty kings, Sety I and Sety II. Nevertheless, most magic spells mentioning Seth aimed to curtail his negative influence.

Jackals: Masters of Secrets

The jackal was identified with two gods who look almost identical in Egyptian art:

  • Anubis: Responsible for embalming and wrapping the corpse of Osiris
  • Wepwawet: "Opener of the Ways" or "Route-finder," a sort of sniffer dog to the supernatural realm

Jackals towed the sun-god's boat through the sky, their tails sometimes represented as rearing cobras. A jackal stretched out alert on top of a chest was the hieroglyphic sign for hery-seshta ("master of secrets"), the vital skill that priest-magicians needed.

A wooden jackal draped in linen and adorned with a floral wreath guarded the innermost room of Tutankhamun's tomb. Inside the chest it rested upon were faience amulets, shabti-figures, djed-amulets, and jewelry, the remains of materials used in ritual performance during the funeral.

Cats and Bastet

Both domesticated and wild cats were admired as hunters. Hunting was a metaphor for overcoming dangerous forces. A tawny wildcat helped the sun-god defeat the evil serpent Apep. In Book of the Dead illustrations, a self-satisfied cat slices a snake's body in two with a knife.

The goddess Bastet had a huge temple in the Delta. In her earliest forms, she had a lioness's head like Sekhmet. Later, Bastet appeared as a beautiful woman with the head of a domesticated cat.

Cats symbolized fertility and maternal care. Without veterinary sterilization, a healthy female cat can birth litters every few months. Bronze cat sculptures and cat amulets proliferated as protective and healing symbols.

Thousands of mummified cats and kittens were deposited at temple sites as offerings. Most were killed young (by snapped necks or drowning) to provide bodies for mummification. Some "cat mummies" contained only earth and pebbles. It was the wrapping and priestly blessing that mattered when offering a cat mummy and asking for divine intervention.

Lions and the Sphinx

The lioness was not to be trifled with. Given to fury and foul moods during which she brought disease, Ra's daughter Sekhmet always appeared with the head of a lioness. Offerings of beer helped pacify her, while her priests worked to cure ailments she caused.

The pharaoh could be represented as the sphinx, a lion's body with a human head covered by the royal nemes headdress. The massive sphinx at Giza came to be worshipped as a form of the sun-god.

Two lions back-to-back, with the sun rising between them, represented the god Aker, guardian of the western horizon (entrance to the netherworld). One lion was named "yesterday" and the other "tomorrow."

Later in Egyptian history, a sphinx-shaped god named Tutu ("master of demons") developed a popular following. With knives in his paws and solar rays streaming from his head, Tutu was a manifestation of the creator-god Amun-Ra who could control demons.

Snakes: The Most Magical Animal

Snakes are the animals most frequently encountered in Egyptian magic. With distinctive sideways locomotion, the habit of appearing suddenly and silently, and a potentially lethal bite, the snake epitomized danger.

The Uraeus (Cobra): The hooded cobra was especially significant. It embodied the sun's powerful heat, emitting venomous flames to protect the king. As the uraeus on the pharaoh's forehead, the cobra represented Wadjet, the Delta goddess who protected the ruler.

Many goddesses took snake form:

  • Renenutet: Cobra-bodied goddess of agricultural fertility
  • Meretseger ("She who loves silence"): Cobra goddess worshipped at the Theban necropolis

Crocodiles: Friend and Foe

Moving between water and land, crocodiles occupied a similar borderland to hippopotami. They could be friend or foe.

In tomb paintings showing cattle crossing canals, crocodiles lurk threateningly. A magician makes a protective hand gesture (two fingers pointing out, two folded into the palm) and recites a water spell to render the crocodile blind.

Turn that murderous power to your own ends through magic, and crocodiles became useful. The crocodile god Sobek was widely worshipped, especially in the Fayum. Priests raised crocodiles in temple quarters, and young mummified crocodiles served as sacred offerings.

Ammut ("devourer of the dead") had the head of a crocodile, mane and foreparts of a lion, and rump of a hippopotamus. She waited near the balance scales during judgment of the dead.

Taweret ("the Great One") had a hippopotamus body with wildcat paws and a crocodile tail. She stood upright with nursing breasts, protecting expectant mothers and young children. She carries the sa-hieroglyph of protection before her.

A similar figure with an entire crocodile forming her backbone was identified with the constellation Ursa Major and the goddess Ipet. Through her mighty body, the creator-gods could be reborn as they traveled through the starry night.

Scarabs, Scorpions, and Small Creatures

Scarab beetles were among the most common amulets. The scarab's habit of rolling dung balls to nurture its eggs led to solar associations (the beetle pushing the sun across the sky). Osiris was said to have a scarab under his head, which emerged with wings to fly into the sky, signaling his regeneration.

Scorpions were always bad news. Associated with the goddess Serket, scorpions symbolized pain and suffering. Scorpion-charmers accompanied mining expeditions. The child Horus grasped scorpions in his hands to demonstrate power over them. Mummy case soles depicted scorpions that the deceased would crush underfoot, defeating evil.

Birds and Feathers

Birds seemed like messengers from another world. Their ability to fly, colorful plumage, and calls made them symbols of supernatural presence. Egyptian texts compared feather colors to the sun's rays. Outstretched wings symbolized protection; beating wings captured the sensation of wind, which might signal supernatural presence.

The Ba: The spirit of a dead person was represented as a bird with a human head, able to fly from the tomb by day and return at night.

The Akhu: The most elevated spirit-forms were represented by the crested ibis hieroglyph. They were "the luminous ones," associated with dazzling light.

Sacred Birds:

  • Vulture: Associated with Nekhbet and Mut
  • Ibis: Always identified with Thoth. Hundreds of thousands of mummified ibises were dedicated at temples.
  • Falcon: Ra and Horus. The temple of Horus at Edfu kept a sacred falcon in a cage high on the temple gateway.
  • Red Kite: Isis and Nephthys. The keening cries were likened to the sisters' mourning wails for Osiris.

Many goddesses (Isis, Nephthys, Nut, Maat) had outstretched wings added to their human arms. Wings were also attached to animals that did not have them, particularly the scarab and the ram. Feathers were a magician's best friend.

Demon Days (Epagomenal Days)

The Egyptian year had three seasons of four months each, totaling 360 days. Five extra days were added between the end of Shemu (harvest) and beginning of Akhet (flood).

These were days of celebration but also danger, as demons ran amok. Magicians made special amulets out of linen or papyrus for clients to wear. The magician drew a series of gods in a row while reciting spells, and the client wore the amulet around the neck.

Some amulets have twelve figures (corresponding to twelve demons Sekhmet unleashed), while others have five, representing Osiris, Isis, Seth, Nephthys, and Horus (each said to have been born on one of the five demon days). Risky times brought some hope with them as well.

The Magical Hand Gesture

For protection against crocodiles and water dangers, magicians used a specific hand gesture: two fingers pointing out, two folded into the palm. This gesture, combined with spoken water spells, could render threatening "marsh-dwellers" blind, protecting herdsmen, cattle, and anyone crossing canals.

Love, Sex, and Fertility Magic

Making love and making babies is what keeps the world turning. The ancient Egyptians understood this, and a rich tradition of magic addressed matters of the heart, the body, and the womb.

Love Magic and Desire

Love spells helped those who wanted to attract a partner, keep one faithful, or punish a rival. One spell invokes Re-Horakhty and the Seven Hathors to make a desired woman "come after me like a cow going after grass, like a maidservant chasing after her children, like a herdsman looking after his cattle."

More troubling spells involved creating clay figures of a desired person, piercing them with thirteen copper needles in specific locations (ears, eyes, mouth, brain, hands, feet, genitals, navel, anus), while reciting "I am piercing your eyes, so that she will remember no one but me." The needles fixed the magical wish to the figure's body rather than harming the actual person. This is similar to how knots bind spoken words.

Some love magic was written in cryptic characters to conceal its meaning from casual readers. A spell to make a woman "crazy with desire" called for a shrewmouse (sacred to the sun-god) to be dried, pounded, mixed with blood from the second and little fingers of the left hand, then secretly added to a cup of wine.

Revenge Magic

Revenge spells offered consolation to the spurned. One recipe for making a hated woman's hair fall out: boil the seeds of a blue lotus, steep them in vegetable oil, and find an excuse to rub it into her head.

Sensuality and Cosmic Renewal

The sensual love poetry of ancient Egypt was often set among lush gardens, life-giving marshes, or drunken festivals in honor of Hathor and other goddesses. New Kingdom tomb paintings show lush banquets where musicians and dancing girls perform in an intensely erotic atmosphere with incense, perfume, and flowers. These scenes may represent religious festivals meant to lure Hathor back to Egypt after she had "flounced off in a huff."

Hathor's return was necessary for Ra to regenerate himself overnight. No less than the continued survival of the cosmos was at stake. Celebrating human fertility was a natural counterpart to cosmic renewal.

Contraception

Contraceptive methods combined physical barriers with magical ingredients:

  • Pessary: Mix sour milk with honey and "crocodile dung" (likely Ethiopian soil or similar substance)
  • Herbal brew: Smoke celery with grains of emmer, mix with beer and vegetable oil, boil, and take for four mornings

Pregnancy Tests and Sex Prediction

One magical-medical text instructed a woman to fill bags with emmer wheat and barley, moistening them daily with her urine. If both varieties sprouted, a baby was on the way. If the barley sprouted first, the child would be a boy; if the emmer sprouted first, a girl.

Protection Against Miscarriage

Spells guarded against unexpected or heavy bleeding. One combines incantation with knotted linen cloth:

"Anubis has come forth to keep the flood from trespassing on what is pure: the land of Tait. Beware of what is in it."

The goddess Tait presided over linen, while Anubis was invoked for his association with sacred linen bandages. Heavy bleeding is compared to a Nile flood exceeding its proper bounds, a good and healthy thing turned destructive.

Childbirth

A seated or squatting position was usual for delivery. Women used four sun-baked clay bricks associated with the cardinal directions. These birth bricks were collectively personified as the goddess Meskhenet, whose name literally means "bricks."

Birth temples (mammisi) built in the Late Period provided divine mothers with sacred spaces for labor and delivery. Decoration included Bes with musical instruments and goddesses to assist at delivery.

Birth Tusks

Hippopotamus ivory wands incised with protective spirits and demi-gods protected laboring women and their children. Many have been repaired after breaks or show wear at their pointed ends, suggesting they marked out protective circles for magic rites. Images include:

  • Lions, baboons, lion-hippo hybrids
  • Frogs, turtles, a winged wildcat with falcon's head
  • Aha (a Bes-like figure meaning "the fighter")
  • A similar female figure grasping snakes in either hand

Childbirth Spells

One spell instructs the magician to place a plant-based poultice on the laboring woman's head while reciting:

"I am Horus. I was thirsty, and I came down from the mountain. I found someone calling out, weeping. His wife was nearing her time. I got him to stop weeping. His wife called out for an image of a dwarf, made of clay. Let someone hurry to Hathor, the lady of Dendera, to fetch her healing amulet, so that Hathor will make this woman give birth!"

Bes in Childbirth

Bes was an important aid to laboring women. His leonine face and exposed genitals frightened away evil spirits. He was prone to bursts of unrestrained dancing and musical performances, strumming a stringed instrument, shaking a tambourine, or banging a drum, whether to frighten off spirits, celebrate the occasion, or cover the cries of the woman in labor.

At Deir el-Medina, houses had elevated platforms decorated with images of Bes and young female musicians, possibly spaces for lovemaking and childbirth.

Baby's First Amulet

Newborn infants received protective amulets:

"Are you warm in the nest? Are you hot among the bushes? Is your mother not with you? Is there no sister to offer a breeze? Is there no nurse for protection? Let there be brought to me beads of gold, beads of carnelian, a sealing-bead with a crocodile on it, and a hand-pendant to slay and to dispel the female demon Mereret, to warm the body, to slay the male and female enemies in the West. You will break free!"

The beads were strung on fine linen and applied to the infant's throat. Each bead carried particular magical meaning.

The Peshes-kef and Opening of the Mouth

The midwife's action of clearing mucus from a baby's mouth may have inspired the Opening of the Mouth ritual performed on statues and mummies. The peshes-kef, a flint tool with a split blade, was used by midwives to cut the umbilical cord and later became part of the ceremonial equipment for Opening the Mouth.

Through these ritual gestures and recitations, the private act of giving birth was transformed into the cosmic act of being reborn after death.

Predicting the Future

Given how many things could go wrong in life in ancient Egypt, people looked for ways to predict or influence the future by supernatural means. Egyptian society valued order and predictability, summed up in the concept of maat (cosmic justice), epitomized by the annual return of the Nile flood. But like the level of the flood waters, which priests monitored at measuring points known today as Nilometers, life did not always yield what you had hoped. If magic could give a hint at what was to come, or deliver divine advice, why not accept a helping hand from higher powers?

All magic tried to create an optimal outcome for the user. Predictive magical practices tried to stop a problem from arising in the first place, whether by seeking supernatural advice or identifying the most auspicious time to take action.

Dream Interpretation

The ancient Egyptians saw sleep as a stage of consciousness in which meeting with a ghost or god, usually bearing a positive or helpful message from beyond, was possible. Dreams are always described as acts of sight, as if the sleeping person is having a vision, and symbols in dreams are treated as omens of what might come.

At the village of Deir el-Medina, a scribe named Qenherkhepeshef owned the earliest known manual for the interpretation of dreams, dating to the reign of Ramses II (c. 1280-1210 BCE). The manual lists dreams in neat rows using a formula:

Good omens:

  • If a man sees himself in a dream, drinking wine → Living according to maat
  • If a man sees himself in a dream, seeing a crane → Prosperity (the word for crane, dja, echoed wedja, meaning prosperity)
  • If a man sees himself in a dream, seeing a large tomcat → A large harvest will come (the mythical cat kills the evil serpent Apep)

Bad omens:

  • If a man sees himself in a dream, having sex with his wife during the day → His god will see his crimes (being caught in the act)
  • If a man sees himself in a dream, writing on a papyrus scroll → His god will judge his crimes (evoking Thoth's role at the judgment of the dead)

Protection During Sleep

Magical measures helped sleepers encourage good dreams and guard against nightmares:

  • Four clay cobras: Place cobras with fire in their mouths (possibly oil lamps) in the four corners of the sleeping room
  • Protective headrests: Sleep on a headrest decorated with images of Bes to ensure sweet dreams

Temple Incubation

Incubation refers to sleeping inside the accessible spaces of a temple, or in rooms specially designed for the purpose, hoping that the god or goddess would appear in a dream. In Roman Egypt, large temples had special sanctuaries set aside as sleeping quarters, and priests were on hand the next morning to help interpret what the sleeper had seen.

Calendars of Good and Bad Days

Another way to decide a propitious or inauspicious time for any activity was to consult a calendar of good and bad days. These calendars probably originated in the temple sphere, where priests followed restrictions on certain festival days, but they circulated more widely too.

The calendars used:

  • Black ink: Good days
  • Red ink: Bad days

Ventures warned against on specific days included building a house, setting off on a journey, or having sex. Many calendars prohibited particular foods or drink on certain days. Day three of the first month of Akhet was lucky as long as you didn't eat fish, while the next day was unlucky for anyone with heart trouble, who was sure to drop dead.

The Demon Days

The worst possible time to start any new venture was during the demon days (epagomenal days) that fell at the end of the 360-day solar year and before the start of the flood season, normally in mid- or late July. These five "extra" days, separating one year from the next, were dangerous times requiring special amulets to protect against the violent forces sent out by an angry Sekhmet.

What a relief it must have been when New Year's Day finally dawned. Festivals in every town and temple greeted the start of the new year, when all the demons were banished.

New Year's Flasks (Wep-Renpet Nefer)

Special flasks holding sacred water were distributed at New Year festivals during the Late Period (c. 650-400 BCE). Made of pale-green faience, many are decorated with images of papyrus plants from the Nile marshes and floral wreaths around their necks. Hieroglyphic inscriptions wish the recipient wep-renpet nefer, literally a good, beautiful, or perfect repeating-of-the-year.

Procession Oracles (Peh-Netjer)

Egyptian oracles were very different from Greek oracles. Consulting an oracle was a public affair that took place during festivals, when a processional statue of the local god was carried out of the temple and paraded through the streets, held aloft by priests. The Egyptians called these festivals peh-netjer ("the god's arrival").

How procession oracles worked:

  1. Yes-or-no questions were written on pieces of papyrus or potsherds
  2. These were left in the path of the procession
  3. The god steered its boat towards the correct answer, as divine power magically moved the priests in one direction

The opinion expressed by the god had the weight of a legal decision. Questions included:

  • "Is it So-and-so who has stolen this mat?"
  • "Is this calf good so that I may accept it?"

Records from the reign of Ramses IX (c. 1125 BCE) show that a workman was granted time off to go and file a petition. In the 7th century BCE, fifty priests witnessed an oracle of Amun-Ra at Karnak when one of their members consulted the god for permission to transfer to another priesthood. This was recorded on a papyrus more than 5 metres (16 feet) long, signed by each witness.

Heka: Lord of Oracles

As the divine embodiment of magic, the god Heka was known as the "lord of oracles and lord of revelations, who predicts what will happen." His name could be written so that it looked like the expression for an oracle, peh-netjer.

Child Mediums and Lot Divination

In later periods, priests and magicians developed further methods for soliciting a god's opinion:

Child mediums: Magic manuals instruct the magician to have a young boy stare into an oil lamp (to mesmerize him) or stand facing the rising sun at dawn (with a similarly blinding effect). The magician then interpreted whatever messages the boy delivered.

Lot divination: The client would toss a numbered die or choose numbered tickets, which the magician interpreted. Some numbered systems involved complex calculations, which ensured the result seemed especially mystical. Divination manuals offered encouragement: "Be patient! This omen is hard at first, but in the end, it is good."

Animal omens: Lists of omens offered interpretations of animal behaviors. If a mouse touched a pregnant woman and jumped away, she should beware the risk of miscarriage. Cows, donkeys, horses, scorpions, owls, and ants could all act as omens. But praying to the gods always offered a way to alter your destiny.

The Bes Oracle at Abydos

During the Roman period, an oracle of the dwarf-god Bes at Abydos was believed to speak to visitors. This ancient city was a gateway between this world and the next, sacred to Osiris. The city's desert cemeteries included the tombs of the first kings of Egypt and the vast temple built by King Sety I (c. 1290 BCE).

As late as the 4th century CE, petitioners sent letters to Bes or came in person from all over the eastern Mediterranean to seek guidance. Pilgrims who slept in the temple's incubation chambers might receive a message from Bes in the night.

The oracle was so popular that Emperor Constantius II ordered its closure in 359 CE. But archaeological evidence shows it continued welcoming pilgrims a hundred years later.

The Hour-Priests (Wnwty)

Egyptian priests were keen observers of the night sky, using their knowledge of astronomy to plot out monthly and annual cycles of religious ritual. From as early as the Middle Kingdom, specialized priests observed the movements of heavenly bodies by night, perhaps from the rooftops of temple buildings.

The hour-priests used their observations to:

  • Plot monthly and annual ritual cycles
  • Judge which days would bring good or ill fortune (the origin of calendars of good and bad days)
  • Connect the seventy-day mummification process to the decans (constellations visible for ten nights each)
  • Divide the night into twelve hours

The Decans and Mummification

The rising and setting of the thirty-six decan constellations was linked to mummification, which lasted exactly as long as the seventy days required for the decans to reappear after their trip to the Duat. This connected the transformation of the dead to cosmic cycles of renewal.

Celestial Associations

The moon, individual stars, and constellations were associated with Egyptian deities:

  • Thoth: The ibis-headed moon-god of writing and knowledge
  • Ipet: The hippo-hybrid goddess associated with Ursa Major
  • Sopdu/Sothis: The dog-star Sirius, identified with Isis, the Great Lady of Magic
  • Five visible planets: Most identified with forms of Horus; Mercury sometimes with Seth; Venus ("The Crosser") depicted as a heron

Depicting the stars and planets on tomb ceilings was a display of sacred knowledge, connecting the deceased to the cosmos they were about to join.

The Development of Astrology

The ancient Egyptians had a good grasp of astronomy but did not develop astrology, the practice of reading the position of stars and planets at birth to gauge character and life-course. The twelve signs of the zodiac grew out of observations in ancient Babylonia.

From around 400 BCE onwards, when Egypt became part of the Greek-speaking world, astrology may have found a ready audience given the ancient relationship between the night sky and Egyptian myth. The earliest horoscope found from Egypt dates to 37 BCE, written in Demotic.

Harkhebi, a scorpion-charmer and hour-priest from the Delta city of Buto in the 2nd century BCE, listed among his accomplishments his ability to read the omens of the sun and the dog-star Sirius. It wasn't astrology per se, but Egyptian thought was leaning that way.

By the first or second century CE, a bilingual Greek and Demotic magic handbook included instructions for casting a horoscope, attributed to Imhotep himself, giving astrology an Egyptian lineage.

The Dendera Zodiac

The temple of Hathor at Dendera had an elaborate ceiling showing the sky as a disc, held up by four goddesses and pairs of falcon-headed spirits:

  • Thirty-six decan constellations surround the edge (360 days of the year)
  • Twelve signs of the zodiac appear inside in familiar forms: scorpion for Scorpio, crab for Cancer, lion for Leo, bull for Taurus, balance scales for Libra
  • The composition maps the night sky at a specific point between 15 June and 15 August of 50 BCE
  • A lunar eclipse (shown as an Eye of Horus) from 25 September 52 BCE is recorded
  • A solar eclipse (Isis holding the baboon of Thoth) from 7 March 51 BCE is also shown

The zodiac was sawed and dynamited out of the temple in 1821 by a French engineer and shipped to Paris, where it now resides in the Louvre. A future no Egyptian priest had ever foreseen.

The Legacy of Egyptian Magic

Egyptian magic did not end when temples closed. It transformed, traveled, and left fingerprints across Western esoteric tradition.

The Spread Through Roman Egypt

When Alexander the Great conquered Egypt in 332 BCE, Greek culture merged with Egyptian tradition. Under the Ptolemaic dynasty and later Roman rule, Alexandria became a center of magical learning where Greek, Egyptian, and Jewish traditions combined.

The Greek Magical Papyri (PGM), dating from the 2nd century BCE to the 5th century CE, preserve this fusion. Written in Greek, Demotic, and Coptic, these papyri contain spells invoking Egyptian, Greek, Jewish, and Babylonian divine names together. Isis, Osiris, and Thoth appear alongside Iao (Yahweh), Abrasax, and Greek deities.

Egyptian magic became international. Roman soldiers, merchants, and travelers carried Egyptian amulets and practices across the Mediterranean and into northern Europe.

Magic Gems and Voces Magicae

Small carved gemstones bearing magical images and inscriptions proliferated throughout the Roman world. These magic gems combined Egyptian imagery with words of power (voces magicae), strings of letters and syllables believed to hold magical force.

The most common figure on these gems was Abraxas (also spelled Abrasax), a rooster-headed figure with snake legs, holding a whip and shield. The letters of ABRAXAS add up to 365 in Greek numerology, representing the days of the year and the cosmic archons. The image appeared on protective amulets, healing stones, and talismans for various purposes.

Other common gem images included:

  • Horus-on-the-crocodiles (the Egyptian healing motif)
  • The ouroboros (serpent eating its tail)
  • Anubis and other Egyptian deities
  • Combinations of divine names and magical characters

The word "Abracadabra" likely derives from this tradition, appearing first in a 2nd-century CE medical text as a cure for fever when written in a diminishing triangle.

Hermes Trismegistos and the Hermetic Tradition

The Greeks identified Thoth with their god Hermes. Over time, a legendary figure emerged: Hermes Trismegistos ("Thrice-Great Hermes"), imagined as an ancient Egyptian priest-king who had written vast libraries of magical and philosophical wisdom.

The Hermetic Corpus, written in Greek between the 1st and 3rd centuries CE, claimed to preserve teachings from this primordial sage. These texts blended Egyptian imagery with Greek philosophy, discussing the nature of the cosmos, the divine, and the soul's ascent.

Renaissance scholars believed these texts predated Moses and Plato, giving Egyptian wisdom supreme authority. This "Egyptian" lineage, though largely invented, shaped Western esotericism for centuries.

Alchemy: The Egyptian Art

The word "alchemy" itself derives from khemia, meaning "the Egyptian art" or "the black land" (Egypt's name for itself, referring to the fertile Nile soil). Whether or not Egyptians practiced transmutation of metals, later alchemists claimed Egyptian authority for their work.

Zosimos of Panopolis (3rd-4th century CE), one of the earliest identifiable alchemists, lived in Upper Egypt and described visions filled with Egyptian temple imagery. He attributed alchemical knowledge to figures from Egyptian tradition.

The Kalasiris Tradition

The Kalasiris were Egyptian priests who practiced publicly as magicians during the Roman period. Named for the fringed linen garment they wore, these figures became famous throughout the Roman world as masters of Egyptian magic. Greek and Roman writers described their powers with a mixture of awe and suspicion.

Modern Revivals

Egyptian magic experienced dramatic revival in the 19th and 20th centuries:

The Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn (founded 1888) built elaborate ritual systems incorporating Egyptian deities, particularly Thoth, Isis, and Horus. Their rituals used Egyptian god-forms, hieroglyphic imagery, and adapted Egyptian mythology for initiatory ceremonies.

Egyptology and Magic: As scholars deciphered hieroglyphics and translated Egyptian texts, magical practitioners gained access to authentic ancient sources. Battiscombe Gunn, a British Egyptologist, translated the "Instructions of Ptahhotep" and other wisdom texts that influenced esoteric circles.

Kemetic Reconstruction: Modern practitioners have established temples and traditions attempting to reconstruct authentic Egyptian religious practice, including devotion to specific deities and adaptation of ancient ritual forms.

The legacy of Egyptian magic extends from Roman-era gems to modern tarot decks (where Thoth appears as The Magician) to contemporary practitioners invoking Isis and Horus. The fundamental Egyptian insight, that word, action, and object must unite to create change, continues to inform magical practice worldwide.
