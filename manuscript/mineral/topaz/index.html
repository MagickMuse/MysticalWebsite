Topaz is a silicate mineral crystallizing in the Orthorhombic system with a Mohs hardness of 8. Its name may trace back to Topazos, the ancient Greek name for a small island off the southern coast of Egypt called Zabargad, or it may derive from the Sanskrit word for fire, "tapas." This crystal carries a long lineage of use stretching back to ancient Greece and Rome, where it was prized for protection, clarity, and connection to higher truth.

Lore

The name Topaz may originate from Topazos, the ancient Greek name for the island of Zabargad off Egypt's southern coast. Interestingly, Zabargad never actually produced topaz. The light yellow-green stone the Greeks and Egyptians called topaz was likely peridot or chrysolite. Greek warriors favored the stone for its protective qualities in battle, believing it enhanced physical strength and rendered the wearer invisible to enemies. It was said to conceal one's position and muffle footsteps, making soldiers nearly impossible to track. Greek and Roman soldiers burnished Topaz into their shields and armor to stay hidden from harm. Romans also considered it a truth-telling stone, capable of exposing deception and revealing the truth in obscure situations. It was even rumored to darken in color when brought near poison or falsehood.

Folklore

All transparent golden yellow stones were once grouped under the Greek word chrysolite, meaning golden stone. The finest were said to lie on a place called Serpent Island, whose very existence was a matter of guesswork, and Pliny the Elder named the island Topazos after the Greek topazein, to conjecture, which is also why topaz came to stand for something hard to find. The island is now identified with the place the Crusaders knew as the Isle of St. John and the Egyptians called Zebirget, where the stone was dedicated to the Sun God Ra, its color and sparkle recalling the rays of the Sun. The islanders held an exclusive license to gather the golden stones, which were reputed to be visible only in the dark.

Because topaz cools quickly once lifted from hot water, it was believed to calm frayed tempers and high passions and to bring down fevers. It was touched to the skin of plague victims to treat their ulcers and blisters, and Indian sages likewise noted its coolness. A stranger notion held that the stone gave off a milky fluid able to ward off rabies. Hildegard of Bingen prized topaz as a remedy for failing eyesight, made by steeping the stone in wine for three days and three nights and then applying the liquid to the eye.

The Nine Gems

In a traditional Indian belief, each of the nine planets is paired with its own precious gem, the set known together as the navaratna. Topaz is one of the two gems assigned to Jupiter, paired with yellow sapphire. The full set assigns the pearl to the Moon, the ruby to the Sun, topaz or yellow sapphire to Jupiter, the diamond to Venus, the emerald to Mercury, red coral to Mars, blue sapphire to Saturn, hessonite garnet to Rahu, and cat's eye to Ketu. Rahu and Ketu are the northern and southern lunar points that come into play during eclipses, which is why they are also called the eclipse planets.
