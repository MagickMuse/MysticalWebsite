Malachite is a copper carbonate hydroxide mineral with a monoclinic crystal structure, registering 3.5 to 4 on the Mohs scale. Its hallmark lies in its unique markings, a marbled swirl of concentric rings and round eyes caused by copper ore.

Magickal Powers

Lifts spirits when totally discouraged

Appearance

Concentric light and dark bands and rosettes. All sizes, often tumbled or polished. Some see shifting desert winds and landscapes in its deep green patterns, while others see the planets of the solar system in their cosmic dance.

Origin

Found in Australia, Bolivia, Canada, Congo, Mexico, Namibia, Romania, Russia, United States, and Zambia. Malachite has been mined in the area around Mount Sinai since as early as 4000 BCE. Its name comes from the Greek "malakos," meaning soft, a reference to its reputation as an ideal carving material.

It is toxic and should be used only in its polished form. Avoid breathing its dust. If used as a gem elixir, apply externally only or make by the indirect method of placing the stone in a glass container and standing this within spring water so that the stone does not touch the water.

Malachite amplifies both positive and negative energies. It grounds spiritual energies onto the planet. It is believed by some people that Malachite is still evolving and will be one of the most important healing stones of the new millennium.

Malachite is already an important protection stone. It absorbs negative energies and pollutants easily, picking them up from the atmosphere and from the body. It should be cleansed before and after use by placing it on a quartz cluster in the sun (do not use salt, which will damage the surface).

Lore

Malachite has been mined near Mount Sinai since approximately 4000 BCE. King Solomon's mines along the Red Sea yielded copper and this powerful healing ore. In ancient Greek mythology, some link malachite to the peacock of Hera, wife of Zeus, each circle representing one of the eyes of Argus, protecting her from afar. Many Egyptian tomb paintings contain malachite powder ground into the paint, and Cleopatra's famous thick green line of malachite eyeliner was thought to ensure her health, longevity, and safety. The use of powdered malachite is discouraged today, as it has toxic properties if inhaled or ingested.

Protection

Don't let the marbled green appearance fool you—malachite is a powerful protector but requires careful handling. Its copper content makes it potentially harmful if misused. Never grind, carve, sand, or manipulate the surface in any way, and absolutely don't breathe in the dust. Don't leave malachite in direct skin contact for extended periods; jewelry should be worn in short bursts, not as an everyday piece unless set in a ring or necklace that prevents direct skin contact.

Despite these cautions, malachite is exceptional at pulling energetic toxins from the air and your body—negativity doesn't stand a chance with this stone in your corner. It absorbs harmful energies and pollutants from both the atmosphere and the physical body, making it valuable for protection work when handled properly.

Amulets and Folk Protection

Malachite carries ripples of blue-green markings, and its copper content gives the stone its color. It was drawn from King Solomon's copper mines near Eilat and prized by the ancient Greeks and Romans, who shaped it into amulets and talismans.

Stones whose bands resemble the feathers of a peacock's tail, complete with the eyes, are known as peacock malachite, sacred to the goddess Juno through her tie to that bird. Such pieces were made into amulets believed to guard against the evil eye, since the eye pattern was worked into their design, and the charm gained further power when carved into the shape of a triangle. Malachite amulets were also given to children to ease teething and to shield them from witchcraft. Because the stone was said to encourage a good night's sleep, it was fastened to the cradles and beds of babies and infants. As a healing stone it was thought to counter feelings of faintness, and when powdered and taken in milk it was believed to ease the pain of a heart attack.
