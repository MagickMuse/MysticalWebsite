Turquoise is one of the oldest known healing and protective stones, crystallizing in the Triclinic system with a Mohs hardness of 5 to 6. It is the traditional and primary birthstone for December and carries an impressive magickal legacy spanning countless cultures. The finest-quality Turquoise has always come from Persia (modern-day Iran), where it was revered as a source of good fortune. Native Americans have cherished it for centuries as a potent purifier and healer, as well as a stone that strengthens and protects warriors in battle. It has been used for amulets since time immemorial and is believed to change color to warn of danger or infidelity. The rarest Turquoise in the United States comes from the Kingman mine in Arizona, known as Sleeping Beauty, the last fully functional commercial turquoise mine in the country. No matter where it is found, Turquoise is prized for its qualities of healing, beauty, and harmony.

Appearance

Turquoise presents as opaque, often veined specimens in shades of blue and green, found in all sizes and frequently polished. Its characteristic matrix patterns of brown or black veining make each piece unique.

Magickal Lore

⚠️ The following draws on closed Indigenous traditions of the Americas. These practices are sacred to Native American peoples and are meant only for those within the originating cultures.

Turquoise is sacred to many American Indian tribes. The Navajo used ground turquoise and coral in sand paintings to bring rain to parched land. Other Southwestern peoples placed turquoise in tombs to guard the dead. The Pueblos laid turquoise under floors as offerings to deities when building houses or kivas. A piece of turquoise was nearly required in an Apache shaman's medicine bag. Other peoples attached turquoise to bows to ensure accurate shots.

Name and Folklore

The name turquoise points to its trade route rather than its source. The finest stones once traveled out of Persia by way of Turkey, which is how they earned the name Turkish stone, since Persia sat within the Ottoman Empire at the time. Persia, now Iran, has always produced the highest quality material.

The stone was long read as a gauge of its owner's wellbeing. It was believed to lose its color and turn pale when its keeper fell ill or died, then slowly recover its former beauty once it passed to a new owner. Because turquoise responds to body heat and to the chemicals in perfumes and sprays, this shifting color was understood as a mark of its sensitivity to the one who wore it.

The stone was also held to make friends of enemies and to stand for generosity and affection. It was thought to confer its luck only when given as a gift, never when bought.

⚠️ The following draws on closed Indigenous traditions of the Americas and is sacred to those peoples. It is offered as cultural history and is meant only for those within the originating cultures. Among the Aztecs turquoise was held so sacred that no single person could own one, for all of it belonged to the Gods alone, and it was used to adorn ceremonial death masks. After the fall of the Aztec and Mayan worlds, reverence for this stone of the Gods carried on among the Pueblo and other Native American peoples, where it remained chiefly the province of the medicine man and the shaman who stood closest to the Gods.

Care

Turquoise sits at Mohs 5 to 6, making it a relatively soft stone that requires gentle handling. It is not sun safe and not water safe; prolonged exposure to sunlight can cause fading, and water can damage its surface and alter its color. Cleanse with smoke, moonlight, sound, or by placing on a selenite plate. Avoid chemical cleaners and perfumes. Store separately from harder stones to prevent scratching. Turquoise makes an excellent elixir when prepared using the indirect method only.
