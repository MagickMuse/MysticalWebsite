History and Lore

The Greek word for diamond is adamas, meaning both untamable and hard, and it gives us the word adamant, also used to describe the brilliance of the stone. The diamond is the hardest of all minerals and serves as the measure for the toughness of every other stone. It was one of the twelve stones set into the breastplate of the High Priest of the Temple in Jerusalem, where it was said to shine brilliantly in the presence of truth and innocence.

Love and Commitment

Its toughness, beauty, and rarity made the diamond one of the most costly of gems and the traditional stone of betrothal. The idea that a suitor must spend a month's salary on the ring is a modern invention of a leading diamond house rather than an old tradition. Although the diamond became a symbol of love and commitment, its earliest use in rings was as a protective talisman, and knights and warriors set diamonds into their sword hilts and shields for that reason. These early stones were worn rough, as they came from the ground, without the faceting that creates their familiar sparkle.

Scribbling Rings

Because the diamond is hard enough to score a mark on glass, scribbling rings became fashionable during the reign of Queen Elizabeth I. A diamond was set with its sharp point facing upward so the wearer could scratch messages onto windows, usually short love letters or secret notes.

Colors and Lightning

Diamonds occur in subtle shades of yellow, blue, pink, white, green, orange, and even black. A rare diamond holding a red flash within it was regarded in India as a symbol of disaster that foretold a swift death for its wearer. The fire that flashes inside a well cut and polished diamond linked the stone to thunder and lightning, and in India it is named Vajra, meaning lightning.

Cursed Diamonds

Diamonds were believed to absorb both good and bad energy, so those who used them for healing were warned not to upset the delicate balance of the stone. In Persia they were viewed with suspicion for this reason. Some famous diamonds, long the objects of envy and greed, carry curses and a reputation for bringing misfortune to their owners.

The Hope Diamond left a trail of ruined lives behind it. Its bad fortune is said to have begun when it was stolen from the forehead of a statue of the Goddess Sita in India, after which the Goddess was believed to have cursed anyone who touched it, and the original thief was said to have been torn apart by wild dogs. The stone later passed through the hands of Marie Antoinette and Louis XVI, both of whom were beheaded. A wealthy banker named Philip Hope bought it, and his nephew Thomas, who inherited it, soon lost his entire fortune. It was next acquired by Abdul Hamid, a Sultan of Turkey, who lost his title and was forced to sell it, and then by the wife of an American newspaper magnate, who lost both her fortune and her only son.

The Koh-i-Noor diamond, whose name means mountain of light, once belonged to Shah Jahan, who built the Taj Mahal for his wife Mumtaz. Whoever owned the diamond was believed destined to rule the world. By the time it was presented to Queen Victoria in 1850 it had gained a reputation for bringing ill luck to any male owner, so Victoria decreed that only women should wear it, and since then it has been set only into the crowns of female royalty.

Spellworking

Legend has it that Europeans first "discovered" African diamonds in a shaman's leather pouch. Anciently, diamonds were worn as polished stones, treasured for their beauty. It wasn't until later that faceting created their dazzling prismatic fire.

Spirituality

Worn, diamond promotes spirituality, even ecstasy—the shaman's ritual state of consciousness. It is often utilized in meditation and spiritual pursuits.

Sexual Healing

Carried or worn, diamond promotes self-confidence in relations with the opposite sex. It is said to be potent for relieving or eliminating root causes of sexual dysfunction, removing cultural blockages. A cleansing, purifying, and releasing stone in matters of sexuality. In India, women wear an unblemished white diamond with a slightly black hue to ensure male children and to conquer infertility.

Fidelity and Reconciliation

Though not a stone of love, diamond is worn to ensure fidelity and to reconcile quarreling lovers. Today it is the most popular wedding ring stone.

Strength and Courage

Because of its hardness and associations with the Sun, diamond is worn or utilized in spells to increase physical strength. In ancient Rome, it was set in steel rings and worn touching the skin to produce bravery, daring, and victory. In ancient India, a diamond set in platinum or silver was worn for victory in battles, also fastened to the left arm.

Protection

Owing to its flashing nature, diamond has long been regarded as a stone of protection. For best results and to ensure luck, the diamond should be faceted into a six-sided cut.

Peace and Sleep

Surprisingly, diamond is a stone of peace when worn. It relieves nightmares and encourages sleep at bedtime.

Scrying

Try scrying with a faceted diamond in soft candlelight, dazzling yourself in its inner world of color and light.

Dark Magick Applications

Diamond can be employed to inflict blindness, whether physical or spiritual, along with confusion and disorientation. It is often given as a gift by those seeking to conceal their true motives and questionable dealings.

The Nine Gems

In a traditional Indian belief, each of the nine planets is paired with its own precious gem, the set known together as the navaratna. The diamond is the gem of Venus. The full set assigns the pearl to the Moon, the ruby to the Sun, topaz or yellow sapphire to Jupiter, the diamond to Venus, the emerald to Mercury, red coral to Mars, blue sapphire to Saturn, hessonite garnet to Rahu, and cat's eye to Ketu. Rahu and Ketu are the northern and southern lunar points that come into play during eclipses, which is why they are also called the eclipse planets.
