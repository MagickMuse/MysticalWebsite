Metals in Spell-casting

Magick in its contemporary form owes its very existence to the discovery of metal and the art of the forge, yet metal remains one of the most overlooked materials in modern practice. This may reflect lingering ancient taboos surrounding both the substance itself and those who worked it.

Like stones and botanicals, each metal broadcasts a distinct energetic signature. Because fewer metals exist compared to the vast diversity of crystals or plants, their core associations are more straightforward to learn.

Of all these, the most magickally potent metal, and arguably one of the most charged substances on the planet, is iron.

Iron: The Metal of Heaven

No metal carries a deeper connection to magick across world cultures than iron. In its pure state, iron does not occur naturally on Earth except in the form of meteorites, which is why ancient peoples gave it the name Metal of Heaven, viewing it as a divine offering from the sky.

Meteorites were carved into images of deities. The original cult statue within the Temple of Artemis at Ephesus, counted among the Seven Wonders of the ancient world, was fashioned from a fallen meteorite. The holiest representation of the goddess Kybele was an unworked meteor, believed by many scholars to still lie buried beneath the foundations of Saint Peter's Basilica, which was constructed over her Roman temple.

Iron's Magickal Properties

Iron is associated with strength, protection, and truth. In magickal terms, it is said to bring back health, strengthen vitality on both physical and spiritual levels, and address impotence. Harmful spirits are universally said to fear iron: it drives them away and keeps them at bay. An iron box guards magickal tools from unwanted energies, much as a lead blanket protects someone during radiation exposure. In parts of Africa, and possibly still in some regions today, people would swear oaths upon iron the way others swear on holy books. Among the most accessible protection spells: tuck an iron blade or implement under your pillow. It is not there as a weapon but as a ward against spiritual intrusion while you rest.

Iron and Menstrual Blood

Apart from menstrual blood, nothing in the natural world shares a stronger bond with magick than iron. These two forces are fundamentally connected. Other metals and stones are sometimes described as the Earth's skeletal structure, but iron ore occupies a different category entirely: it has long been viewed as the Earth's own menstrual blood.

This connection makes iron both immensely powerful and volatile. It serves as a tool for healing in magickal ritual and in practical fields such as surgery, dentistry, and acupuncture. Yet it is also an instrument of harm: blades and firearms wound and kill. Iron occupies a space of duality, holding the capacity for both restoration and destruction.

The Smith: Earth's First Professional Magickian

The art of the forge, humanity's first true alchemy, was shrouded in secrecy for hundreds of years. Those who possessed its knowledge could craft weapons capable of dominating their neighbours entirely. Through constant contact with the most magickally charged substance known, and because they alone held its secrets, smiths became far more than skilled craftspeople. They were the world's earliest magickians, known as Masters of Fire, and they became the first professional spell-casters, hired to perform workings on behalf of others.

Power and Persecution

Smithcraft created a paradox. Because the smith maintained intimate contact with the most charged substance in existence and shaped it according to his intent, he was seen as possessing more magickal force than ordinary people. Iron steadily renewed and fortified his personal energy.

However, because iron ore could be interpreted as the Earth's menstrual blood, a substance typically surrounded by restriction and taboo, the ironworker who worked with it in plain sight and without pause risked being seen as contaminated. On one hand, the smith was strong enough to shatter those prohibitions and still flourish. On the other, this contact made more conservative elements of society deeply uncomfortable. Whatever spiritual cleansing or protective practices the smith required for his own wellbeing were unknown to outsiders, and therefore suspect.

The Accelerator of Time

Another perspective comes from Central Asia's Turkic tribes, who believed raw metals originated from the Earth's bowels. These raw materials were waste products that would eventually mature into finished metal on their own if left buried long enough. The smith's furnace, in this view, served as a substitute for the Earth's womb, giving birth to metal artificially. The Master of Fire was therefore believed to exert power over Time itself by accelerating this natural process. This granted him abilities in healing and prophecy, but it also placed him perpetually on the edge of spiritual catastrophe: digging ore from the Earth, rooting through her body, was considered a violation. Only the ironworker knew whether and how he could be spiritually cleansed.

The Smith's Many Roles

Beyond metalwork, the ironworker typically served as healer, herbalist, dentist, surgeon, body artist, and often the sole person permitted to perform circumcision, making him responsible for spiritual initiation. He carved amulets, designed rituals, and consulted with spirits on behalf of others: part artisan, part shaman. The secrets of the forge were fiercely guarded, and smiths organised into closed lineages, passing methods down through bloodlines alone.

With the smith came an idea that would reshape magick forever: that one could hire another person to perform a working on your behalf. From this, an entirely new social role emerged: the professional spell-caster.

The smith's wife, the woman who shared her life with the Master of Fire, bore his children, and often served as his professional partner, became a magickally significant person through her own authority. Her responsibilities commonly included herbal medicine, midwifery, women's health, body art with henna (henna being another substance linked to menstrual blood), and above all, divination. In some traditional African communities, a wedding might be delayed until the smith's wife could arrive to dress the bride's hair, apply henna, or otherwise attend and bless her.

Outsiders by Nature

Societal ambivalence toward the smith cannot be overstated, and it would eventually colour attitudes toward magick itself. The history of ironworking runs parallel to the history of magickal practice. Although the smith was essential to his community, he and his family remained apart and distinct. People needed their services but were afraid to draw too close or allow them to live as fully integrated members of society. Their very skill set them apart.

Scripture connects metalworkers with the Kenites, a lineage traced back to Cain, standing apart from the Twelve Tribes of Israel. The best-known Kenite is Jael, who despite carrying no conventional weapon, slew the retreating general Sisera using a hammer, the quintessential tool of the forge.

Smithcraft clans became nomadic, moving from settlement to settlement as long as work lasted. This pattern persists in parts of Africa and Western Asia, where some Bedouin groups still await the smith's arrival to perform circumcisions.

Complicating matters further, those who served the dominant population as smiths often belonged to marginalised communities. This created a deeply layered dynamic: the metalworker carried out sacred duties and held spiritual knowledge belonging to traditions that were not necessarily his own.

In Europe, smithcraft is tied to Tinkers, Travellers, and most prominently the Romany, who accompanied Tatar and Mongol armies as metalworkers. Romany culture as first encountered by Europeans followed the classic ironworking clan pattern: men worked the forge while women practised divination. In rural Africa and Western Asia, smiths were often of Jewish origin, though their personal practices may have strayed far from mainstream Judaism. The Ethiopian Jewish community known as Beta Israel faced persecution rooted in both their faith and their hereditary role as ironworkers. The Ineden, smiths connected to the Tuareg people, are also believed to have Jewish roots. They serve the majority group across a sweeping range of roles: forging metal, crafting jewellery, making weapons, carving wood, treating illness, preparing herbs, composing poetry, performing music, and advising on spiritual matters. Essential to traditional Tuareg culture, they are simultaneously assigned low status.

Christianity and the Smith

With the rise of Christianity in Europe, ironworkers were demonised and identified with the devil, who was frequently depicted in the form of a smith. Some of their traditional spiritual authority endured: in the original Gretna Green weddings, a couple could cross the border from England and be legally married by the smith.

Saint Patrick's Breastplate, a well-known Irish prayer attributed to the saint, calls upon God for protection against "incantations of false prophets, against the black laws of paganism, against spells of women, smiths and druids, against all knowledge that is forbidden the human soul."

Working with Metal in Spell-casting

Because modern metals are rarely extracted from the Earth using traditional propitiating rituals and respectful ceremony, any new metal you bring into your practice should be thoroughly cleansed and charged before use. That said, both silver and iron are considered incorruptible: although they can be used for harmful purposes, negative vibrations should not cling to them the way they might to other metals or materials.

Metal is woven into all manner of workings, from the simplest to the most elaborate. One of metal's greatest strengths is that it steadily, constantly, and consistently radiates its power. This means the spell continues working even after your direct participation has ended. Magick rings, for example, need only be crafted, charged, and consecrated once. After that, they broadcast their own energy, drawing toward you whatever they have been programmed to attract, even as your attention moves elsewhere.

Ordinary household and farm tools also carry magickal significance. What we now consider mundane was once recognised as exceptionally charged. The magick remains; it is only human perception that has shifted. Hammers, knives, and other metal implements are shared by many trades, but the anvil belongs to the smith alone and is therefore saturated with power and sacredness.

An anvil can be converted into an altar and used as such even by someone who is not a smith.
