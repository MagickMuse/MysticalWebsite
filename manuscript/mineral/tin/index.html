Magical Lore

An old Cornish spell claims that to turn tin into silver, all the magician need do is place it in a tank of ants on a certain night of the Moon's cycle. Typically, the spell neglects to specify which night of the lunar cycle is required.

Divination

On New Year's Eve—a prime night for foretelling future trends—melt a small quantity of tin in an iron cup over a flame (a gas jet will work). Once the metal is melted, throw it into a bucket of icy water. Examine the metal's shape and the folds or patterns present on it, then divine the future from the nodule. This divination method is similar to that used with lead.

Luck

Tin is carried as a good-luck piece. Its Jupiter correspondence makes it naturally expansive and fortune-bringing.

Money

Tin may be shaped into money-attracting talismans, such as minutely carved tiny images of dollar bills or other currency symbols. Its connection to Jupiter makes it particularly suited for prosperity and abundance magic.

The Seven Magickal Metals

Tin is the metal of Jupiter, one of the seven magickal metals that the ancients paired with the first seven planets. The full set links silver to the Moon, mercury to Mercury, copper to Venus, gold to the Sun, iron to Mars, tin to Jupiter, and lead to Saturn. In the Dharmic religions, sacred artifacts such as singing bowls and temple statuary are made from a blend of all seven metals.

History and Lore

In alchemy, tin and the planet Jupiter are marked by the same symbol. Tin does not corrode, and it is classically combined with copper to make the alloy bronze, a use that reaches back to at least 3500 BC. The earliest tin mines were those of Cornwall and Devon in England, and the British Isles were so renowned for their tin that the Greeks called them the Kassiterides, the tin-producing lands. Tin and its mines came to be linked with the fairy folk, or little people, said to dwell especially in the underground tunnels that held the metal.
