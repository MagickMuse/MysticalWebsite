Magical Lore

Because iron is seldom found in pure form except in meteorites, the earliest iron available for use by humans was obtained from these strange celestial objects. Meteorites, observed falling from the heavens, were used to make simple tools, supplementing bone and stone implements.

Throughout most of the world, humans eventually learned how to remove iron from its ore, making it available for wider use. Once this occurred, it was soon limited to purely physical applications and was restricted in magic and religion. In ancient Greece, no iron was brought into the temples. Roman priests could not be shaved or scraped with iron during bodily cleansing.

Ireland, Scotland, Finland, China, Korea, India, and other countries have severe taboos against iron. Again and again in ancient rituals, fire was made without iron, altars built without its use, and magical rituals performed only after divesting the body of all traces of the metal. Herbs were usually collected with noniron knives, owing to the belief that the vibrations of this metal would "jam" or "confuse" the herb's energies.

However, iron did have its place in magic—specifically, it was worn or used in protective rituals. Its powerful, projective vibrations were thought to be feared by demons, ghosts, fairies, genii, and other fantastic creatures.

Protection

For heavy protection, place small pieces of iron in each room of the house or bury at the four corners of your property. In earlier times, iron fences were sometimes used to halt the flow of negativity into the home.

To guard specifically against Lilith, the spirit blamed for endangering childbirth, keep an iron knife, whether full-size or a small amulet, under one's pillow throughout pregnancy and during labor.

During protective or defensive magic, wear an iron ring engraved with the symbol of Mars (♂). Or, obtain a three-inch thick white candle and eight old iron nails. Warm the nails by a fire (or in a red candle's flame), then thrust each into the white candle in a random pattern. Light the nail-studded candle and visualize yourself as guarded, protected, secure.

Classical Romans drove nails into their house walls to preserve their health, especially during times of plague. Because of its protective effects, iron was sometimes thought to be sacred, and thieves in ancient Ireland wouldn't dare to steal it.

Strength

Wearing iron or carrying a small piece of this metal enhances physical strength and is an excellent talisman for athletes.

Healing

Iron is used during healing rituals. A small piece is placed beneath the pillow at night—this was originally done to scare away the "demons" that had caused the disease but can be thought of as strengthening the body's ability to heal itself.

Iron rings or bracelets are worn to draw out illnesses from the body, dating back to at least ancient Roman times. A curious ritual from Germany to cure toothache: pour oil onto a piece of heated iron; the fumes which rise from the iron will act on the problem.

In old Scotland, healing stones—quartz crystals or holey stones—were kept in iron boxes to guard against supernatural creatures who might steal them.

Grounding

Iron is worn for grounding, for closing down the psychic centers, and for impeding the flow of energy from the body. This isn't the best during magical ritual but is fine when the subject is under psychic or emotional attack, is physically depleted, or wishes to focus on physical matters.

Horseshoes and Luck

Iron horseshoes and the nails that attach them to hooves are ancient magical tools. They might have first been used in ancient Greece, where they were called seluna and were associated with the Moon and the goddess Selene.

A horseshoe hung in the home over the front door confers protection. While theories differ as to the "proper" way to hang the horseshoe, placing it points up is traditional. Ideally, it is to be nailed with three of its original nails.

An old iron horseshoe nail is sometimes bent into a ring and worn for luck and healing.

Return of Stolen Goods

If you have had something stolen from you and have a fireplace handy, try this: Take a horseshoe nail that you've found by chance. Drive this into the fireplace, visualizing the stolen object returning to your home.

Weather Magic

In China, dragons were thought to fear iron. When rain was needed, pieces of the metal were thrown into "dragon pools" to upset the creatures and send them into the sky in the form of rain clouds.

Death Rites

In old Scotland, iron was used to avert danger when a death had occurred in the house. Iron nails or knitting needles were thrust into every item of food, cheese, grain, meat and so on, to act as a lightning rod, attracting the confusing vibrations that death may arouse within the living and thus sparing the food of possible contamination.

Iron as the Blood of the Earth

Iron stands among the primary constituents of blood, and because the metal and the liquid carry a similar scent, iron came to be seen as the blood or life force of the Earth itself. The rust red of weathering iron deepened this sense of living vitality. By the same reasoning, whatever grows from the soil holds a share of that spirit, corn most of all given its value as a crop. To settle the spirit, people would take a bite at a piece of iron and so render it harmless.

⚠️ For Aboriginal Australians, iron is a sacred and magickal material used in rites and ceremonies alongside blood, with the two treated as interchangeable. This is a closed practice sacred to Aboriginal Australian peoples and is meant only for those within the originating culture.

Sky Iron

Since pure iron was scarce apart from what fell as meteorites, early peoples prized the metal that dropped from the heavens. In Tibet this sky iron is worked into singing bowls whose vibrational tone is believed to draw good spirits and to heal both body and soul.

Repelling the Uncanny

Iron held a reputation for turning away witches, ghosts, and other malevolent beings, and folktales tell of supernatural creatures left powerless the moment they were struck with it. In the shape of the horseshoe its protective force grows stronger still, for the form recalls the protective Goddess, the crescent moon, the chalice, and the yoni.

Mars and Blacksmithing

Iron answers to the planet Mars, both for its reddish cast and for its strength and hardness and the fire required to reshape it. The craft of blacksmithing, the working of iron, was said to have been granted by the Gods themselves.

The Seven Magickal Metals

Iron is the metal of Mars, one of the seven magickal metals that the ancients paired with the first seven planets. The full set links silver to the Moon, mercury to Mercury, copper to Venus, gold to the Sun, iron to Mars, tin to Jupiter, and lead to Saturn. In the Dharmic religions, sacred artifacts such as singing bowls and temple statuary are made from a blend of all seven metals.

Iron Against Evil

Iron repels evil of every kind, and most malevolent spirits cannot bear to be near it. It guards around the clock, whether you are awake or asleep. Beyond protection, iron enhances and stimulates the growth of your own magickal power, making you stronger and better able to protect yourself so that less outside spiritual protection is needed. It also invigorates general vitality and energy. Its power is so strong that even shouting the word "iron" is said to rout evil spirits.
