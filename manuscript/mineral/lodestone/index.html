A Bridge Between Worlds

Lodestone occupies a singular position in the mineral kingdom. As magnetic iron ore, it straddles the boundary between the realm of stone and the realm of metal, drawing on the magickal properties of both. Few substances on Earth carry as much raw magickal force. Where other minerals radiate their energy passively, lodestone actively pulls, drawing money, love, success, healing, and good fortune toward whoever works with it.

Gender and Pairing

Lodestones can be worked alone or in matched pairs, and practitioners in many traditions assign gender to individual specimens. Rounded lodestones are considered feminine, while elongated or phallic-shaped specimens are classified as masculine. Determining a lodestone's gender is considerably simpler than sexing most animals. The distinction matters when a working calls for a specific polarity or when a pair is needed to represent two complementary forces.

Historical Lore

Wherever human cultures have encountered lodestones, they have recognized their power. Alexander the Great is said to have issued lodestones to his soldiers as protection against djinn. In Chinese magickal tradition, lodestone rings were exchanged at weddings to bind the couple's fortune together. Ancient Roman temple artisans carved statues of Venus from lodestone and Mars from iron, then positioned them so that the magnetic pull between the two figures physically demonstrated the force of attraction between the divine lovers.

The scientific name magnetite traces back to the ancient city of Magnesia, where lodestones were first mined in quantity. According to one Christian tradition, the slab that cradled Christ's body during the three days between crucifixion and resurrection was lodestone. When Godfrey of Bouillon led the First Crusade to Jerusalem and prayed in the Holy Sepulcher, a voice reportedly told him that victory was certain if he carried a fragment of the stone away with him. He obeyed and prevailed. The kings who followed him ignored the stone, and the Holy Land slipped from their grasp.

A Benevolent Force

Lodestone is exclusively a force for attraction and benefit. Like the root charm High John the Conqueror, it has no role in hexing or harmful workings. The only real way to misuse a lodestone is to neglect it entirely and leave its power untapped.

Childbirth

Lodestone's very presence is said to encourage delivery. Exploit its drawing, magnetic properties by gently massaging the laboring mother's abdomen or aura, demonstrating the suggested route of departure to the baby.

The Living Stone

Beyond the memory that all minerals possess, lodestones are credited with consciousness, intelligence, and a soul. They respond to care, reward attention, and are considered more actively alive than any other stone in the mineral kingdom.

This perception carries a practical consequence: lodestones must be fed regularly to maintain their strength. Their preferred nourishment is fine iron shot, also called iron filings, iron dust, or in Hoodoo tradition, magnetic sand. You cannot simply share your own meal with a lodestone the way you might toss a treat to a dog. They require their own specific sustenance. Magnetic sand itself carries its own drawing power and appears as an ingredient in many spells, both alongside and independent of lodestones.

⚠️ The practice of feeding lodestones magnetic sand belongs to Hoodoo, a closed African American tradition that should be practiced only by those raised within it.

The Compass and the Lore of Attraction

Lodestone is a type of magnetite, though the two have slightly different crystalline structures. Magnetite is fairly common while true lodestone is rare, and a piece must be electrically charged, by a lightning strike for example, before it gains the magnetic pull that made it so important a mineral. The compass owes its existence to lodestone, and had that discovery never been made the course of world history would have unfolded very differently.

The Chinese were the first to recognize what the stone could do, since it aligns itself with the Earth's magnetic field, and they shaped it into the world's first compass. References from about AD 100 onward describe a south pointer, most likely a lodestone spoon whose curved base let it spin freely within a hollowed dish.

The stone was also known to the ancient Greeks. Pliny the Elder told of a herdsman named Magnes whose iron hobnailed boots and iron staff tip were drawn to pieces of lodestone. Many ancient peoples believed stones held souls, and a stone that could pull objects toward it and set them moving seemed to possess a mind of its own. For that reason it was given water to drink and iron filings to eat. The ancient Egyptians called it Haroeri, meaning Grandson of the Earth Goddess.

Although lightning gave the lodestone its power, it also drew lightning down, so it was not to be carried or worn during a storm. Because it pulls things toward itself, the stone was linked with both attraction and retention. Shopkeepers believed it encouraged customers, and sex workers used it to draw clients, and it was also held as a symbol of love and the attraction of opposites.

The temple of Konark, on the coast of the Bay of Bengal in India, is said to have carried such a huge and powerful lodestone atop its Sun Temple that it wrecked passing ships, both drawing the vessels in and throwing off their compasses. Rumor holds that the stone was carried away by voyagers, after which the temple eventually collapsed.

Magickal Lore

The lodestone is considered a living stone in contemporary folk magick. On Fridays, it is placed in a small bowl of water to "drink," then laid in full sunlight to dry. When dry, iron filings are sprinkled on it as "food." Some keep the stone in a red bag and sprinkle water and iron filings on it weekly.

The stone was associated with Hercules and became a symbol of strength and invulnerability.

A few centuries ago, it was believed dangerous to carry lodestone during thunderstorms as it attracted lightning. A knife rubbed with the stone was said to inflict fatal wounds. Some believed a diamond or garlic could strip the stone of its powers, though Giambattista della Porta disproved this in his 1558 work Natural Magic. To restore powers, the stone was anointed with linseed oil, placed in a goatskin bag, and covered with dirt.

Power and Magickal Amplification

The lodestone is a power stone used to strengthen spells. It is added to sachets or herbal amulets, placed on the altar, or worn to increase the magician's ability to rouse and release energy. In medieval ceremonial magick, lodestone was engraved with an armored man figure to empower rituals. The larger the stone, the more inherent power within it, since greater size means stronger magnetic force.

Attraction Magick

Lodestone's basic use is attraction. Because it is a natural magnet, it draws objects or energies to its user and can be used in any type of spell. A lodestone set in a man's belt buckle draws success in all undertakings. Its placement near the "third chakra" (about two inches below the navel) stimulates personal power and the will.

Healing Magick

Lodestone draws out disease and pain from the body. Healers use it as a focusing device for their energies, passing it over or placing it directly on afflicted areas, particularly pain in the hands and feet. It is also carried, often anointed with healing oils such as sandalwood. The stone is said to be effective for rheumatism, headaches, and healing wounds. Placed in a black bag suspended on a black ribbon around the neck, it was historically used for gout.

A small lodestone set in silver sharpens the sight. Set in gold, it strengthens the heart.

Healing Spell: Hold the lodestone in your hands, then shake it vigorously while visualizing your illness draining from you and going into the stone. Bury the stone in the earth for a week following the ritual. Any lodestone used in healing rituals to absorb disease should be cleansed after each use.

The stone is worn to smooth over troubles in a relationship, especially arguing. Its basic function is to cool tempers to allow true communication. A coral necklace with a lodestone suspended from it was once worn to facilitate easy childbirth. Women wear lodestones to encourage wandering partners to return home.

Male Sexual Dysfunction

In ancient Assyria, lodestone was used in a sexual rite of sympathetic magick. The man placed lodestone in oil and rubbed the resulting "infusion" on his body and penis to ensure satisfactory intercourse. The woman rubbed parzilli (iron powder) on her body to enhance her attractiveness. In sixteenth-century India, a king ordered his cooking utensils fashioned of lodestone to ensure his continuing virility.

To use: Hold the stone in your receptive hand, visualizing satisfactory, complete, joyous sexual relations. Carry the stone or place it beneath the mattress to release its powers.

Care

With a Mohs hardness of 5.5 to 6.5, lodestone is moderately durable and both water and sun safe. Cleanse with running water, moonlight, or sound. Traditionally, lodestones are "fed" by sprinkling iron filings on them and given water to "drink" on Fridays, then dried in sunlight. Any lodestone used in healing work to absorb negativity should be cleansed in salt water after each use.
