How to Recognize Fake Crystals

Fake stones are prevalent on the market. Knowing what to look for protects against unscrupulous vendors.

Amber and Jet

Real amber and jet do not float in water. If yours floats, it's plastic. Neither stone should sit in water for extended periods, as both can be damaged.

Amethyst and Citrine

Amethyst is frequently heat-treated at approximately 400°F (204°C) until it turns orange, then sold as Citrine. True citrine does not grow in small geodes. It forms as single points, often containing black flecks of hematite and goethite that give it its characteristic golden hue. 💎Smoky Citrine has a distinctive olive tone.

Dyed Stones

Howlite Turquoise, Agate, and other stones are commonly dyed to resemble more expensive varieties. Signs of dyeing include:

  • Natural cracks in the stone surface appearing darker than surrounding areas
  • Unusually bright colors: fuchsia agate slices, dark navy blue stones, and vivid green agates
  • A white mark when scratched with steel (genuine stones won't show this)

Malachite

Authentic Malachite displays soft, muted colors with unpredictable patterns of loops and whorls. Fake malachite, whether reconstituted from offcuts or made from plastic, shows darker colors, regular patterns, and bold black bands. True malachite feels heavier than expected due to its high copper content.

Safety Note: Malachite, Agate, Chrysocolla, and similar stones can be toxic. Never cut, polish, grind, or carve them without proper protective equipment.

Moldavite

This green tektite's popularity has surged dramatically. Authentic Moldavite contains bubbles, inclusions, and natural texture. It feels rough and textured, not smooth, but also not like fine sandpaper.

Opal

Natural Opal displays organic color flash with variations in tone and shape depending on the viewing angle. Fake opals show uniform flash that remains identical regardless of how you observe them.

Note: Opalite is man-made glass, not a crystal. It may also be sold as sea opal, opal moonstone, or similar names.

Quartz

If a quartz is named after a fruit (cherry, strawberry, etc.), it's likely dyed glass. Glass can still serve magickal purposes. Just know what you're purchasing.

Turquoise

Howlite Turquoise is much stronger than water-soluble Turquoise, making it a common substitute when dyed blue. Dyed magnesite is also sold as turquoise. Deep cracks indicate magnesite rather than genuine turquoise.

Reconstituted turquoise is made by mixing ground turquoise offcuts with resin. To test: fake turquoise fluoresces under UV light. Consider carrying a small blacklight when shopping for stones.

Aura Crystals

Stones with "Aura" in the name are created by electroplating precious metals (one atom thick) onto Quartz, producing rainbow flash effects. To identify treatment, run a fingernail along the stone's surface. It won't have the natural texture of untreated Quartz. These stones can still be enjoyed and used in practice when purchased knowingly.
