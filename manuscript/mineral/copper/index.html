Magickal Lore

Copper has long been linked with the divine. During ancient Mesopotamian times, it was attributed to the Queen of Heaven and to goddesses associated with the planet Venus, including Ishtar, Astarte, and Inanna. It was also sacred to the Sun in Babylon and to the early inhabitants of the Pacific Northwest.

Energy Direction

Copper is well-known as a conductor of electricity. Wands fashioned from copper tubes, topped with quartz crystals and sometimes wrapped with leather, are used in magickal ritual to direct energy. Copper is also worn during ritual to heighten the magician's ability to direct energy toward the magickal goal.

Healing

Copper stimulates healing by balancing the body's polarity, the flow of projective and receptive energies. Blockages in this energy pattern lead to imbalances and disease. In Mexico, a copper penny placed on the navel before a trip prevents motion sickness. Copper is worn for relief of rheumatism, arthritis, and painful conditions. Copper wire loosely fastened around legs and arms relieves cramps. For health applications, copper is worn on the left side by right-handed people, the reverse for left-handers.

Luck

Copper is a lucky metal, perhaps because of its past solar attributions, and can be used in combination with any luck-bringing gemstones.

Love

As a metal of Venus, copper is worn to attract love. Emeralds set in copper and worn are particularly effective for this purpose.

Protection

Anciently, seeds of the mimosa were set into copper rings and worn, especially during confrontations, for protection against ills and negativity.

Money

Copper is used to draw money. Older copper pennies, especially those minted in leap years, have long been placed in the kitchen to attract money to the household.

As a Color

The warm, earthy tones of copper bridge the gap between gold's solar power and brown's grounding stability, making it ideal for workings that require both ambition and practical manifestation.

Copper's Journey to You

Copper is one of the oldest metals in human use, with evidence of it replacing stone tools as far back as 8000 b.c.e. It was the first metal smelted and cast in molds in Mesopotamia around 4000 b.c.e., and one of the first metals intentionally alloyed, combined with tin to produce bronze from around 3500 b.c.e. The Romans called it "Cyprium," the metal of Cyprus, the island that supplied much of their copper. Beyond human craft, copper appears throughout the natural world: as a component of other minerals such as malachite and chrysocolla, within ocean corals and mollusks, and as a trace element in the human body, concentrated in the blood and liver.

Worn as jewelry directly against the skin, it supports circulation and eases stiffness and discomfort, particularly in the hands and wrists. Held in the hands, it gently returns the holder to their natural state of balance.

Did You Know?

Copper is present in the human body as a trace element essential to the production of hemoglobin. Among the best dietary sources of copper is dark chocolate.

Metal of Venus and Protective Lore

Copper was one of the seven magickal metals of the ancients, a set in which each metal answers to a planet, its presiding deity, and a day of the week. The full set pairs silver with the Moon, mercury with Mercury, copper with Venus, gold with the Sun, iron with Mars, tin with Jupiter, and lead with Saturn, and in the Dharmic religions sacred objects such as singing bowls and temple statuary are made from a blend of all seven metals. As the metal of Cyprus, an island sacred to Venus, copper became her metal and the metal of the planet that bears her name.

It has long served as protection against lightning, since copper rods conduct the charge safely into the ground, and from this it gained a wider reputation for guarding against evil influences. In Egypt an engraved copper disc called a hypocephalus was placed beneath the head of a mummy, drawing on the metal's ability to hold heat. Copper amulets were easily engraved with invocations and charms, and the metal laid over the skin was believed to soothe insect stings.
