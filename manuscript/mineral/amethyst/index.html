Amethyst is a variety of quartz forming in the Trigonal crystal system with a Mohs hardness of 7, found in locations across the globe.

Magickal Powers

Cleans the aura's shields and protects them; draws in divine energy; helps to gain spiritual blessings; removes tensions and stress

Appearance

Purple variety of quartz ranging from pale lavender to the deepest royal violet, transparent to translucent, often found in geodes, vitreous luster. Its purple tones are produced by manganese, while the depth and richness of the color depends on the amount of iron present in its matrix.

Colors

Lavender, Purple, Violet

Origin

Brazil, Uruguay, Zambia, Madagascar, India, United States, Canada, Russia, Australia, South Korea

History

Evidence of amethyst use in rudimentary Neolithic jewelry dates back roughly 25,000 years across Europe. St. Valentine was said to wear an amethyst ring carved with an image of Cupid, connecting the stone to its role as the traditional birthstone for February.

Name and Legend

The name traces to the Greek amethystos, meaning "not intoxicated." Tradition taught that a stone held under the tongue or worn by a drinker would let them take in as much wine as they wished without ill effects, and goblets were carved from amethyst toward the same end. An account from 1750 even directed that the stone be bound over the navel to prevent drunkenness.

A Greek myth accounts for this link to sobriety. Dionysus, god of wine, felt slighted by mortals who overlooked his divine standing and set his lions upon the next person to pass in order to display his power. The traveler was a young woman named Amethystos, bound for the temple of Artemis. Hearing her cries, Artemis spared her further suffering by turning her into a pillar of clear, sparkling crystal. Stricken with shame, Dionysus poured wine over the stone in penance, and it stained the crystal its violet color.

Symbolism and Status

Its standing as the stone of sobriety made amethyst a favored choice among bishops, which earned it the name bishop's stone. The violet and purple hues tied it as well to the spiritual realms. Because purple dye was costly and historically reserved for the wealthy and the powerful, a crystal that held that prized color by nature was esteemed highly and read as a natural emblem of authority. That regard reaches back to the ancient world, where amethyst appeared as one of the twelve stones set into the breastplate of the High Priest of the Temple in Jerusalem. The Egyptians prized it as well, and shaped pieces have been recovered from the tombs of the Pharaohs, laid over the heart of the deceased.

Protective Lore

Alchemists and magicians of the Middle East treated amethyst as a guard against evil spells, an effect they believed grew stronger when the stone was engraved with the sacred names of the Sun and the Moon and paired with swallow feathers. They held that bodily ailments arose from a troubled soul, and that the high vibrational frequency of amethyst could help restore balance. For this reason the stone was powdered and worked into elixirs, an idea that anticipates the later belief that amethyst harmonizes body, mind, and spirit.
