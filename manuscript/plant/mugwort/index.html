Mugwort (Artemisia vulgaris) is a powerful herb sacred to Artemis, used for psychic protection, prophetic dreams, and safe travel.

History

The Latin name Artemisia comes from Artemis, the Greek moon goddess associated with the wild. Her Roman counterpart, Diana, became one of the goddesses most strongly associated with the witches' sabbat and the Wild Hunt, a motif in European folklore in which a group of supernatural hunters (sometimes believed to be elves, faeries, or the dead) pursues their prey across the night sky.

Mugwort is the first plant mentioned in the Nine Herbs Charm, an Old English protective charm recorded in the tenth-century Lacnunga manuscript:

Remember, Mugwort, what you made known,
What you arranged at the Great proclamation.
You were called Una, the oldest of herbs,
you have power against three and against thirty,
you have power against poison and against infection,
you have power against the loathsome
foe roving through the land.

Its mention in a charm against poisoning and infection reflects mugwort's widespread use throughout Europe as a protective magickal plant. Mugwort was believed to preserve the wayfarer from fatigue, sunstroke, wild beasts, and evil spirits generally. A crown made from its sprays was worn on St. John's Eve to gain security from evil possession, and in Holland and Germany one of its names is St. John's Plant, because of the belief that if gathered on St. John's Eve it gave protection against diseases and misfortunes.

Also known as cronewort, mugwort is thought to have made its way to North America during the sixteenth century. A remarkably prolific plant, a single mature specimen can produce up to 200,000 seeds, which travel easily on the wind. Scholars believe mugwort first arrived on eastern North American shores as an accidental passenger, seeds that had lodged in the hulls or cargo of ships crossing from Europe.

In traditional Chinese medicine, pulverized mugwort is used to create moxa (from the Japanese mogusa or mo kusa), the substance used in a treatment called moxibustion. A small amount of specially prepared mugwort is burned on or near certain acupuncture points in order to revitalize the flow of blood and qi (life energy).

Characteristics

Mugwort is recognizable by its deeply lobed, fingerlike dark green leaves, which reveal a silvery, nearly woolen texture on their undersides. A hardy perennial, it develops a dense, expansive, and shallow root system. Fresh shoots emerge in early spring, and the plant reaches full flower between July and September.

Magickal Uses

  • Travel protection: Mugwort is a powerful protector for travelers. Carry dried mugwort in a mojo bag (especially the Travel Protection Hand) to ensure safe journeys, ward off harmful spirits encountered on the road, and protect against mundane travel dangers.

  • Prophetic dreams: Place mugwort under your pillow or in a dream pillow to encourage prophetic and lucid dreams. The herb helps maintain psychic protection even while sleeping and traveling in the astral realm.
  • Safe passage: Historically used by travelers and pilgrims who would place mugwort in their shoes to prevent fatigue and ensure safe passage. The herb was believed to protect travelers from evil spirits, dangerous animals, and getting lost.
  • Psychic visions: Burn mugwort as incense or drink as tea (with caution) to enhance second sight, scrying abilities, and connection to the spirit world.
  • Clearing spaces: Fresh mugwort can be bound into a bundle and dried to create a smudge stick for clearing spaces of negative energy or lingering ghosts.
  • Astral lift-off: Surround the bed with fresh potted mugwort plants to help achieve astral lift-off during sleep.
  • Mirror and crystal ball charging: Rub fresh leaves on magick mirrors and crystal balls to strengthen their powers.
  • Scrying incenses: Add to scrying, clairvoyance, and divination incenses.
  • Third eye tradition: The odor is said to open the third eye and heighten awareness.
  • Disease demon banishing: According to Ainu and Japanese magical tradition, disease demons despise mugwort. Hang bunches throughout the home, concentrating on vulnerable areas such as doorways, windows, and the sickroom, to disperse and repel these malevolent spirits.
  • Summer Solstice gathering: Gather on Summer Solstice to protect against disease and misfortune.
  • Waxing Moon harvest: Pick before sunrise during the waxing Moon, preferably from a plant that leans north. Powers are strongest when picked on the full Moon.
  • Strength and courage: Carry mugwort when facing difficult journeys or challenges to maintain physical endurance and spiritual fortitude.

  • Dragon Boat Festival gathering: Mugwort picked on the Dragon Boat Festival, the fifth day of the fifth Chinese month, is used in door charms against malicious spirits and toxic influences.

Mugwort and Dreams

Mugwort is the preeminent dream herb. It does not produce dreams on its own: its action is to fling open the access point to the dream state, making the process more available, vivid, and retrievable. Even those who rarely recall their dreams will typically experience clear, memorable dreams under its influence. Most commercial dream teas, herbal pillows, and bath blends marketed for dream work are primarily mugwort, with other herbs added for flavor rather than magickal potency.

Mugwort enhances multiple dimensions of the dream experience simultaneously. It stimulates the frequency of dreaming, increases the clarity and vividness of individual dreams, extends the window of time available for recalling them upon waking, and because it also sharpens psychic perception, improves the dreamer's ability to interpret what they have experienced. It provides spiritual protection during the dream state, which can embolden the dreamer to pursue experiences in the dreamworld that they might otherwise avoid.

Mugwort tea is said to increase psychic ability, sometimes enough to induce an altered state of consciousness comparable to a mild psychedelic. Those sensitive to its psychoactive effects may prefer to make a charm out of dried mugwort and sleep with it under the pillow rather than drinking the tea. Either way, setting a clear intention beforehand deepens the work. An example: "It is my intention to receive from my ancestors an answer to this question in my dreams tonight."

Not everyone responds well to mugwort. For some, the dreams it produces are too intense, too frequent, or too disturbing for restful sleep. It is worth experimenting and noting personal reactions. Mugwort may serve best as a periodic tool for stimulating or jump-starting dream work rather than as a nightly practice.

Mugwort is a close botanical relative of wormwood and shares its characteristic bitterness. It is among the original bitter herbs. Additional ingredients in dream blends are typically included to make the preparation palatable, not to amplify mugwort's effects.

The Artemisia Family

The artemisia family bears the name of the Greek lunar goddess Artemis, who rules magick, fertility, psychic gifts, and wild female nature. Her favorite plants share her nature: they bear profound gifts but are prickly, volatile, and potentially dangerous, and few plants are more closely tied to the magickal arts. Mugwort and Wormwood are its two most famous members. Their unromantic English names advertise their power as pest repellents, and beyond inducing psychic ability, both banish powerful evil spirits as if they were little worms. This makes them especially valuable during psychic journeying, where they guard against psychic dangers met along the way.

The two work well in tandem: mugwort uncovers power, and wormwood provides the safety needed to learn to use it.

The Mother Herb

Mugwort is the primary sacred herb of the Anglo-Saxons, also called the mother herb and the witch herb. Lacking the notoriety of its cousin wormwood, it remains little known outside metaphysical communities, since it has few uses beyond magickal ones. Like most artemisias, tarragon excepted, it tastes bitter, which limits its place in the kitchen.

Its magickal uses, by contrast, are unlimited. No plant has greater associations with magick, and none better helps a practitioner reach their maximum magickal power. Nearly every commercial tea or herbal product sold to enhance psychic ability is made mostly of mugwort. Mugwort does not give psychic ability; it uncovers what is hidden within, revealing psychic gifts never known to exist.

Forms and Safety

Mugwort is available in several forms, and these are not interchangeable. Each acts differently.

Essential oil (Armoise): Mugwort Essential Oil is not safe for any therapeutic use. Its active constituents are far more concentrated in this form than in any other, and it is potentially neurotoxic. A bottle of mugwort essential oil is also easily confused with the flower remedy, which shares similar packaging. Always confirm which product you are handling before use, particularly if internal use is intended.

Dried herb: The dried botanical is safe for most people in moderation. It is used in teas, baths, herbal pillows, and spellwork. An infused oil can be prepared from the blossoms. The dried herb should not be used by pregnant women, nursing women, women actively attempting to conceive, or children, as it can cause profound uterine contractions. Aside from pregnancy, the herb is generally safe for adults in moderate doses. A working guideline: mugwort is a women's herb in the traditional sense, and if you are not old enough to menstruate, you are not old enough for mugwort.

Flower essence remedy: The flower remedy is the safest and most accessible form, particularly when psychic enhancement is the goal. Follow the manufacturer's dosage instructions for internal use, or add to baths and massage oils.

Cultivation

Mugwort grows freely on wasteland and disturbed ground and is not necessarily easy to cultivate deliberately, which reflects the independent character of its patron goddess. If it establishes itself for you without difficulty, this is considered a sign of the plant's willingness to work with you and support your magickal and psychic development. Mugwort can be grown from seed, but the most reliable method is propagation by cuttings or root division in autumn. Plant it around the perimeter of your home, grow it in pots indoors, and incorporate it into a dedicated witch's garden.

Magickal Teas

Mugwort can be brewed as a magickal tea to honor Hecate, support divination, and spark inspiration. Drink 1 to 2 cups only during the spell event itself.

Botanical Description

An erect, much-branched perennial herb of the Asteraceae family, up to 1.5 metres high, with purplish stems, dark green divided leaves which are downy white beneath, and numerous small reddish-brown or yellow flowers.

Origin & Distribution

Believed to have originated in eastern Europe and western Asia; now found in temperate zones all over the world. The essential oil is produced in southern France, Morocco, Germany, Hungary, India, China, and Japan.

Folk Tradition (Medicinal)

A herb of ancient repute, used as a woman's plant: a womb tonic, for painful or delayed menstruation, and as a treatment for hysteria and epilepsy. Also used to expel worms, control fever, and as a digestive remedy. Tradition holds that St John the Baptist wore a girdle of the leaves in the wilderness. In the East, the white fluffy underside of the leaves is used for moxibustion (often combined with acupuncture); the compressed dried herb is burned over an acupuncture point to stimulate it with heat. Moxa was also used in Europe to relieve gout and rheumatism. The herb is current in the British Herbal Pharmacopoeia as a specific for amenorrhoea and dysmenorrhoea.

Other Species

There are many species in the Artemisia group, which includes Wormwood and Tarragon. There are also several different types of mugwort, such as the great mugwort (A. arborescens) and the Chinese mugwort (A. moxa and A. sinensis), both of which are used to make moxa in Japan; their oils contain mainly borneol.

Meaning When Gifted

When mugwort is gifted it carries the meaning of tranquillity and happiness.
