Characteristics

A perennial bushy herb of the Lamiaceae family reaching up to 1 metre high, with a sturdy, hairy stem, large, fragrant, furry leaves, and white flowers tinged with purple.

Origin & Distribution

Native to tropical Asia, especially Indonesia and the Philippines. Patchouli is extensively cultivated for its oil in its native regions and also in India, China, Malaysia, and South America. The oil is also distilled in Europe and America from imported dried leaves.

Related Species

Closely related to the Java patchouli (Pogostemon heyneanus), also known as false patchouli, which is occasionally used to produce an essential oil.

Folk Tradition

In the East, patchouli oil is used generally to scent linen and clothes and is believed to help prevent the spread of disease (prophylactic). In China, Japan, and Malaysia, the herb is used to treat colds, headaches, nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, abdominal pain, and halitosis. In Japan and Malaysia it is used as an antidote to poisonous snakebites. Recorded actions include antidepressant, anti-inflammatory, anti-emetic, antimicrobial, antiphlogistic, antiseptic, antitoxic, antiviral, aphrodisiac, astringent, bactericidal, carminative, cicatrisant, deodorant, digestive, diuretic, febrifuge, fungicidal, nervine, prophylactic, stimulant, stomachic, and tonic.

Botanical Information

Pogostemon cablin (Lamiaceae family). Feminine flowering herb originating from India and South Asia. Also known as Pucha Pot, Pucha-pat, Graveyard Dust, and Patchouly. Leaves used for fermentation to create essential oil.

Herbal Lore

Historic herb reaching centuries before psychedelic associations. Layered with textiles shipped from India to England to protect from insects during sea voyages. Associations with luxury, money, and beauty developed from this practice. Antimicrobial properties kept fabrics from molding, creating protection associations.

Associated with Indian ink. Patchouli and camphor give ink signature scent. Perfect for writing money spells with traditional Indian ink.
