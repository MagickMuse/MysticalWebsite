Ivy (Hedera helix) guards, binds, and blesses in love and fidelity.

Magickal Properties

Gender: Feminine (source: Cold)

Deities: Bacchus/Dionysus, Osiris, Cerridwen, Saturn

Powers: Protection, Visions, Healing, Fidelity, Love, Resurrection

Ritual Uses

The thyrsus used in Bacchic rites was often wound round with ivy. During the Bacchanal revels, the leaves were chewed for visions. Ivy symbolizes resurrection.

Saturnalia and Winter Solstice: Used to decorate temples during Saturnalia and the Winter Solstice.

Guardian on walls: Ivy is a guardian when growing on the walls of a house or other buildings.

Magickal Uses

  • Good luck and bridal charm: Carried by women for general good luck and worn by brides for the same purpose.
  • House ward: Where ivy grows or is strewn it guards against negativity and disaster.
  • Fidelity and love: Used in charms to preserve fidelity and strengthen love. Paired with holly in traditional magick.
  • Bacchic crown: Ancient revelers wore crowns of ivy at the Bacchanalia, believing the plant prevented the worst effects of drunkenness. Cabbage was considered another such antidote.
  • Tavern signs: A green ivy plant or carved ivy leaf hung above a door once marked a place where good wine was poured. The custom survives in pub names and signage across the British Isles.
  • Spotting a witch: Folk tradition holds that a crown of ivy will reveal a witch to those who wear it.
  • Scottish soulmate sprig: A sprig of ivy carried in the bosom is said to draw the wearer's soulmate closer.

Dionysus and the Vine

Ivy climbs by vigorous tendrils much as the grapevine does, and like the vine it belongs to Dionysus, who is often shown using ivy to bind the nubile young women who would otherwise resist him. That same binding nature is part of why ivy turns up in love charms. The plant was also held able to both cause and cure drunkenness; an old remedy for a hangover was to drink vinegar in which ivy berries had been boiled, though most of the plant is poisonous and the cure should never be attempted, however fierce the headache.

The Maternal and the Clinging

A house with ivy growing over it was thought to be sheltered by the plant's maternal nature, though the same clinging habit was read as a less attractive feminine trait.

Ogham: Gort

Ivy appears in the Ogham Tree Alphabet, where it is called Gort.

Folk Medicine

Ivy has a long history of practical use alongside its magickal one.

  • Air purifier: Living ivy plants are valued for clearing stale and polluted indoor air.
  • Burns: Crushed leaves have been used as a poultice on minor burns.
  • Upper respiratory support: Preparations of the leaves have been used in folk remedies for coughs and chest complaints.
  • Sunburn and skin: External applications have been used to soothe sunburn, draw out skin toxins, and reduce the appearance of cellulite.
  • Stimulant: The leaves are considered mildly stimulant in traditional herbalism.
  • Insecticidal: Ivy preparations have been used to repel and kill garden pests.
  • Caution with the berries: Ivy berries are emetic and cathartic and should not be eaten. They are toxic in any meaningful quantity.

Winter Green and the Honored Dead

Ivy stays green all winter long, and for that reason it has been gathered alongside holly to deck the dark months. Where holly bristles with sharp protection, ivy clings and softens, the gentler half of the winter pair.

Its evergreen nature is also what binds it to the dead. Because it never browns, ivy was carried to gravesides and laid on headstones as a sign of life everlasting. In Ireland, ivy wreaths were placed on graves at Samhain, when the veil between living and dead was thinnest and the dead were closest to home.

Fairy Doctor's Reverence

Ivy taken from a grave is treated with great care in Celtic folk practice. The traditional fairy doctors of Ireland, the cunning folk who treated those struck or stolen by the fae, would not carry a leaf of ivy plucked from a headstone. To do so was to invite the dead, or those who walk with them, to follow you home.

This caution sits beside another fairy belief: that a person seized by the fae might vanish for seven years before being returned. Ivy is one of the plants understood to grow at the boundary between the human world and the fairy one, and it is handled with the respect such liminal plants demand.

Wales and the Green Lady

Welsh fairy lore is full of color. The Tylwyth Teg are said to wear blue petticoats, an echo of the older Druidical blue of office. The Moyddin of Cardiganshire are tiny green-clad fairies who haunt the woods in May.

In Glamorganshire, the Green Lady of Caerphilly walks the ruins of Caerphilly Castle in a green robe. When approached, she dissolves into a thick growth of ivy on the castle wall. Her transformation makes ivy itself her sign: the slow green tide that reclaims old stone, the place where a fairy and a plant become one.

Meaning When Gifted

When ivy is gifted it carries the meaning of wedded love, fidelity, friendship, and affection.
