Plant Grimoire

History of Herbalism

A journey through the traditions and trailblazers that shaped the practice of working with plants.

Ancient Roots

Herbs have been used by people since ancient times to address ailments and ease pain. Through generations of trial and error, herbal remedies were crafted and passed along through oral tradition. Monasteries were constructed around herb gardens specifically to cultivate plants for their healing properties, and these gardens became some of the earliest pharmacies and hospitals. The sick would travel to them seeking relief. At the same time, wise women tended their own gardens and foraged through hedgerows and woodlands, developing remedies independently.

Early Monastic Herbalism

Hildegard von Bingen (1098-1179) was a theologian, musician, visionary, nun, and healer whose two medical volumes were shaped by her experience cultivating a monastic garden. Her work was remarkable for several reasons: she drew connections between the health of individuals and the health of the surrounding environment, she cataloged the many medicinal uses of plants, and she wove in references to the four elements, the four directions, and the four seasons. Her writings offer a fascinating window into how herbs were used for healing in medieval times.

Herbs in the Ayurvedic Tradition

Ayurveda, meaning "science of life," is possibly the oldest recorded medical system. Originating in India thousands of years ago, Ayurvedic medicine takes a holistic approach, focusing on the entire being rather than isolating individual symptoms as the Western tradition tends to do. Highly personal and individualistic, Ayurveda incorporates treatments derived from herbs, minerals, animals, and even metals. Turmeric, for instance, is widely used in Ayurvedic practice to address inflammation. Herbal extracts are administered alongside diet and lifestyle adjustments such as yoga.

Traditional Chinese Medicine

Dating back three thousand years, the use of herbs in traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) remains widely practiced today. TCM incorporates a vast herbal component alongside acupuncture, massage, exercise, and dietary practices to bring about therapeutic results. Practitioners look for energetic imbalances within the body and treat the whole person rather than isolated symptoms. The goal is to maintain balance between yin and yang energies and harmonious qi, the energetic life force of the body. Herbs are prepared as tonics, teas, tinctures, and encapsulated powders, and as with Ayurveda, they are combined with other treatments and lifestyle practices such as acupuncture and tai chi.

Western Herbalism

The history of Western herbalism traces a path from ancient Mediterranean references through a series of groundbreaking publications that shaped how practitioners understand and use plants.

Dioscorides to Turner

For centuries, healers and physicians relied on the herbal written by Pedanius Dioscorides, which described plants from the Mediterranean. Many herbs and remedies within it were misidentified or didn't exist at all, born instead from the collective imaginings of writers who had never directly observed them. This led to plants being incorrectly used, sometimes worsening the very conditions they were meant to treat.

William Turner, often called the father of English botany, sought to correct this. Published in three parts between 1551 and 1568, his work identified and described medicinal plants from surrounding regions rather than relying on distant Mediterranean references. It contained illustrations, some detailed and colored, others less so, and is considered one of the most important scientific works of its era.

John Gerard

What began as a personal fascination with gardening influenced centuries of herbalism. In 1597, John Gerard published a work detailing over one thousand species of herbs, many of which he cultivated and tended himself. Informed by the Flemish botanist Rembert Dodoens, it became known as the first botanical catalog. Gerard included not only lists of herbs, their habitats, and growing cycles, but also their "virtues" (efficacies) and the folkloric traditions surrounding their use. His publication featured over 1,800 detailed engravings, a remarkable visual advancement.

Nicholas Culpeper

No herbal history is complete without Nicholas Culpeper, a radical in his day who later came to be recognized as one of the first medical general practitioners. He treated the poor people of London, creating and prescribing herbal remedies during a time when diseases ran rampant. Frequently at odds with contemporary physicians and apothecaries, Culpeper translated Latin medical knowledge into English, making herbal remedies accessible to a far greater population for the first time.

⭐
Culpeper and Astrology: Culpeper held a well-known belief in astrology and drew strong associations between herbs and the planets and stars. His work contributes to many of the magickal associations of herbs still held today.
🔬
A Trailblazer Beyond Herbalism: Culpeper was also a pioneer in women's healthcare and among the first to recognize the role of the egg in conception, a revolutionary hypothesis at a time when it was believed that male semen alone was responsible for bringing forth human life.

Ancient Herbal Witchcraft

One of the most remarkable windows into the history of magickal herbalism is the Lacnunga (meaning "remedies"), a manuscript written in a combination of Latin and Old English dating from the tenth or eleventh century. It contains detailed accounts of how herbalism was practiced with magickal intent, including methods for enchanting herbs with invocations and charms. This ancient pagan practice of combining words of power with actions of intent is considered a precursor to modern spellcasting.

Mugwort,Plantain,Fennel,Betony

The Lacnunga also preserves the Land Ceremonies Charm, an elaborate rite for blessing land before cultivation. Practitioners dug sods from each of the four cardinal directions under cover of night, anointed them with a potion of oil, honey, yeast, milk, and herbs, and carried them to a sacred site to receive the first light of dawn. The holes were filled with crosses or rune stones to form a protective magickal circle around the field. Before any ploughing could begin, the tools themselves were consecrated with a paste of Frankincense, Fennel, and salt, and the first furrow received a loaf of bread as an offering back to the earth.

Secret Witch Names for Herbs

While some ancient rituals, like the Land Ceremonies Charm, contain Christian overtones, it was fairly common for herbs to be associated with witches. Many herbs held secret witch names that were common in the Wiccan tradition. Secret names served two purposes: they protected the ingredients of a spell or charm from being discovered, and they protected the witch. If no one could identify the herbs in a practitioner's workings, they were far less likely to face persecution. Using coded names was a method of self-preservation for early witches. For modern practitioners, using these secret names can be a way to honor the past, acknowledging that these secrets were once necessary to keep the practitioner from harm.
