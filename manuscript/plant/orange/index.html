Characteristics

An evergreen tree of the Rutaceae family, smaller than the bitter variety, less hardy and with fewer or no spines. The fruit has a sweet pulp and non-bitter membranes. One distinguishing feature from Bitter Orange is the leaf stalk: the bitter orange's is broader and shaped like a heart.

Origin & Distribution

Native to China, sweet orange is now extensively cultivated in America (especially California and Florida) and around the Mediterranean (France, Spain, and Italy). The expressed oil is mainly produced in Israel, Cyprus, Brazil, and North America; the distilled oil mainly comes from the Mediterranean and North America.

Related Species

Numerous cultivated varieties of sweet orange exist, including Jaffa, Navel, and Valencia. There are also many subspecies, such as the Japanese orange (Citrus aurantium var. natsudaidai). Sweet orange is the close counterpart of the Bitter Orange, from which Neroli and Petitgrain are obtained.

Folk Tradition

A very nutritious fruit, containing vitamins A, B, and C. In Chinese medicine, the dried sweet orange peel is used to treat coughs, colds, anorexia, and malignant breast sores. Li Shih-chên writes that the fruits of all the different species and varieties of citrus are considered by the Chinese to be cooling; if eaten in excess they are thought to increase 'phlegm,' which is probably not advantageous to the health. The sweet varieties are said to increase bronchial secretion, while the sour promote expectoration. They all quench thirst and are stomachic and carminative. Recorded actions of sweet orange include antidepressant, anti-inflammatory, antiseptic, bactericidal, carminative, choleretic, digestive, fungicidal, hypotensive, sedative, stimulant, stomachic, and tonic.

The Golden Fruit and the Bride's Blossom

The orange, whose name passed through the Spanish naranja from the Sanskrit narang and Persian narang, came first from the Far East. A legend tells that Gaia, the Earth goddess, gave Hera and Zeus golden fruit on their wedding day, and these Golden Apples of the Hesperides are thought to have been oranges. The orange's tie to brides endures, though from a different root: the Crusaders saw the Saracens use orange blossom at weddings, both for its glorious scent and because the prolific tree, bearing flower and fruit at once, was a symbol of female fertility, and the custom of brides wearing or carrying orange blossom was carried home to Europe. Oranges mark weddings in China and Vietnam too, where newlyweds are given baskets of the fruit. The bitter Seville orange, brought to Spain by the Moors in the eleventh century, gives us marmalade, and so prized was the fruit that the Moors forbade any but a follower of Islam to eat the orange or drink its juice, holding it too exquisite and heavenly to share.

Botanical Information

Citrus sinensis (Rutaceae family). Masculine fruit tree originating from Asia. Also known as Sweet Orange and Bitter Orange. Originated in China, made way to South Africa by fifteenth century through trade routes. Peels, fruit, flowers, and leaves all magickally useful.

Herbal Lore

Glows with love, luck, and bright divination. Ideal for purification due to acidic properties. Fiery and solar associations burn away impurities and situations no longer benefiting us. Doctrine of Signatures: Golden fruit for prosperity and success. Thick outer skin suits protection magick. Associated with goddesses of home and hearth.

Ritual Uses

Dried orange peel and seeds are added to love sachets; fresh or dried blossoms draw wedded bliss, and blossoms added to a bath increase attractiveness. Orange peel belongs in prosperity powders, incenses, and mixtures; in Chinese tradition the orange has long been revered as a luck symbol. For a simple seed divination, think of a yes-or-no question while eating an orange and count the seeds: even means no, odd means yes. Use orange in fertility charms, happiness sachets, and harmony work in relationships. Dried peel in a dream pillow promotes deep sleep and beneficial dreams. Orange's fiery solar nature purifies: add peels to purification baths and cleansing rituals, and use it on Sundays for legal and success workings. Orange juice is often drunk in rites in place of wine, and an infusion of orange peels drunk before festivities was traditionally said to guard against intoxication. Distilled orange-flower water is added to love and lust potions and baths for attraction and sensuality.

Magickal Teas

Orange peel can be brewed as a magickal tea to aid divination, draw love, and boost luck. Drink 1 to 2 cups only during the spell event itself.

Moon Magick

Key themes for lunar workings: Love, divination, well-being, luck, money, beauty, purification, creativity.

Meaning When Gifted

When orange blossom is gifted it carries the meaning of innocence, eternal love, marriage, and fruitfulness.
