Linden (Tilia europaea; Tilia spp.) is a protective, lucky tree of love, sleep, and immortality lore.

The Tree of Truth

In parts of northern Europe, in Germany and Scandinavia, the lime or linden holds the same mighty place that the oak holds for the British and the Celts, and it should not be confused with the citrus tree that bears lime fruits. Linked to the Norse goddess Freya and counted her guardian tree, it grows to such a size that one tree in Germany needs over a hundred props to hold it up, and so it long served as a striking landmark for meetings and conferences, as other great and distinctive trees did. Because it is the Tree of Truth, these gatherings sometimes took the form of courts convened to uncover the facts of a disputed matter. For all its size, the reciprocal protection of Freya and her husband Thor, the god of storms, was held to make it proof against lightning, and like the baobab the linden was granted the standing of a member of the village community.

History

Since the late Middle Ages, infusions of linden flowers have served as a folk remedy for fever, headaches, indigestion, and hysteria. Their gentle, calming action made them a trusted household treatment across much of Europe for centuries.

Characteristics

Linden trees are large deciduous specimens, also known as basswood, capable of reaching up to 100 feet in height. The tree blooms in spring, producing white and yellowish flowers that must be gathered promptly and dried thoroughly in the sun, as the blossoms are delicate and spoil quickly. The flowers are highly fragrant and are commonly prepared as a tea.

Magickal Uses

From the source book: A symbol of love and passion. It may have been the ruler of the Summer Solstice in lowland areas where heather does not grow. Often used in love incense and with lavender in sleep pillows.

  • House and land ward: Extensively used in Europe as a protective tree; branches are hung over the door or the tree grown in the garden.
  • Sobriety: The bark, carried, prevents intoxication.
  • Love and immortality: Leaves and flowers are used in love spells; as a tree of immortality, leaves are used in workings of long life.
  • Sleep pillow: Linden and lavender equally mixed make excellent sleep pillows for the insomniac.
  • Good‑luck wood: Good‑luck charms are carved from the wood and carried.
  • Calming and Clarity: Linden flowers carry nerve-calming properties and are used in magick for de-stressing, clearing emotional turbulence, and restoring inner calm. Adding a linden infusion to a ritual bath is an effective way to prepare oneself before a sabbat or rite.

Magickal Teas

Linden (Tila) can be brewed as a magickal tea to draw love, boost luck, connect with the Otherworld, strengthen protection, and promote rest. Drink 1 to 2 cups only during the spell event itself.

Botanical Source

Tilia vulgaris (also classified as T. europaea) of the Tiliaceae family. The tree is a tall, graceful specimen reaching up to 30 metres, with smooth bark, spreading branches, and bright green heart-shaped leaves. Clusters of yellowy-white flowers carry a remarkably powerful scent.

Distribution

Native to Europe and the northern hemisphere. Common across England, France, and Holland.

Other Species

The broad-leaved lime (T. platyphylla) and the small-leaved lime (T. cordata) are among the related types within the genus.

Folk Medicine

Linden tea, known as tilleul, is widely drunk on the Continent and especially in France as a general relaxant. The flowers are also called upon for indigestion, palpitations, nausea, hysteria, and the catarrhal symptoms that linger after a cold. Linden honey carries its own high reputation, used in both medicinal preparations and liqueurs. Culpeper described the flowers as a "good cephalic and nervine, excellent for apoplexy, epilepsy, vertigo and palpitation of the heart." Lime flowers appear in the British Herbal Pharmacopoeia, indicated for migraine, hysteria, arteriosclerotic hypertension, and feverish colds.

Therapeutic Actions

Mild astringent, antispasmodic, bechic, carminative, cephalic, diaphoretic, diuretic, emollient, nervine, sedative, and tonic.
