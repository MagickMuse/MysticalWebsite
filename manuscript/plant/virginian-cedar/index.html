Virginian Cedar

Notes/Warnings
The wood is non-toxic and a renowned natural insect repellent. The essential oil is a powerful abortifacient with reported cases of fatal poisoning during pregnancy and must be avoided in pregnancy in any form. Externally the oil can cause local irritation and sensitization in some individuals, so always dilute well.

Virginian Cedar (Juniperus virginiana) is the slow-growing, narrow-crowned evergreen of mountainous eastern North America, the so-called Eastern Red Cedar. Despite the cedarwood name, it belongs to the juniper family rather than to the true Cedrus cedars.

Characteristics

A coniferous, slow-growing evergreen up to 33 metres high, with a narrow, dense, pyramidal crown, fragrant reddish heartwood, and small brown cones. Mature trees can reach majestic stature with trunk diameters over 1.5 metres.

Origin & Distribution

Native to North America, especially the mountainous regions east of the Rocky Mountains.

Related Species

Many cultivars of red cedar exist. Its closest European relative is the shrubby red cedar Savin (Juniperus sabina), a small, low-growing juniper with a much harsher, more toxic oil profile. Virginian Cedar is also closely related to East African Cedarwood (J. procera), an East African juniper distilled commercially for its similar oil.

The true Cedrus cedars belong to a different family altogether (Pinaceae). See Cedar for the Lebanon species and Atlas Cedar for the most commonly distilled true cedar today.

Folk Tradition

North American Indigenous peoples used Virginian Cedar for respiratory infections, especially those involving an excess of catarrh. Decoctions of leaves, bark, twigs, and fruit treated menstrual delay, rheumatism, arthritis, skin rashes, venereal warts, gonorrhoea, pyelitis, and kidney infections.

The wood is an excellent insect and vermin repellent. It deters mosquitoes, moths, woodworm, and rats, and was once paired with citronella as a commercial insecticide.
