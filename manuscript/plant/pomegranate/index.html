Source Specific Uses

Symbol of fertility and parthenogenesis (reproduction without fertilization). It was the only fruit allowed in the Hebrew Holiest of Holies. It was the mystic fruit of the Eleusinian Mysteries and was revered by the ancient Egyptians. Associated with the Winter Solstice.

History and Mythology

The pomegranate is one of the oldest cultivated fruits in the world, native to the region stretching from Iran to the Himalayas and cultivated throughout the Mediterranean for millennia. Ancient Egyptians buried it with the dead as an offering for the afterlife. In ancient Greece it was the fruit of the underworld, and it is through Greek myth that the pomegranate acquired its most enduring magickal meaning.

While held in the realm of Hades, Persephone ate pomegranate seeds (six in some versions, only a few in others) without knowing that consuming the food of the dead would bind her to that world. Because of those seeds, she could not return fully to the living. Instead she was condemned to spend a portion of each year reigning as queen of the underworld, her fate sealed by what she had eaten. The pomegranate became the symbol of that bargain: the fruit whose seeds wed the goddess of spring to the lord of the dead, and whose eating set the cycle of seasons into permanent motion.

The pomegranate's association with Persephone makes it a plant of dual nature. It belongs equally to abundance and to death, to fertility and to the underworld, to the sweetness of the living world and the permanence of the realm below. It is the fruit of binding oaths, of irreversible crossings, and of the sacred mysteries of descent and return.

Ritual Uses

Ancient Mediterranean cults used pomegranate in rites of fertility, protection, and the afterlife.

Magickal Uses

  • Fertility: Eat seeds to increase fertility; cast a fruit and count the seeds that spill to divine number of children.
  • Wealth: Carry the dried skin; add rind to money and wealth incenses; use a branch to discover or attract hidden wealth.
  • Guardianship and ink: Hang branches over doorways to ward off evil; use the juice as a substitute for blood or as magickal ink.
  • Childbirth protection: Hang fresh pomegranate boughs over the thresholds of a birth chamber to ease delivery and bar malicious spirits from entering.

A Fruit That Asks for Patience

A pomegranate cannot be eaten the way an apple is eaten. You cannot tear into it, you cannot drink it down like an orange. The seeds must be lifted out one by one, jewel by jewel, and the eating becomes a slow, almost ceremonial act. This is part of why the pomegranate keeps appearing in stories about thresholds and underworlds. To eat it is already to slow down, to commit, to choose seed by seed what you take into the body. The fruit teaches a sacred, sometimes-frustrating patience.

Eat Nothing in the Otherworld

Across many traditions, the rule for visiting the realm of the dead, the fairy kingdom, or any otherworld is the same: do not eat or drink anything offered. To accept food in such a place is to enter into debt or enchantment, perhaps through a mysterious plant working on the body, perhaps simply through the taking. Persephone broke this rule with six seeds, and the rule has held in folk practice ever since. Anyone working with the pomegranate in underworld magick should keep this in mind. The fruit is what binds.

The Forbidden Fruit

Many scholars suggest that the pomegranate, not the apple, was the forbidden fruit of the Garden of Eden. The story of Eve eating what she should not have eaten and the story of Persephone eating what she should not have eaten echo one another so closely that they may be two tellings of one older pattern: a woman, a fruit, a crossing made permanent. Wherever you place yourself in the argument, the pomegranate carries the weight of being the fruit that changes everything once it is tasted.

Inanna-Ishtar, Gula, and the Buried Pomegranate

The pomegranate is sacred to Inanna-Ishtar, whose earlier name was Inanna, and to the Sumerian goddess of death and healing in her Gula path. Their priestesses, unmarried women dedicated to serving the sick and the dying, buried whole pomegranates beneath the floors of homes where someone lay ill. The buried fruit was understood to draw the sickness down into the seeds and into the earth, and to call resurrection and continued life back up into the body of the sick. The rite tied together the goddess of healing and the goddess of death, and let the pomegranate stand for both.

The Tree That Was Once a Nymph

Although the fruit is most often spoken of with Persephone, the pomegranate tree itself originates in the domain of Bacchus, the Greek Dionysus, god of wine, celebration, and harvest. The story tells of a lovely nymph who was prophesied to wear a crown. Rather than marry her off to fulfill the prophecy, Bacchus transformed her into a tree whose every fruit wears its own small crown at the top. Prophecy kept, freedom kept, crown delivered without a husband. Every pomegranate on a tree is the nymph's coronation, still happening.

A Daughter Made Into a Tree

Another Greek myth tells of a young woman whose widowed father turned his attention on her in a way she could not survive. She ended her own life to escape him, and the gods, pitying her, lifted her dying body into the shape of the pomegranate tree. The fruit's red juice carries the memory of that grief. The tree is in this telling a refuge and a transformation made out of escape from a predatory parent.

Agdistis and the Blood-Born Tree

The origin story of the pomegranate told by the Phrygians belongs to Agdistis, a primordial intersex deity born with both male and female reproductive organs. In some tellings, Agdistis took the blade alone; in others, a jealous Dionysus tricked Agdistis into a self-inflicted wound during sleep. Either way, the male genitalia were torn away, and the blood that fell from that wound watered the ground. From that bloodied earth grew the first pomegranate tree, its juice the deity's blood made fruit. See the entity page for Agdistis for the full lineage and ritual cautions.

Menoeceus and the Pomegranate at the Tomb

Menoeceus was a Theban who ended his own life at the command of the oracle at Delphi, named for his grandfather who had done the same. From his tomb grew a pomegranate tree, and the juice inside its fruit was understood to be his blood, still flowing. The image of a pomegranate tree on a grave became, by this lineage, a sign that the dead were still feeding the living.

Sekhmet and the Pomegranate Wine

In Egypt, the pomegranate's association with blood passed into the story of Ra calming the bloodlust of Sekhmet. Sekhmet, the goddess of war, chaos, destruction, and also healing, had been loosed on humanity in a torrent of slaughter that could not be stopped by force. Ra had thousands of jugs filled with pomegranate wine and poured out across the land. Sekhmet, taking it for the blood of those she had killed, drank her fill, fell drowsy and satiated, and ended the destruction. The story is why pomegranate juice still substitutes for blood in offerings to her and in any rite where calming a furious force is the goal.

Kali, Pomegranate Teeth

The Hindu goddess Kali, after battling demons, returned with her teeth stained as red as pomegranate flowers. The image is one of victory and of the cost of victory both. Pomegranate flowers are offered to Kali in rites where the work involves cutting away what must be cut away and accepting the blood-color that comes with it.

Ganesha's Pomegranate

The elephant-headed god Ganesha is depicted again and again holding a pomegranate, and his connection to the fruit is strong enough that a commercial cultivar of pomegranate is named Punica granatum 'Ganesh,' bred to produce heavy crops of large pinkish-rinded fruits. The pomegranate in Ganesha's hand is the sign of abundance promised and removed obstacles paid for in advance.

On Gravestones

The pomegranate appears on gravestones across many cultures. Carved as a whole, unopened fruit, it represents everlasting life. Carved with the seeds visible or spilling, it represents hope, unity, love, passion, and abundance, the fullness of what the dead leave behind for the living. The same logic carries into artwork and altar imagery.

The Shapeshifting Battle

In the Tales of the Arabian Nights, Scheherazade tells the story of the second of the three Calenders, friar-like wanderers each missing one eye. In the second Calender's story, a king's daughter summons an afrit, a fire-spirit from the land of the dead, who is enraged at being called. The two enter a shapeshifting duel: lion against sword, scorpion against snake, eagle against vulture, panther against wolf. At one point the afrit becomes a pomegranate and tries to roll away from the lady-wolf. She pounces, and the pomegranate bursts, scattering blood-red seeds across the floor. The lady becomes a rooster and pecks up every seed except one, which falls into a pool and becomes a fish, and the battle continues until the afrit is reduced to a heap of ashes. The pomegranate is the fruit chosen at the moment of greatest peril, the form whose seeds can hide one part of the self until it is safe to rise again.

Why Grenades Are Named for Pomegranates

The early French word for pomegranate was grenade. The fruit's many seeds packed in a single chamber, and the way they burst out when the fruit splits, gave the military weapon its name. Grenadine, the red syrup, is also a direct descendant of the word, and is essentially pomegranate syrup. The botanical Punica granatum carries the same root. To work with a pomegranate is to work with something whose name has been borrowed by every other thing that scatters when it bursts.

Resilience and the Worm

Pomegranates are unusually resistant to worms and parasites that ruin other tree fruits. For this reason the pomegranate has come to represent resilience, the ability to remain whole through what would have ruined another. Offer the rind in workings for endurance, for protection of the body against what is trying to enter it, and for the keeping of a household that has survived hard times.

Pomegranate as Blood Substitute

The deep red juice of the pomegranate is the standard substitute for blood in ritual where actual blood is impractical, dangerous, or unwanted. Use it in offerings to deities who require blood, in spells of binding and oath, in menstrual or cyclical work, and in rites of the dead. A small bowl of pomegranate juice on the altar reads, to the unseen, as a small bowl of blood freely given.

Medicinal Notes

Medicinally the pomegranate is a vermifuge. A strong tea made from the bark of the tree has been used to clear tapeworms. The rind, high in tannins, is astringent and, properly diluted, has served as a wash for skin infections, a gargle for throat irritation, and a treatment for diarrhea and vaginal infections. Strong doses can induce vomiting, so dosage matters. The seeds and juice carry antioxidants, vitamin C, potassium, and magnesium. The juice has been shown to lower blood pressure and cholesterol and is being studied for anticancer activity. The flowers, rind, and root bark all have herbal uses; the roots and stems are not recommended for internal use, and the husk is not recommended for internal use when diarrhea is present.

Miniature Cultivars for the Altar

Several miniature varieties of pomegranate, notably Punica granatum 'Nana,' are grown as ornamentals. They do not produce especially viable fruit, but they make beautiful living additions to a magickal garden or a botanical altar. A potted Nana on the working table is enough to bring the tree into a small space.

Aphrodite's Fruit and the Tree of Knowledge

Because of the jewel-like seeds that burst from within when it is cut, the pomegranate is a powerful symbol of fertility and so was held an aphrodisiac; its very name means apple of many seeds. This made it ever-present at weddings, though it was kept from sacrificial rites as too seductive a fruit. Aphrodite favoured it, and as befits a tree of the goddess of love it stands for passion, sensuality, and sexual love, while older images tie it to her forerunner, the goddess Astarte. The pomegranate was also called the Tree of Knowledge, and in the Bible its name, rimmon, means to bear a child; in Syria it shared the name of the sun god Hadad Rimmon, who also ruled procreation. In Rome married women wore coronets of pomegranate twigs, and in ancient Egypt the corpses of the great and good were given necklaces of its blossoms before burial, a concession to the fruit's otherworldly weight. In Buddhism it is one of the Three Blessed Fruits, beneficent and a sign of fecundity and a prosperous future, while in Christianity it became an emblem of compassion that set aside its sensual side altogether.

Vulva Symbolism and Protection

The pomegranate is one of the traditional symbolic stand ins for the female genitals, the most protective of all images. Display pomegranate imagery in the home or wear it as jewelry to borrow that protective power. See Vulva.
