Wolf's Bane (Aconitum napellus), also known as Monkshood and "the Queen of Poisons," is one of the most dangerous plants in magickal practice.

Magickal Properties

Element: Water

Powers: Protection, Invisibility, Shapeshifting

Folk Names

Monkshood, Aconite, Wolfsbane, Queen of Poisons, Blue Rocket, Friar's Cap, Helmet Flower

Magickal Uses

Protection: Traditionally used in protection spells against werewolves and other supernatural threats.

Invisibility: Associated with spells of invisibility and concealment in historical grimoires.

Flying ointments: Historically included in flying ointment recipes. This practice is extremely dangerous and not recommended.

Species Across the Northern Hemisphere

The Aconitum genus contains more than three hundred species spread across the northern hemisphere. A. napellus belongs to Europe, A. carmichaelii to central China, A. uncinatum to North America, and A. ferox to India.

How Aconite Came Into Being

One origin tale carries the plant back to Cerberus, the many-headed dog of Hades who guards the entrance to the Underworld. Depending on the telling, Cerberus has anywhere from one to fifty heads, though most often three, with serpents along his spine and a dragon's tail. When Heracles dragged him up into the daylight, the hound grew disoriented in the brightness and began to foam at the mouth. Wherever that spittle struck the earth, aconite sprouted. The plant called wolfsbane traces its root to the magickal poison vomit of a supernatural canine.

Ovid's Metamorphoses tells the same tale by way of Medea, who prepared a poisonous draught from a drug long held in reserve, drawn from the foam Cerberus shed when Alcides forced him up from the nether skies. The name Aconite is said to come from the plant's power to unlock all bars and force its passage through rock. Hekate stands in the shadow of this story too, since Medea was her priestess.

Wolfsbane and the Werewolf

Wolfsbane lives at a dichotomy. It was treated both as a werewolf antidote and as the very substance that could induce an animal frenzy. The bane in the name speaks to its use as a poison cast at wolves, but it also speaks to wolf magick itself, the use of plants in the exacting dose that witches, healers, and shapeshifters once held knowledge of. Too much aconite kills or maddens. The right amount opens the psyche.

In his 1912 Book of Werwolves, the Irish folklorist Elliott O'Donnell described lycanthropic streams and flowers capable of transforming a person into a werewolf, with the antidote often growing nearby. Of Aconitum anthora he noted a yellow form shaped like a snapdragon and a red form like an ox-eye daisy, both said to metamorphose the plucker and wearer into a werwolf. Both share the same vivid colour, the same thick sticky sap, and the same faint sickly odour. Both are native to Austria-Hungary and the Balkan Peninsula, occasionally met with in damp, marshy ground.

In the Flying Ointment

Aconite was named among the recorded ingredients of the flying ointments. Witches rubbed those salves on themselves to take flight into the darkness, enter the astral plane, and gather the wisdom of the stars.

The Tuatha Dé Danaan's Helmets

In her 1887 Ancient Legends, Mystic Charms and Superstitions of Ireland, Lady Wilde described a cavalcade of Tuatha Dé Danaan knights riding seven-score steeds with jewels on their foreheads like stars, the riders clothed in green mantles fringed with gold and golden helmets upon their heads, golden greaves on their limbs, each bearing a golden spear. The image fits the yellow wolfsbane exactly: golden helmets upon a green mantle. The plant offers another bridge between the fairy and the field.

The Poisoned Arrow

The aconites grow in hilly country, their blue or yellow helmet-shaped blossoms standing in elegant terminal bunches, their lustrous green leaves cut into radiating sprays. The violence of their poison earned them the names dog's-bane and wolf's-bane. In earlier ages, arrow-heads and lance-heads were soaked in aconite juice to poison the wounds made in war and make them mortal.

In Chinese Medicine

A. carmichaelii has been used in Chinese herbal medicine for thousands of years as an anti-inflammatory and anti-arrhythmic. Practitioners apply it for heart disease, asthma, abdominal pain, joint pain, and gynaecological disorders including dysmenorrhea. The medicine relies on neutralising the deadly alkaloids while preserving the herb's potency. One drop too many drops the patient into coma or death. The active alkaloid is aconitine.

Unleashing the Inner Witch

Aconite can be used to unleash the inner witch and as a powerful amulet to remove obstacles and shift a life. With any poison plant there is the gamble that the purging sweeps something beloved away along with what was meant to go. The work is a be-careful-what-you-wish-for working. Aconite is most effective when grown in a protected corner of the garden where no pet, child, or unsuspecting visitor can reach it. Treated with reverence, it lends psychic and spiritual enlightenment.

Habit and Habitat

A shade-loving plant that likes damp soil, aconite adds a lurking quality to any garden. Stamens peer out from behind brilliant purple hoods. It is a plant that cannot hide yet still holds secrets. The hooded flowers are likened to the cowls of Benedictine monks, which is the origin of the name monkshood. In Norse mythology the same little hoods are seen as Odin's helmets or Thor's hat.

Hekate's Herb

Aconite was held sacred to Hekate, the queen of witches, who lent it one more of its many names, Hekate's herba. The symptoms of aconite poisoning, among them hallucinations, dizziness, and nervous excitement, are likely what fed the old belief that the herb helped witches take flight. Folklore adds that carrying the seed of the plant renders the bearer invisible.

☠️
DEADLY POISON: Handle with extreme care. Eating wolf's bane or getting root juice into a cut could stop your heart. All parts are highly toxic. Do not touch without gloves. Do not ingest under any circumstances. Even skin contact can be dangerous.

Meaning When Gifted

When wolf's bane, also called monkshood, is gifted it carries the meaning of "beware, danger is near," along with misanthropy and chivalry.
