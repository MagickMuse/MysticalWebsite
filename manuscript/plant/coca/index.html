Coca (Erythroxylon coca) is a sacred plant indigenous to Bolivia and Peru with a long history of ritual and magickal use.

Overview

The coca plant is indigenous to Bolivia and Peru and has a long history of ritual and magickal use there. Cocaine, the illegal stimulant, is a derivative of the coca plant; indigenous ritual incorporated the whole plant, not refined, concentrated derivatives. There is tremendous resentment among traditional ritualists for the way their sacred plant has been manipulated, corrupted, and politicized.

⚠️ Caution! Potentially poisonous and, depending upon where you are, most likely to be illegal!

The Food of the Gods

The use of coca leaves in South America is ancient. Mummies dating back to the fifth century have been found buried with a supply of the leaves. Chewed, the leaves produce a stimulant effect and leave the mouth feeling numb. The Incas recognized the plant's special qualities, called it the food of the Gods, and reserved its use for sacred rituals. With the collapse of the Inca Empire, those restrictions on the sacred herb loosened.

The controversy now surrounding coca comes from cocaine, its primary chemical compound. Its value as a medicine is overshadowed by its recreational use. Cocaine is highly addictive, and the profits tied to it give the plant's cultivation profound political and social consequences. Discouraging its cultivation has become a matter of policy in the United States.

Spanish Inquisition and Colonial Persecution

Coca use was discouraged by the Spanish, not because of its addictive qualities but because chewing coca leaves was associated with heathen devotion to the "huacas," the indigenous sacred shrines. The danger associated with coca was perceived as spiritual, not physical. Coca was a reminder of Peru's Pagan past that the Inquisition preferred to erase.

For the Spanish Inquisition, coca was the plant most identified with Peruvian witches and organized opposition to the new religion and regime. However, wealthy mine-owners wished to encourage coca's use so as to stimulate worker productivity.

The Royal Compromise of 1569

On October 18, 1569, a compromise was reached when King Phillip II urged priests to beware of the use of coca in witchcraft and superstition but to allow its use as medicine, especially as a stimulant to encourage the heavy forced labor imposed upon the Indians.

Traditional Magical Uses

Coca leaves were used in:

  • Peruvian love magic
  • Offerings to the spirits
  • An ingredient of psychotropic brews

International Associations: Magic and Stimulation

Coca's international associations with magic and stimulation, rather than with addiction, still existed during the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, when power potions first became popular in the United States, including the one that bears its name, Coca Cola®.

The 1630 Witch-Hunt Edict

In 1630, an edict against stargazers (astrologers) and witches was posted on all church doors in Peru. Among their crimes, witches were accused of using "certain drinks, herbs, and roots" including coca, San Pedro, and datura.
