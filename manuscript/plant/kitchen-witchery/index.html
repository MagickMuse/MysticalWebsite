Kitchen Witchery

The kitchen witch is a beloved figure in magickal tradition. While practitioners may identify as solitary, coven-based, or aligned with specific paths like Gardnerian, Alexandrian, Dianic, Celtic, or eclectic traditions, the kitchen witch stands apart as a deeply secular and grounded practitioner. The altar is the home and hearth. The coven is the community. The spells are edible, nourishing, and made with intention.

The Way of the Kitchen Witch

The kitchen witch performs every task with magickal intent. Sweeping the floor is not just cleaning: the cupboard broom becomes a magickal tool and the routine chore becomes a banishing spell. Preparing a meal is never simply cooking. Those who gather at the table become the coven, honored guests who receive the nourishment and healing the witch provides.

The kitchen witch is adept with culinary herbs and understands their magickal associations. Ingredients are just as likely to come from the greengrocer or the farmer's market as from an occult shop. Knowing which common herbs correspond to planets, deities, and moon phases transforms everyday cooking into spellwork.

Are You a Kitchen Witch?

Do you knead intention into bread as you work the dough? Do you pause to appreciate the transformations that happen when heat meets raw ingredients? Do you feel reverence for the aroma, beauty, and potency of the herbs you use? Do you find peace and power in a cup of tea, a transcendent stillness that others might only reach through formal meditation?

If any of this resonates, you may already be practicing kitchen witchcraft.

Feasting and Merriment

Sabbat rituals often conclude with feasting and merriment. Including magickal intention in your cooking is a wonderful way to prepare for the communal meal following a ritual. Even the preparation of food can be a ritual in and of itself. Adding auspicious herbs can amplify the magickal intent behind any sweet or savory dish. When you add herbs, you are not just adding flavor: you are adding a culmination of a seasonal cycle alongside the magickal properties of your herbs of choice. How you choose to do this will depend on the time of year and your intention.

Elixirs and Potions

The classic image of the witch preparing a potion is iconic. Modern witches are more likely to craft a potion or elixir for enjoyment and wellness than to cast a spell on someone. Adding a pinch of magick can enhance your experience with food and drink, as any kitchen witch knows.

The recipes and elixirs of kitchen witchery are designed to soothe or stimulate, calm or excite, depending on your will and your wish. As you gather and bless your ingredients, you establish a soul connection. Eating and drinking is one of the most intimate things you can do: it engages all the physical senses. Adding magick activates the metaphysical senses as well. When you take in nourishment, you are accepting strength and healing into your physical body, which can strengthen your psychic and magickal abilities. The body, the mind, and the spirit are intertwined.

This relationship of three entities in one form appears across many traditions. In Wicca the trinity is the maiden, mother, and crone. In secular witchcraft it is the mind, body, and spirit. The law of three is a reminder that the energy we send out returns to us threefold: a karmic call to put forth good works that harm none and benefit all beings. Magickal potions, elixirs, and feasts consumed by the body become part of the mind and spirit as well. Nourishment happens on many levels, and at every level, magick is afoot.

Incorporating Magick into Cooking

Knowing which herbs to use and what their magickal properties are can change the way you cook. You can project an intention into your food by using edible magickal herbs, physically taking in the energy of the spell or charm you wish to create.

Cooking with intention can also imbue your food with magick. Anything that you prepare with intention has the same power as a spell. A ceremonial bread baked and eaten with magickal intent is every bit as powerful as a carved candle or a hand-sewn charm.

Stirring sunwise (clockwise) is an invoking gesture. This is where you project those things you wish to call in or manifest.

Stirring widdershins (counterclockwise) lends energy to those things you wish to release or ward off.

Kitchen witchery can be a very intense and powerful type of spellwork because the crafting is done not only with intention, but the results are taken into your body. Just as a freshly baked loaf of bread represents transformational elemental magick, herbs can attune your psyche with the powers of water, earth, air, and fire. The fire element is not only the sun fire that allows herbs to photosynthesize: fire is also the hearth fire, the warmth of the oven and the stovetop, the brightly lit candles that grace the table when your beloveds gather.

Edible Flowers

One way to create a magickal recipe is to use edible flowers. Flower eating has been a part of various cultures for centuries. Squash blossoms appear frequently in Italian feasts, while in Japan, chrysanthemum tea is common. Roses and violets have been included in numerous dishes for hundreds of years. Modern witches can tap into this ancient practice and add magickal energy to their cooking.

Think of what the flower represents. In witchcraft, the goddess and the god are honored. The goddess manifests in her triple aspect: the maiden, the mother, and the crone. The flower is the maiden, the mother is the fruit, and the crone is the seed that is revealed at the end of the herb's life cycle. Adding flowers to your feast is a beautiful, magickal, and edible way to honor the goddess by having her represented as an integral ingredient.

Flower Maidens in Mythology

The Celtic goddess Blodeuwedd is one representation of the flower maiden. Another is Persephone, the daughter of Demeter. The goddess Flora, who was celebrated in ancient Rome, is another flower maiden who represents the season of spring.

Common Edible Flowers

Borage blossoms: The beautiful blue flowers of the borage herb have a light taste reminiscent of cucumber and can be described as slightly sweet. Their sacred star-shaped petal formation makes them a magickal ingredient for salads. Because they are slightly sweet, you might enjoy creating sugared flowers with borage blossoms.

Chive blossoms: Chives are a grassy herb with long, slender leaves that are richly aromatic. Part of the onion family, chives have beautiful lavender-colored blossoms with a flavor similar to chives, only milder. Add chive blossoms to any food in which you would use chives: tossed in salad, sprinkled on an omelet, or as a topping for potato salad or a garnish for cream cheese.

Dandelions: The bitter-tasting leaves of the dandelion are a well-known remedy for aiding the digestion of fats, but the bright yellow flowers are edible too. Since the flowers also have a bitter taste, they make a lovely, if sharp, addition to salads. For something different, try adding dandelion petals and honey to your favorite cornbread recipe.

Squash blossoms: If you have ever grown any type of squash, you know how abundant they can be, especially summer squash. Trim and collect the squash blossoms, which can then be sauteed, fried, or used in a stuffing. Savory and delicate, squash blossoms have been a part of Italian cooking for centuries.

Calendula: Although calendula is a type of marigold, do not confuse Calendula officinalis with tagetes, the marigolds commonly found in flower gardens. Calendula petals can be used in the same way as saffron, to impart color and flavor. Often referred to as "the poor man's saffron," Calendula officinalis can be used in starchy dishes such as rice, breads, and biscuits.

Nasturtium blossoms: Nasturtiums occur in such a wide range of colors that they add a dramatic bit of magick to savory dishes. Their peppery flavor makes them an attractive ingredient for tossed salads or as a garnish for soups.

The Kitchen Witch Effigy

The kitchen witch is often represented by a small doll on a broom, originating from eastern European tradition. She is usually depicted in her crone form: prominent nose and chin, seated on her broom with a kerchief or conical hat. Hung in a kitchen window, she brings good luck and wards against misfortune. Her energy is soothing, patient, and wise. A symbolic companion to the true kitchen witch who rules the powers of fire, earth, and air to create magick and nourishment from natural ingredients.
