Ginger (Zingiber officinale) is a tropical flowering plant whose aromatic rhizome is widely used as a spice and powerful magickal ingredient for enhancing spells and attracting success.

History

Ginger has been in continuous use for more than 2,500 years, with its origins in China. Over time it spread throughout the world and became a staple of both culinary and medicinal traditions. In Chinese medicine it is valued as a digestive aid and stomach soother, particularly effective against nausea.

Characteristics

The rhizome -- the underground stem -- is the part of the plant most commonly used. Depending on its region of origin, ginger may be called Jamaica ginger, African ginger, or Cochin ginger. It is a pungent, extensively studied herb whose effects on motion sickness symptoms are among its best-supported medicinal properties.

Magickal Uses

The root is placed on the altar and around a ritual circle involving elemental spirits as an offering, harvested in Asia in the autumn. Eating ginger before performing spells lends them power, since the practitioner has been "heated up" by the ginger, and this is especially true of love spells. Whole ginger roots are planted and grown to attract money, or the powdered root is sprinkled into pockets or onto money for the same purpose. Ginger is used in success spells to ensure the success of a magickal operation. In the Pacific the Dobu islanders chew ginger and spit it at the "seat" of an illness to cure it, and also spit chewed ginger at an oncoming storm while at sea, to halt it. A root of ginger hung in a doorway serves as a magickal ward protecting women in labor, while ginger root buried near an entryway guards the home. Wine steeped with ginger has long been used as a love potion. Ginger carries associations with long life and immortality within its broader magickal tradition.

Aphrodisiac Properties

Ginger's sharp, warming taste stimulates the senses, and the knobbly root often resembles a human form, reinforcing its age-old reputation as an aphrodisiac.

Culinary Uses

Ginger is a versatile kitchen witch ingredient, used in teas, healing remedies, soups, stews, and savory dishes. As a spell amplifier, it intensifies the magickal intent of any recipe it enters.

Seasonal Associations

Ginger is associated with winter, aligning with Yule and Imbolc, and carries the magickal principle of repose and rest.

Moon Magick

Key themes for lunar workings: Health, healing, love, money, abundance, success, power.

Botanical Source Detail

Zingiber officinale of the Zingiberaceae family is an erect perennial herb reaching up to one metre. Each year the rhizome sends up a green reed-like stalk with narrow spear-shaped leaves and white or yellow flowers on a spike rising directly from the root. The thick, spreading, tuberous rhizome is very pungent.

Distribution

Native to southern Asia and extensively cultivated throughout the tropics, including Nigeria, the West Indies, India, China, Jamaica, and Japan. Most of the essential oil is distilled in the United Kingdom, China, and India.

Varieties and Related Species

Several regional varieties of Zingiber officinale are used to produce oils with slight variations in constituent profile; the African oil, for example, is generally darker than oils from other regions. The closely related galangal (Alpinia officinarum), another member of the Zingiberaceae family, is sometimes called ginger root or Chinese ginger.

Folk Medicine

Ginger has served as a domestic spice and remedy for thousands of years, especially in the East. Fresh ginger is used in China for rheumatism, bacterial dysentery, toothache, malaria, and for cold and moist conditions such as excess mucus and diarrhoea. In the West it is best known as a digestive aid; the British Herbal Pharmacopoeia specifically indicates it for flatulent intestinal colic. Preserved and crystallised ginger is a popular sweet across both East and West.

Therapeutic Actions

Analgesic, anti-oxidant, antiseptic, antispasmodic, antitussive, aperitif, aphrodisiac, bactericidal, carminative, cephalic, diaphoretic, expectorant, febrifuge, laxative, rubefacient, stimulant, stomachic, and tonic.
