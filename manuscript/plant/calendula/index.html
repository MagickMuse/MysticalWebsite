Calendula (Calendula officinalis) is an annual herb with bright orange or yellow daisy-like flowers.

History

Calendula has roots in both Ayurvedic and traditional Chinese medicine. During the medieval period it was prized as a magickal, medicinal, and culinary herb all in one. In the Greco-Roman world it featured in ritual observance, and its blossoms appear in the garlands that adorn Hindu deities as well as in ancestral offerings during the Day of the Dead in Central America. For the Romans, it was a symbol of joy.

Characteristics

Calendula's vivid flowers have been used as a natural dye, a food flavoring, and a coloring agent for cheese. Native to southern Europe, the plant takes its name from the Latin calends -- meaning the first day of the month in the ancient Roman calendar. The blossoms open with the morning sun and close again in the late afternoon, faithfully following its arc across the sky.

Culinary Uses

Parts used: Petals

Calendula petals can be used in the same way as saffron, to impart color and flavor to dishes. Often referred to as "the poor man's saffron," calendula can be dried and stored for use in starchy dishes such as rice, breads, and biscuits. Do not confuse Calendula officinalis with tagetes, the marigolds commonly found in flower gardens.

Beauty & Skincare

Calendula is one of the most versatile herbs in botanical skincare. Its anti-inflammatory properties soothe irritated and sensitive skin, making it a reliable remedy for acne, rosacea, and general redness. It can be infused into oils, salves, and creams for topical application.

Botanical Description

An annual herb of the Asteraceae family, up to 60 cm high, with soft, oval, pale green leaves and bright orange daisy-like flowers.

Origin & Distribution

Native to southern Europe and Egypt; naturalized throughout temperate regions of the world. Widely cultivated, especially in northern Europe, for domestic and medicinal use. The absolute is only produced in France.

Folk Tradition (Medicinal)

A herb of ancient medical repute, said to comfort the heart and spirits. It was also used for skin complaints, menstrual irregularities, varicose veins, haemorrhoids, conjunctivitis, and poor eyesight. The flowers are current in the British Herbal Pharmacopoeia, specific for enlarged or inflamed lymph nodes, sebaceous cysts, duodenal ulcers, and inflammatory skin lesions. The infused oil is useful for a wide range of skin problems including cracked and rough skin, nappy rash, grazes, cracked nipples, varicose veins, and inflammations.

Other Species

There are several species of marigold, but the common marigold (Calendula officinalis) is the one generally used medicinally. It should not be confused with tagetes or taget from the Mexican marigold (Tagetes minuta) or the African marigold (T. erecta), the oil of which is also often called calendula. See Marigold for the Tagetes genus.
