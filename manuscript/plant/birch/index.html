Birch (Betula pendula) is a graceful deciduous tree with distinctive white bark and triangular leaves.

Magickal Properties

  • Gender: Feminine
  • Planet: Venus
  • Element: Water
  • Deity: Thor
  • Powers: Protection, Exorcism, Purification

Folk Names

Beithe, Bereza, Berke, Beth, Bouleau, Lady of the Woods

Magickal Uses

For exorcism: Birch twigs have been used to exorcise spirits by gently striking possessed people or animals, since the birch is a purificatory or cleansing herb.

For protection: The tree is used for protection, and Russians used to hang a red ribbon around the stem of a birch to rid themselves of the evil eye. The birch also protects against lightning.

For traditional witchcraft: The traditional broom of the Witches was made of birch twigs, and cradles were once manufactured from birch wood to protect their helpless charges.

Spirit sign: If a spirit appears wearing or carrying birch leaves and branches, this is the sign of a benevolent spirit.

For love divination: Birch is the divinatory tree for matters of love. Use it in workings of love and conception, as it is the tree of conception and birth.

Birch oil carries the characteristic odor of Russian leather and can be used in ritual work and perfumery.

Characteristics

A decorative tree growing 15 to 20 metres tall, with slender branches, silvery-white bark that breaks into delicate scales, and light green oval leaves. The male catkins are short (2 to 5 cm) while the female catkins can grow up to 15 cm long.

Origin & Distribution

Native across the northern hemisphere. Found throughout Eastern Europe, Russia, Germany, Sweden, Finland, the Baltic coast, northern China, and Japan.

Other Names & Related Species

Also known as European White Birch and Silver Birch. Botanical synonyms include B. alba var. pubescens, B. odorata, and the most widely used scientific name today, B. pendula. Many cultivars exist, and the closely related Paper Birch (B. papyrifera) and B. verrucosa are also tapped for birch bud oil and birch tar.

Not to be confused with Sweet Birch (B. lenta), whose essential oil is dominated by methyl salicylate and is potentially toxic.

Folk Medicine

Birch buds were once a staple of European hair tonics, valued for stimulating the scalp and bringing shine to dull hair.

Birch tar, a thick black distillate of the bark, has been used across Europe for centuries to treat chronic skin complaints, including psoriasis, eczema, and stubborn dermatitis.

In Scandinavia, young birch leaflets and twigs are bound into bundles and used in the sauna to tone the skin and stir up circulation. The fresh sap, tapped in early spring, is drunk as a seasonal tonic. The buds, leaves, and bark together have a long reputation in herbal medicine for rheumatic and arthritic conditions, particularly where kidney function needs support, as well as for fluid retention, urinary infections, and stones.

The White Tree

Birch trees are unusual: their bark is white unlike the more usual brown. Birches are the botanical equivalent of the sacred white doe or buffalo. They are symbolic of light, purity, healing, and magick.

Tree of Birth and New Beginnings

Birch is the tree of birth and new beginnings. This isn't merely mystical palaver but is based on some historical truth: birch trees are believed to have been the first to cover the land emerging from the Ice Age. Its use is certainly ancient; Ötsi, the Neolithic "Ice Man" who was found frozen in an Alpine glacier was carrying a birch bark bag when he perished.

Female Qualities and Goddesses

Birch is believed to epitomize female qualities. If oaks are essentially male, then birches are female. They are associated with powerful goddesses like Brigid and Sarasvati. Baba Yaga lives in the heart of a birch forest.

Etymology and Writing Material

The name allegedly derives from Sanskrit bhurga, meaning "tree whose bark is used for writing upon." Birch bark is used in that manner among various Native cultures of North America, most notably the Ojibwa, who put birch to many uses, but also in Russia, where birch bark "paper" is incorporated into spell-casting to leave messages for nature spirits.

Amanita Muscaria Connection

Amanita muscaria mushrooms grow beneath birches so birches are closely identified with these hallucinogenic mushrooms. The mushrooms may be understood as gifts of the tree. Birch wine and beer are also made.

Traditions of New Beginnings

Various traditions illustrate the identification of birch trees with new beginnings:

  • The birch is the first tree in the Ogham alphabet (Beth)
  • Cradles are traditionally carved from birch wood to provide blessings and protection and a good start for a new baby
  • As the tree of new life, the birch was frequently chosen to be the maypole
  • Birch is among the most traditional materials for crafting a witch's broom

The Fascis: Roman Symbol of Authority

Roman officials carried bundles of birch twigs as symbols of authority. A bundle of birch twigs with an axe in the center was known as a fascis and was originally intended as a symbol of generation and fertility. (Axes were symbols of rebirth and fertility deities, both male and female.) The fascis was appropriated by Mussolini and the word has since derived new meanings.

The Virgács: Fertility Blessings

Once upon a time, bundles of birch twigs were used to slap cattle and women (gently!) to boost fertility and offer blessings and protection. Many horned deities carried similar bunches of birch twigs. English has no specific word for this bundle of birch twigs but in Hungarian the word virgàcs (pronounced veer-goch) names this item. Krampus, Santa Claus' Central European "helper" is never without his virgàcs. The symbol also survives among the traditional accoutrements of the chimneysweep as well as among the birch twigs used to enhance the experience of the Finnish sauna.

Of Note

Of the many birches in the world, eighteen species are native to the United States. The one most North Americans picture when they hear the word birch is paper birch (Betula papyrifera), with chalk-white bark that peels in long horizontal strips. ⚠️ All eighteen North American species have been used medicinally by Indigenous peoples of those lands. The Anishinaabe call white birch wiigwaas and paper birch wiigwaasaatig. These traditions are sacred to the originating cultures and should only be practiced by those within them.

Medicinal Properties

Leaves are taken as a tea or tincture for urinary tract infections and cystitis and as a diuretic to move excess fluid. The same preparations support the body through gout, arthritis, and rheumatism.

Bark, with the fresh internal side peeled and laid directly against the skin, eases sore muscles. The bark also carries betulinic acid, an antimalarial and anti-inflammatory compound that has shown favorable results in recent study as an anticancer agent, particularly against colon cancer.

Sap of Betula lenta runs the sweetest and is boiled down into syrup. Birch sap is also the source of xylitol, a natural sweetener that resists tooth decay and now appears in many commercial toothpastes.

The bark, leaves, and sap of paper birch (B. papyrifera) are all used in the care of wounds.

Bark as Material

Birch bark is one of the great worked materials of the northern world. It is shaped into baskets, canoes, and the outer covering of dwellings, and it has carried writing for centuries.

The Sauna

In Finland, birch is the wood of the sauna. Cups carved from birch dip into the water that is poured onto the hot stones, and bundles of fresh leafy branches are used for gentle lashing of the skin to bring blood to the surface. The sauna is treated as a place of health and of spiritual connection. Sauna spirits or elves are said to watch over the room, and offerings of fresh birch branches are left to keep them appeased.

The Welsh Maypole

In Wales, the maypole is called the Bedwen and is traditionally cut from birch. In early Salem, Massachusetts, the household broom was made of birch as well.

Kindness and Sincere Love

Birch is powerful and abundant, but its character is gentle. It is the tree of kindness, sincere love, and compassion, and it belongs in invocations and spells for those qualities. Birch branches were once exchanged as tokens of affection between young lovers. Birch also gives protection to those traveling over water.

The Magickal Yule Log

Birch is the traditional magickal Yule log, burned at the turn of the year to bring luck into the household.

Calling the Forest Lord

Leshii, the Slavic Forest Lord, is a spirit best left undisturbed. When the work calls for him, the practitioner stands inside a circle of young birch saplings and summons him from there.

The Eyes on the Bark

The dark horizontal lenticels on white birch bark are read as eyes that hold the boundary of a grove and keep fairies from passing in. Where the eyes do not hold, fairies live among the trees themselves. In the Highlands of Scotland, the Ghillie Dhu, also spelled Gille Dubh, is a solitary fairy who dwells in birch forests. He is cloaked in moss and leaves, keeps to himself, and is described as kind, with particular tenderness toward lost children.

A Welsh fairy story tells of a man named Tudur who meets a tiny man in moss breeches with a fiddle under one arm, a coat of birch leaves, a helmet made of a gorse flower, and pumps of beetle wings. The little man plays his fiddle and lures Tudur into a fairy circle, where the music nearly enchants him. Tudur resists. The dress matches the Ghillie Dhu, but the temperament does not, so this is taken to be a different fairy altogether.

Baba Yaga and the Red Ribbon

In one Russian fairy tale, a wicked stepmother sends a young niece to fetch a needle and thread from her "aunt," who is in fact Baba Yaga. The girl goes first to her dead mother's sister and is warned what to do at the witch's door: tie a ribbon around the birch tree that would otherwise lash her in the eye, pour oil on the hinges of the doors that would creak and bang, throw rolls to the dogs that would tear her in pieces, and give a piece of bacon to the cat that would scratch out her eyes.

This is why young Russian women still tie small red ribbons onto newly sprouting birch trees on festival days, encouraging the trees to thrive and asking, in return, for the trees' protection.

The Birch Tree and the Fool

A second Russian tale begins with a man who had three sons, two clever and one who could tell no lies. When the father died, the clever brothers split the estate and the youngest was left with a single skinny ox.

The brothers set off to sell their goods at market. The fool tied a rope to his ox and walked it through a forest where a very old birch tree creaked in the wind. He took the creaking for haggling. "If you want to buy the ox," he called to the tree, "the price is twenty rubles." The tree creaked on. "Alright, on credit," he said. He tied the ox to the trunk and went home.

The next morning the ox was gone, eaten by wolves in the night, and the tree still creaked. The fool demanded payment. The tree creaked. "One more day," he allowed, and went home again. His brothers, learning he had sold the ox to a withered birch in the forest, groaned.

On the third morning he returned with a hatchet. The tree creaked, no money, no ox. Furious, he swung his hatchet into the trunk. The old birch had a hollow, and in the hollow thieves had stashed a pot of gold. "So that was what you were keeping from me," said the fool. He carried home as much as he could.

His brothers' eyes went wide at the gold, and the three returned for the rest. They agreed never to tell anyone. On the way back they met a Diachok, a layman who served the local church. The clever brothers said they were hunting mushrooms. The fool called out, "Gold, see." When the Diachok reached for the sack, the fool struck him dead with the hatchet. Horrified, the brothers dragged the body home and hid it in the cellar. A few days later, as the village searched for the missing man, the brothers killed a goat, buried it in the cellar in the body's place, and moved the Diachok deep into the woods.

When the villagers came to the door and asked where the Diachok was, the fool answered cheerfully that he had killed him days ago and that his brothers had put him in the cellar. The mob stormed in. From below, the fool called up, "Did your Diachok have dark hair?" "He did." "And a beard?" "Yes." "And two horns?" The crowd stared. He came up the stairs and threw the goat's head at them. The villagers left the brothers alone after that.

The birch in this tale is the keeper of buried wealth and the unwitting accomplice to a fool whose strange luck protects him because no one can ever quite tell whether he is speaking the truth or speaking nonsense.

The Pioneer Tree

Fast-growing, the birch is often set among the saplings of slower trees such as oak to shelter them while they take hold, and from this nurturing habit it draws its reputation for protection. As the first tree to show itself in a young forest it earns the name pioneer tree, a fittingness echoed in the Ogham alphabet, where Beth, the birch, stands as the very first letter and marks the start, the beginning, the birth.

Bright Bark and the Tree of Both

The root of the word birch is shared with bright, and the bark of the silver and Himalayan birches is just that, luminous and beautiful and held as a sign of purity. It does not rot, lending the tree an air of indestructibility, and being supple enough to write upon it has served as a parchment for spells. ⚠️ Among the Ojibwa, the birch bark scrolls of the Midewiwin, or Grand Medicine Society, carry the sacred symbols that recall the secrets of the society and become a treasured possession of the initiate, holding a significance akin to the tracing board of Freemasonry. These traditions belong to the originating cultures and should be practiced only by those within them.

The birch is at once male and female and bears the attributes of both, a tree of the Sun and of the Moon alike, for it carries male and female flowers upon the same stem. Its slender trunk, gracefully drooping branches, and delicate leaves have earned it the name Lady of the Woods, while the dense clusters of small twigs that gather in its crown are known as witches' brooms.

World Tree of the Druids and the Siberian Peoples

The birch is sacred not only to the Druids but to the Siberian peoples, who hold it as the very emblem of the World Tree and the Axis Mundi. In their symbolism the tree stands at the center of initiation, climbed to draw a symbolic link with the gods and circled nine times for the nine steps to Heaven, a circling that mirrors the old custom of dancing about the maypole, once performed around a living tree. The sweet sap of the birch can be drawn off as syrup or fermented into wine, and the inner bark holds a pain-easing virtue. The tree lends its name as well to a form of corporal punishment once known as birching.

Meaning When Gifted

When birch is gifted it carries the meaning of grace and meekness.

Birch Grove of Psychic Protection

Birch trees are associated with fertility, meaning not only literal reproductive capacity but also growth and creativity, especially psychic, magickal, and spiritual growth. Birch protects as well as enhances, which matters because emerging talents must be nurtured and protected. Surround the home with birch trees to enhance and protect your own psychic power.
