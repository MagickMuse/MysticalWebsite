Tarragon (Artemisia dracunculus) is a fragrant herb in the Artemisia family, used for love and the hunt.

Characteristics

A perennial herb with smooth narrow leaves, an erect stem up to 1.2 metres tall, and small yellowy-green inconspicuous flowers.

Origin & Distribution

Native to Europe, southern Russia, and western Asia. Now cultivated worldwide, especially in Europe and the United States. The essential oil is mainly produced in France, Holland, Hungary, and the United States.

Other Names & Related Species

The so-called French tarragon, or sativa, is cultivated as a garden herb. It is a smaller plant with a sharper flavour than the Russian type and is a sterile derivative of the wild species.

Folk Tradition

The leaf is commonly used as a domestic herb, especially with chicken or fish, and to make tarragon vinegar. The name is thought to derive from an ancient use as an antidote to the bites of venomous creatures and mad dogs. It was favoured by the maharajahs of India, who took it as a tisane, and in Persia it was used to induce appetite. The leaves were considered heating and drying, and good for those with the flux or any preternatural discharge. The leaf was also formerly used for digestive and menstrual irregularities, while the root was employed as a remedy for toothache.

Safety

The essential oil is moderately toxic due to estragole (methyl chavicol) and possibly carcinogenic. Use in moderation only and avoid during pregnancy.

The Little Dragon

Tarragon, the baby of the Mugwort family, is known as the little dragon. It is the only artemisia that doubles as a common kitchen herb, but it holds magickal powers just like its more notorious relatives. Dragon is a frequent euphemism for menstruation, and tarragon is used to magickally access women's primal psychic powers.
