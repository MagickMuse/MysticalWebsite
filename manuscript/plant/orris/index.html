🐰Ostara

Orris (Iris germanica var. florentina; Iris germanica; Iris pallida) is famed for drawing and holding love, guarding, and scrying. It belongs to the Iridaceae family.

Characteristics

A decorative perennial plant reaching up to 1.5 metres high, with sword-shaped leaves, a creeping fleshy rootstock, and delicate, highly scented, pale blue flowers.

Origin & Distribution

Native to the eastern Mediterranean region, orris is also found in northern India and North Africa. Most commercial orris is produced in Italy, where it grows wild. The oil is mainly produced in France and Morocco, with smaller quantities from Italy and the USA.

Related Species

There are many species of iris, and cultivation has produced further types. In Italy, the pale iris (Iris pallida) is collected indiscriminately with the Florentine orris (Iris florentina), which has white flowers tinged with pale blue, and the common or German iris (Iris germanica), which has deep purple flowers with a yellow beard. Other species used medicinally include the American blue flag (Iris versicolor) and the yellow flag iris (Iris pseudacorus).

Folk Tradition

In ancient Greece and Rome, orris root was used extensively in perfumery, and its medicinal qualities were held in high esteem by Dioscorides. The juice of the root was used for cosmetic purposes, and the root bruised in wine was employed for dropsy, bronchitis, coughs, hoarseness, chronic diarrhoea, and congested headaches. In Russia the root was used to make a tonic drink with honey and ginger. Iris is little used medicinally these days but still appears in the British Herbal Pharmacopoeia as formerly used for upper respiratory catarrh, coughs, and diarrhoea in infants. Recorded actions of the dried root are antidiarrhoeal, demulcent, and expectorant; the fresh root is diuretic, cathartic, and emetic, and causes nausea and vomiting in large doses.

Magickal Uses

  • Love drawing: Whole root carried; powder added to love sachets; sprinkled on sheets, clothing, the body, and around the house. Orris powder is widely known as “Love Drawing Powder.”
  • Protection: In Japan, roots and leaves were hung from eaves and added to bath water to ward off evil spirits and for personal protection. Although European-derived magick uses orrisroot almost solely for love and seduction, Japanese tradition prizes it for spiritual protection. Whole roots hung from the ceiling protect a space, and an infusion of the root added to the bath protects the bather (see Orrisroot Protection Bath).
  • Divination: Suspend a whole root from a cord as a pendulum to answer questions.
  • Purification: Fresh flowers placed in the space to be cleansed; the three petals invoke faith, wisdom, and valor.
  • Blessing & Wisdom: The fleur-de-lis symbolism connects Iris to Juno and the qualities of harmony and peace.
  • Scent fixative: Dried orrisroot preserves other fragrances in perfumery and magickal blends. This "fixing" quality extends to love magick, where it helps hold love in place.

Iris, Goddess of the Rainbow

The flower takes its name from Iris, the Greek goddess of the rainbow and a female messenger of the Gods, counterpart to Hermes. She wore a rainbow-colored veil that streamed behind her as she flew and, like Hermes, traveled on winged sandals, and the blossom echoes her in its iridescent shades of purple, yellow, blue, and white. Iris was believed to guide the souls of women into the next world, so in Greece her flowers were laid on the graves of girls and women in thanks to the goddess for tending their spirits.

The plant was once called the sword lily for the shape of its leaves, and though the name gladiolus means little sword, the true iris and the gladiolus are different flowers. Alongside the lily, the iris is said to have inspired the fleur-de-lys, and like that stylized emblem it stood for both spiritual faith and worldly authority. Amulets carved from the root were carried to guard the wearer from malice and from hostile spells.

In Japan the iris serves as a purifier and preserver, planted on thatched roofs to protect the home because it was thought to prevent fire. The fifth of May is the day of the iris bath, when the leaves of the shobu are steeped in hot water so the ritual may protect the bather through the year to come, and the chopped leaves are also added to sake as a further blessing.
