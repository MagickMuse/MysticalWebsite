Other Names & Related Species

The genus Acacia spans more than four hundred species. The body of this page focuses on Senegal acacia (Acacia senegal), source of gum arabic. Several close cousins are commercially distinct in fragrance and herbal practice.

  • Cassie (Acacia farnesiana): the sweet acacia of West Indian origin, source of cassie absolute. See Cassie and Cassie Absolute.
  • Mimosa (Acacia dealbata): a perfumery cousin used for mimosa absolute. See Mimosa.
  • Roman cassie (Acacia cavenia): another perfumery acacia traded under similar names.
  • Gum Arabic / Gum Acacia (Acacia senegal): the gum exudation profiled here. See Untitled for the resin entry.

Ritual Uses

The wood is used as fuel in sacred fires in India, and is also used in building temples.

Magickal Uses

A sprig of the tree placed over the bed wards off evil, as it does when tucked into the turban in Eastern countries. Twigs set above the bed also guard the sleeper against spiritual dangers that arrive in the night. When the wood is burned with sandalwood the psychic powers are stimulated. Acacia is also used in money and love spells, although in the latter case the outcome would be a platonic love. Sawdust or finely ground twigs can be worked into a thick paste with aromatic herbs and oils, shaped into scented beads, and strung once dry. Acacia flowers are burned as offertory incense to a goddess during love rituals and added to psychic vision incense blends. Burned as incense, acacia stimulates and enhances psychic ability and opens contact with the sacred, a bond to the holy that stretches back through the myth of Osiris to the Ark of the Covenant.

Additional Notes

Also referenced under Arabic Gum, used to purify areas of negativity and evil when burned as incense or smoldered alone on charcoal.

Purification

Burn gum arabic as incense or smolder alone on charcoal to clear areas of negativity and malevolent energy. This purifying application is one of the most widely cited traditional uses across African and Near Eastern traditions.

Interchangeability

The species of acacia that produce gum arabic and gum acacia are so closely related that one product can be substituted for the other in formulas. Gum acacia is particularly valued in protective and psychic awareness formulas.

In Freemasonry

In Freemasonry, acacia represents initiation and serves as a symbol of rebirth in Masonic funerals. In the legend of Hiram Abiff, an acacia branch marks his burial place.

Desert Resilience and the Hidden Secret

The acacia thrives in barren desert country, and single specimens have endured for thousands of years through drought and famine, giving both beasts and people shelter from the scorching Sun and yielding edible leaves and seeds. For this it became an emblem of protection and of resilience. Its vicious thorns are said to guard the secrets the tree conceals, and its wood, hard, close-grained, of a beautiful orange colour, proof against insects and seemingly incorruptible, marked it as a sign of purity, so that it was burned only for particular magickal ends.

Sacred Timber: Shittim, Ark, and Cross

The acacia stood long before the Bible was written, and it is thought to be the Shittim tree of the Old Testament, whose timber, overlaid with gold, was wrought into the Ark of the Covenant. The Ark itself is believed to hold profound magickal, spiritual, and protective power. It is among the trees named as the source of Christ's crown of thorns, the same cruel crown once worn by sacrificial kings, and the Cross itself, sometimes called a tree, was likely cut from strong and durable acacia. So sacred was it to the Jewish people that it was never put to common use such as the making of furniture.

Hiram Abiff and the Innocence of the Soul

In Freemasonry a sprig of acacia is laid upon the coffin at a brother's funeral in memory of Hiram Abiff, the builder of King Solomon's Temple, whose betrayal echoes that of Christ and stands among the order's central tenets. The sprig laid on his grave marks not only death and resurrection but his refusal, like the tree, to give up certain secrets. Its evergreen leaf signifies the immortality of the soul, and its incorruptibility the purity of Hiram's own. Fittingly, the Greek word for innocence, akakia, is the very name of the tree, for Hiram too was innocent, choosing death over surrendering the password that would have raised his assassins to the rank of Master Mason. The acacia was likewise the emblem of the Arab Goddess Al'Uzza, who presides over birth and death, the turning seasons, and the planets and stars.

Meaning When Gifted

When acacia is gifted it carries the meaning of secret love, though tradition holds it unlucky to give to a woman.
