Ash (Fraxinus excelsior) is a tall, stately tree long revered as the World Tree connecting upper, middle, and lower realms.

Magickal Properties

Gender: Masculine

Planet: Sun, Neptune

Element: Fire, Water

Deities: Odin, Thor, Poseidon, Gwydion, Mars, Uranus

Powers: Water, Strength, Intellect, Willpower, Protection, Justice, Balance, Harmony, Skill, Travel, Wisdom

Magickal Uses

For protection: Ash is powerfully protective. Plant an ash tree in your yard to protect your home and family. Ash leaves placed under the pillow induce prophetic dreams.

For sea magick: Ash wood is traditionally used for protective amulets by sailors. Leaves placed in a bowl of water beside the bed protect against drowning and seasickness.

For healing: Ash wands are used in healing rituals. The leaves and bark have long been used in folk medicine.

For the World Tree: The ash is identified with Yggdrasil, the World Tree of Norse mythology, connecting the nine worlds. This cosmic connection makes ash powerful for journeying between realms and for gaining wisdom.

For Druidic ritual: Druidic wands fashioned from ash bear spiral decorations carved into the wood. In Druidic tradition ash is ruled by the Sun, and its leaves were scattered in the four directions with an invocation to the forces of life, used for workings of wealth, success, healing, and other solar matters.

For protection against drowning: Carve an equal-armed cross from ash wood and carry it as a protective amulet against drowning.

For poppet work: When mandrake root is unavailable, poppets carved from ash roots substitute well in healing and other ritual work.

For sea rituals: Ash is suited to sea rituals of all kinds.

The Meliai: The spirits of the ash are called the Meliai, who bring inspiration to those who work with the tree.

Additional Notes

The ash is the World Tree of Norse tradition, Yggdrasil, upon which Odin hung to gain the wisdom of the runes. This cosmic tree connects the upper world of the gods, the middle world of humans, and the lower world of the ancestors. Working with ash connects the practitioner to this axis mundi.

If ash grows with oak and hawthorn, fairies are said to dance among them. This sacred triad marks places where the veil between worlds is thin.

Amulet Uses

Ash tree leaves carried as an amulet can be used to bless or to curse.

Native Range

With more than sixty species, the ash grows across a wide swath of the world. Native populations range from Northeast Asia through China and the highlands of Pakistan, India, and Afghanistan, across France, England, Scotland, and Ireland, and into North America.

Medicinal Properties

Nearly every part of the ash tree carries medicinal value. The plant is antiseptic, anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, anticancer, antifungal, antiviral, and antioxidant. It can be used to treat topical wounds, infections, and bruising, and to ease gout, arthritis, general inflammation, edema, and urinary diseases.

Yggdrasil and the Sacred Wells

In Norse cosmology, Yggdrasil is the world tree beneath which the gods assemble, and it is named as an ash. Branches stretch across the world and into the heavens to embrace the whole cosmos, while roots reach deep into the earth and the underworld. The tree is the seat of life and of death. Beneath it is the horn that will herald the end of times, Ragnarok. Norse myth also names the ash as the wood from which the first human being was made.

Modern readings of the Poetic Edda count three wells associated with Yggdrasil's roots, with one held by an above-ground root. To this day, a well that stands near an ash tree is considered holy.

Greek and Roman Lore

The Greeks and Romans counted the ash as a tree of good luck. Cupid used ash for his arrows until he found cypress to be more effective. Ash is said to ward off serpents and evil, and a fire built of ash wood or ash leaves carries protective force.

The Ash and the Adder

A Cornish belief holds that no snake is ever found near an ash tree, and that a single branch of ash will keep a snake from approaching a person. Folkard recounts the story of a child who received a portion of bread and milk at the cottage door each day and shared it with a poisonous adder. The reptile came regularly each morning, and the child, charmed by its companion's beauty, encouraged the visits. When the mother, a field labourer who left the child alone all day, learned of the friendship, she found she could not keep the snake away. She finally bound an ashen twig about the child's body. The adder no longer came near, but from that day forward the child pined and at last died, by all accounts of grief for the lost companion who had so fascinated it.

Sap, Nail Clippings, and the Singing Voice

Ash sap carries curative and protective virtues. An old Scottish Highland custom held that a baby's very first food should be a taste of ash sap. If an infant's first nail clippings are buried beneath an ash tree, the child will be gifted with a beautiful singing voice.

The Yule Log

Ash and birch are the two woods most often chosen for the sacred Yule log, which is burned to bring luck and good health into the new year.

Protection from Bewitchment

The ash tree guards against bewitchment and enchantment. Its branches may be wrapped around the horns of cattle or around the rails of a child's cradle for safekeeping. If you should witness a fairy procession, or fall under a fairy spell that draws you toward the ring where they cavort, take up an ash branch for protection.

The Ghost in the Ash at Killiecrankie

A ghost story from the turn of the twentieth century places a vengeful spirit in the safe harbor of an ash tree. A woman finds herself out a little too late in the evening at the Pass of Killiecrankie, the beautiful Highland glen where a Jacobite battle was once fought, and which is reputed to be haunted. She sees the ghosts of a band of Highlanders running across her path and follows them against her better judgment. The land before her is strewn with the bodies of Scottish and British soldiers. In that moment the specter of a Highlander maiden drops from the ash tree before her, draws a phantom dagger with a wicked grin, and stabs it into the hearts of the British soldiers lying dying on the ground.

The Hardy Tree of London

A London ash, known as the Hardy Tree, stood for at least a hundred years as a strange monument to the dead. In 1865, when the rising King's Cross Station needed room to expand, the cemetery in its way was dismantled and the occupants reinterred in a mass grave. Dozens of tombstones were piled together with an ash planted at the center, said to be a monument by Thomas Hardy to those who had been moved. The roots of the ash grew round the stones into a morbidly curious sight. The tree collapsed in 2022. Reopened discussion of its history surfaced photographs of the ash from 1926 without the stones at its base, leaving open the question of whether the Hardy connection was myth or whether the photograph was misidentified. Either way, the tree stood wrapped about the monuments to the dead for at least a century.

Mistletoe of the Ash

Mistletoe is most often paired with oak, but it also grows on certain species of ash. One such mistletoe, Viscum fraxini, has been shown to be effective in the treatment of colon cancer.

Queen of the Forest

Among the symbolic trees the ash is one of the greatest, said by some to have been the first tree ever created, and the wealth of myth gathered around it from every corner of the world testifies to its standing. If the oak is the King of the Forest, the ash is its Queen, tied to the color and element of silver and to such feminine powers as water and the Moon. The goddess Nemesis, daughter of the sea god Oceanus, carried an ash wand as a token of divine justice, while oaths were sworn upon spears of ash wood. The quality of the timber speaks to the tree's meaning, for ash is strong, will not split, and being hard and close-grained takes a fine polish. In the age when wood served where metal later would, ash was shaped into axles so that wheels might turn smoothly, and witches' broomsticks of ash carried their riders swiftly through the air and bound them to the Goddess.

The Norns and the Well of Mimir

The most renowned ash of all, and the one that reveals the deepest meaning of both the tree and the tree archetype, is Yggdrasil. Its roots reach to the very heart of the earth where the Norns dwell, those beings who decide the fates of mortals, and down to the hidden well of Mimir, the source of all the secrets of magickal power and mystical revelation. Its branches climb into the heavens and shelter the whole of the universe, the one unchanging feature of a changing world, and in this the ash becomes a symbol of stability.

Union, Fertility, and the Mother Tree

Because the ash was seen to live at once in the world of spirit and the world of matter, it became a symbol of the union of opposites and therefore of marriage. It is also a tree of fertility, and amulets of ash wood are said to draw love. Where an older ash has grown or split so that a hole forms in its trunk, it was the custom to pass newborns or small children through the opening to secure their protection or to cure their illnesses, a sign of the great tree as both mother-protector and healer. Small offerings of food or coins were pressed into the ground about the roots when this rite was performed. Protective charms were carved from the wood, and garters of ash were worn against the malice of witches. Because its roots take on a peculiarly human shape, much like those of the mandrake, they were used in healing charms, the root standing in for the afflicted person. In the Ogham tree alphabet the ash, or Nuin, is the third letter.
