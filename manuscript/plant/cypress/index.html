Cypress (Cupressus sempervirens) is a tall evergreen tree with slender branches and a statuesque conical shape. It bears small flowers and round, brownish-grey cones or nuts.

Origin & Distribution

Native to the eastern Mediterranean, cypress now grows wild in France, Italy, Corsica, Sardinia, Sicily, Spain, Portugal, North Africa, England, and to a lesser degree the Balkan countries. Cultivation and distillation usually take place in France, Spain, and Morocco.

Related Species

Many other species of cypress are found throughout the world and are used to produce essential oils, including C. lusitanica from Kenya. With regard to oil quality, C. sempervirens is considered superior.

Folk Tradition

Ancient civilizations valued cypress highly as both a medicine and an incense, and Tibetans still use it as a purification incense. It benefits the urinary system and is considered useful where there is excessive loss of fluid, such as heavy perspiration, menstrual loss, and diarrhoea. The cones were described as very drying and binding, good for stopping fluxes of all kinds. Chinese tradition considers the nuts very nutritious, beneficial for the liver and respiratory system, and helpful for checking profuse perspiration.

Magickal Uses

Symbols: Cypress symbolizes death, resurrection, and forgetfulness, and was often used in funerals.

Minoan cult: There was a sacred cypress cult in ancient Minoan culture that celebrated the mysteries of Hercules and his mother Aphrodite.

For consecration: Make a fire of cypress wood and consecrate ritual objects in its smoke.

For protection: Hang up a small bough of cypress for protection.

For lunar and seasonal incenses: Add cypress to incenses used during the waning moon or at the Winter Solstice.

For illumination on death: Carry cypress leaves to become illuminated concerning death in all its aspects.

The Tree of Eternal Life

Like all evergreens the cypress was taken as a symbol of eternal life, a fittingness made plain by the great age of many of its kind, for some have lived beyond five hundred years, and one standing by a Franciscan church near Rimini is said to have been planted by St. Francis of Assisi himself around the year 1200. Across most of the Mediterranean the cypress is the tree of funerals, yet it is also a hopeful sign of incorruptibility and of the resurrection of the soul. Its especially sweet scent deepens its sacred standing, and the fragrance drifting over graveyards was believed to purify the air. For that same purity and incorruptibility its wood is shaped into sacred objects, such as the shaku, the scepter borne by Shinto priests. In ancient Greece the cypress was a tree of the Underworld, and it was said that the first thing a soul met on entering the nether regions was a white cypress standing beside Lethe, the Fountain of Oblivion, whose waters washed away all memory before the soul was born again.

When cypress is gifted it carries the meaning of mourning.
