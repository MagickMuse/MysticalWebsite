Folk Medicine

The common turnip (Brassica rapa subsp. rapa) is rich in lutein for eye health, along with fiber, calcium, vitamin C, and potassium. Its tender greens are nutrient-dense and can be cooked much like kale or other brassicas. The closely related Brassica napus subsp. napus, called turnip or neep or swede in much of the British Isles and Canada and rutabaga in the United States, is similarly packed with fiber, potassium, and calcium, though its greens are not commonly eaten. Both subspecies support heart, teeth, and bone health and carry anti-inflammatory and anticancer properties.

The Brassica Family

The turnip is one branch of a tangled botanical family. The familiar American turnip (Brassica rapa subsp. rapa) is white-skinned with a purple top and white interior flesh, and is closely related to bok choy (Brassica rapa subsp. chinensis) and to oil-crop varieties (Brassica rapa subsp. oleifra). Brassica napus, widely grown for oil as rapeseed, includes the subspecies B. napus subsp. napus, a purple-skinned, yellow-fleshed root vegetable known across Scotland, England, Ireland, and much of Canada as turnip, neep, or swede, and known in the United States as rutabaga. To muddy the waters further, in Germany and parts of Europe the word turnip sometimes refers to what is more commonly called kohlrabi today (Brassica oleracea var. gongylodes).

The Folkloric Turnip

In Scotland, the neep was introduced during the Agricultural Revolution of the 18th century, the very period when folklore, fairy tales, and superstitions were being widely collected and written down. The B. napus subsp. napus, the neep or swede, is the more likely candidate behind the old stories. It grows larger than its pale cousins, its yellow-orange flesh matches the warm glow shown in early lantern images, and it was the more commonly grown root in Ireland, Scotland, and the rest of Europe where so many of these tales originate. The American-style turnip was sometimes grown there only as livestock feed.

The Original Jack-o'-Lantern

Long before pumpkins became the face of Halloween, jack-o'-lanterns were carved from turnips. Pumpkins are native to North America and were not introduced into Ireland until the early 1500s. Samhain, the Irish ancestor of Halloween, and its rituals reach back centuries before that, and among them was the carving of turnip lanterns. The most famous Irish origin story for this practice is the tale of Stingy Jack, who tricked the Devil twice, was refused by both heaven and hell, and was set to wander forever with a single burning coal carried inside a hollowed turnip.

Turnip Lanterns and the Samhain Coal

Turnips are a plentiful fall crop, ripening just as the days shorten, which makes them an excellent vessel for a lantern. As the dark closed in, turnip lanterns were set on doorsteps and windowsills as a guiding light. On Samhain night, when the veil is thinnest and all manner of ghouls and goblins are abroad, the carved lantern serves to ward off would-be intruders, including the Devil and old Stingy Jack himself. Turnips and other large roots were also carved out as a safe vessel for carrying a blessed coal from the Samhain bonfire home to the hearth, securing luck and health through the darkest part of the year.
