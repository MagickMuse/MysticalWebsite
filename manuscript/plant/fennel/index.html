Fennel (Foeniculum vulgare)

Symbolism

Fennel belongs to the element of fire and is sacred to the divinities who rule that element. Prometheus was said to have carried fire down from the Heavens to the Earth hidden in a fennel stalk, and the pith of the plant is held to be fireproof. Its fresh scent and sharp, reviving taste make it a symbol of rejuvenation, and to include fennel in a bouquet is to declare the recipient worthy of praise.

Magickal Uses

Grow fennel around the home for protection and hang it at windows and doors to prevent evil spirits and negative energies from entering. Carry the seeds for personal protection; fennel is one of the most versatile protective herbs, used in mojo bags to ward off harmful energies, spirits, and spells. Place a piece of fennel in the left shoe to prevent wood tick bites during outdoor activities. Add fennel to sachets and mixtures for cleansing spaces and promoting recovery from illness or spiritual attack. Hang fennel above doorways or sprinkle seeds across thresholds to prevent malevolent forces from crossing into the home. On Summer Solstice eve, hang fennel over doors to ward off evil spirits. Fennel is said to confer longevity, strength, and courage upon those who work with it. Stuff keyholes with fennel to prevent ghosts from entering a room; note that if a ghost is already inside when the keyhole is blocked, it may become trapped there.

Magickal Teas

Fennel can be brewed as a magickal tea to support healing, honor or invoke the Horned God, strengthen protection, and aid purification. Drink 1 to 2 cups only during the spell event itself.

Botanical Information

Foeniculum vulgare (Apiaceae / Umbelliferae family). Biennial or perennial herb, also classed as vegetable when the Florence variety is grown for its fleshy bulb. Grows up to 2 metres: a tall, upright herb with finely feathery leaves and clusters of small golden yellow flowers carried in flat umbels.

Distribution

Bitter fennel is native to the Mediterranean region and grows wild across France, Spain, Portugal, and North Africa, where the wild plants yield the "weed" oil. It is cultivated extensively worldwide, the main oil producers being Hungary, Bulgaria, Germany, France, Italy, and India. Sweet fennel is thought to have originated on the island of Malta, introduced by monks or crusaders thousands of years ago, and is now grown principally in France, Italy, and Greece.

Varieties

The two principal commercial varieties are bitter or common fennel (F. vulgare var. amara), slightly taller with less divided leaves and occurring both wild and cultivated, and sweet fennel (F. vulgare var. dulce), always cultivated and also called Roman, garden, or French fennel. Both are closely related to Florence fennel (F. azoricum), a smaller plant with a large cylindrical fleshy root eaten as a vegetable. Other named regional varieties include the German or Saxon, Russian, Indian, and Japanese fennels, each yielding slightly different oils.

Folk Medicine

A herb of ancient medical repute, traditionally believed to convey longevity, courage, and strength. It has been used to ward off evil spirits, strengthen the eyesight, and neutralise poisons. Both western and eastern herbalism prize it for obstructions of the liver, spleen, and gall bladder, and for digestive complaints including colic, indigestion, nausea, and flatulence; for the last reason it has long been an ingredient of children's gripe water. It has traditionally been used for obesity, possibly through a mild oestrogenic action that also increases the milk of nursing mothers. Still current in the British Herbal Pharmacopoeia, where it is used locally for conjunctivitis, blepharitis, and pharyngitis.

Therapeutic Actions

Aperitif, anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, antiseptic, antispasmodic, carminative, depurative, diuretic, emmenagogue, expectorant, galactagogue, laxative, orexigenic, circulatory stimulant, splenic, stomachic, tonic, and vermifuge.

Aphrodisiac Properties

The ancient Egyptians used fennel as a sexual stimulant. Fennel contains plant estrogens that help balance female hormones, which may account for its traditional association with fertility and desire.
