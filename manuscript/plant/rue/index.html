Rue (Ruta graveolens) is the bitter, blue-green Mediterranean shrub of the Rutaceae family long held sacred as a magickal protector and guarded for its toxic essential oil.

Botanical Description

An ornamental, shrubby herb with tough, woody branches, small smooth bluish-green leaves, and greeny-yellow flowers. The whole plant has a strong, aromatic, bitter or acrid scent.

Origin & Distribution

Native to the Mediterranean region. Rue grows wild extensively in Spain, Morocco, Corsica, Sardinia, and Algeria, and is cultivated mainly in France and Spain for its oil, also in Italy and Yugoslavia.

Related Species

There are several different types of rue, including summer rue (Ruta montana), winter rue (Ruta chalepensis), and Sardinian rue (Ruta angustifolia), which are also used to produce essential oils.

Folk Tradition

A favoured remedy of the ancients, especially as an antidote to poison. Rue was seen as a magickal herb by many cultures and as a protection against evil, and was also used for nervous afflictions: it was said to help disorders in the head, nerves, and womb, convulsions and hysteric fits, the colic, weakness of the stomach and bowels, and to resist poison and cure venomous bites. Recorded actions of the oil include antitoxic, antitussive, antiseptic, antispasmodic, diuretic, emmenagogue, insecticidal, nervine, rubefacient, stimulant, tonic, and vermifuge.

Safety

Rue Essential Oil is an oral toxin and an abortifacient and is also a skin and mucous membrane irritant. The oil should not be used at all in aromatherapy. Rue is unsafe for pregnant people or for those actively attempting to conceive.

History

In the pre-Christian world, rue was cultivated around temples and regarded as sacred in ancient Rome. Early Christians, who called it the “herb of grace,” adopted it as a ward against witchcraft, an irony given its deep roots in Pagan tradition. This apotropaic reputation gave rise to another of its names: witchbane. Historically, judges kept sprigs of rue at their benches to ward off disease and contagion brought into courtrooms by prisoners. Rue is also one of the most common ingredients in Four Thieves' Vinegar, a folk preparation used as a health tonic and, in magickal practice, as a ward against attack or to sow discord between enemies.

Ritual Uses

Used in exorcism and purification rites; fresh sprig as aspergillum for salt water.

Infant and Postpartum Protection

Hanging rue in a house where a newborn is present provides magickal and spiritual protection for the child. New parents may also be given sprigs of rue to wear immediately after birth, to protect against the Evil Eye and ward off malevolent spirits during the vulnerable postpartum period.

Magickal Uses

  • Hex-breaking baths: Add to baths to break curses and send back ill workings.
  • Evil eye ward: Worn or ingested historically to avert the evil eye (ingestion not recommended today).
  • Healing and clarity: Place leaves on the brow for headaches; sniff fresh rue to clear the head in love matters.
  • Threshold protection: Hang at doors or add to sachets for home wards; rub fresh leaves on floors to send back ill spells.
  • Circle protection: Mix juice with morning dew and sprinkle a protective circle.
  • Reversing amulet: Carry rue in a reversing mojo bag (such as A Reversing Hand with eucalyptus and salt) to send harmful magick back to its sender.
  • Protective jewelry: Wear fresh or dried rue as an amulet to break the Evil Eye and protect against malevolent spells. A simple sprig pinned to clothing provides powerful protection.
  • Creativity and psychic ability: Rue is associated with sharpening creative thought and enhancing psychic perception, making it a useful ally in workings aimed at opening the mind and heightening second sight.

Additional Notes

Said to grow best when stolen; toads dislike rue. Observe safety cautions for internal use.

Geographic Distribution and Folk Names

Rue grows over much of Asia and Europe. The plant's many folk names reveal its magickal significance and varied associations:

"Mother of the Herbs"

This title indicates rue's importance in the herbal and magickal hierarchy. As "Mother of the Herbs," rue holds a position of authority and primacy among magickal plants.

"Weasel" or "Weasel Snout"

These names indicate rue's affiliation with that magickally powerful trickster animal. The weasel connection links rue to cunning, protection, and the ability to outwit malevolent forces.

"Herb of Grace"

This folk name is intended to indicate that rue has been incorporated into Christian tradition; it is considered among the Virgin Mary's blessed herbs. Rue is used to ward off malicious magick. It's also called "witchbane" because it allegedly keeps witches away.

However, rue's historical association with witchcraft, magick spells, and Pagan ritual predate its associations with Christianity. The Christian appropriation of rue represents a later attempt to redirect the plant's use.

Italian Magickal Traditions

Rue is the primary plant in Italian magickal traditions. It was sacred to Diana, Aradia, and Mars. In Italy and elsewhere, rue is famed for breaking the power of the Evil Eye.

Against the Basilisk

Rue and weasels are among the few able to withstand the basilisk's deadly eye. This legendary ability places rue among the most powerful protective plants, capable of defending against even the most lethal supernatural threats.

Simple Protection

Something as simple as a sprig of rue pinned to one's clothing prevents the Evil Eye as well as many other malevolent spells. This ease of use made rue accessible protection for common people in daily life.

The Cimaruta Amulet

Among the other rue-associated Evil Eye preventatives is the Italian cimaruta amulet. Cimaruta literally means "sprig of rue." It is an amulet formed in the shape of a sprig of rue, and usually made from silver, tin or some silver-colored metal.

The cimaruta is enhanced by small charms that hang from the "fingers of rue." Most are associated with fertility such as:

  • Keys
  • Hearts
  • Fish
  • Crescent moons
  • Horns

This amulet combines rue's protective power with fertility symbolism, creating a powerful talisman.

Clairvoyance and Protective Power

Rue is believed to promote clairvoyance. It was also a primary tool of protective magick. Rue's ability to prevent malevolent magick and return negative spells may be understood to resemble that of powerful witches able to turn back malevolent spells cast by others. Rue's association with restoring and sharpening sight, both physical and metaphysical, appears in John Milton's Paradise Lost, in which the archangel Michael uses rue to clear Adam's vision following the Fall.

The Witch's Power

Any witch powerful enough to break a malicious spell could cast one of her own, if she so chose. Rue possesses this same dual nature, equally capable of defense and offense, protection and curse.

Component of Ancient Curses

Rue was thus a component of ancient curses; the plant strengthening the necessary verbal component. The herb's power amplified the spoken word, making maledictions more potent and effective.

Etymology of the Verb "Rue"

Despite the apparent connection, the verb "rue" (meaning to feel regret or sorrow) and the plant name share no etymological link. The plant derives its name from the Latin ruta, while the verb traces to the Old English hrēow, meaning repentance or sorrow.

Herbal Abortifacient: Antiquity Through the Middle Ages

However, rue's most recognized use from antiquity through the Middle Ages was as an herbal abortifacient. This was rue's primary reputation for centuries, connecting it to women's reproductive autonomy and the regulation of fertility.

Ophelia's Mad Scene in Hamlet

Armed with this knowledge, Ophelia's mad scene in Shakespeare's Hamlet takes on different resonance. When Ophelia distributes herbs in her madness, her references to rue carry significant meaning.

Shakespeare's audience most likely would have understood the reference; this was fairly common knowledge, at the time, not obscure secret wisdom. The association between rue and abortion would have been immediately apparent to Elizabethan audiences, adding layers of meaning to the scene that are often lost on modern readers.

Shadow Reputation

Rue, thus, had a shadow reputation as a dangerous, frequently illicit botanical. This reputation stemmed from:

  • Its use as an abortifacient, which placed it at the center of women's reproductive control
  • Its power in curse work
  • Its association with pre-Christian witchcraft traditions

Despite Christian attempts to sanitize rue as the "Herb of Grace," its darker associations persisted throughout the medieval German magickal practice and beyond.

Midsummer's Eve Herb

Rue was also among the primary Midsummer's Eve herbs. This association with the summer solstice links rue to the height of solar power and the liminal time when the veil between worlds grows thin, reinforcing its connections to magick, divination, and transformation.

The Hour of Gathering

Rue gathered in the first light of morning took on a mystical virtue it lacked at any other hour. Cut at dawn it was the true Herb of Grace, fit for both charms and healing medicine; cut at any other time of day it reverted to ordinary rue, good for nothing but curses. Catholic priests bound it into bundles to sprinkle holy water, trusting it to drive away evil spirits, a protective role rue likely earned through its old repute as an antidote to poison and to the venomous bites of snakes and mad dogs.

Meaning When Gifted

When rue is gifted it carries the meaning of mercy and pity.
