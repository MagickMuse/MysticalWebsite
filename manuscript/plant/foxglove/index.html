Foxglove (Digitalis purpurea) is a tall biennial with pendulous, thimble‑shaped flowers; powerfully protective but dangerously poisonous.

Magickal Properties

Gender: Feminine (source: Cold)

Planet: Venus

Element: Water (source: Earth/Air)

Powers: Protection, Health, Creativity, Imagination

Entities: Astarte/Hathor, Freyja

Magickal Uses

Fairy lore: The markings on foxglove blossoms are said to be where elves have placed their fingers. The plant is sacred to fairies, and cultivating it in the garden invites their presence and protection.

Garden ward: Grown in the garden, foxglove welcomes the fairies and draws their protection, warding off unwelcome intruders and inviting the wisdom of other plants in through the fairy kingdom.

Love work: Under the dominion of Venus, foxglove lends itself to love spells and rituals, especially those concerned with empowerment and self-love.

Omen of true love: To spot a foxglove in the day is taken to mean you will see your true love before nightfall.

Cut flower caution: Bringing foxglove indoors as a cut flower is considered unlucky. It is a poison plant fiercely guarded by unseen forces from the Other World, so work it with care.

Old Welsh charm: Leaves were used to make a black dye to paint crossed lines on cottage stone floors to bar evil from entering.

The Hidden Language of the Bells

A Digitalis purpurea in full bloom is the source of its own fairy lore. Each pendulous bell carries an array of purple mottling, pinks, whites, and near-blacks arranged in patterns that look almost written, as though the fairies had penned secret messages inside the flower for those clear-sighted enough to read them.

Witch's Gloves and Thimbles

Few plants carry a longer string of common names, and the names themselves spell out the powers. Witch's Gloves and Goblin's Gloves describe witches and fairies slipping the little bells onto each finger when cavorting in the woods. Witch's Thimble and Lady's Thimble describe the same flowers worn while sewing magickal garments. Whatever the wearer, the flower is medicine and magick worn at the fingertip.

The Foxes and the Fairy Bells

The names foxglove and foxbell point to an older bond between fairies, foxes, and the plant. Fairies, the stories say, gave foxes the secret of the bells: slip the blossoms onto the paws to move silently into the chicken coop, and ring them softly to send a sound only other foxes can hear, warning the pack when a hunter is afoot. The story is strongest in Norse and Finnish telling. In Finland the fox is a sacred animal, and the aurora borealis is called foxfire, the sun reflecting off the tails of magickal foxes running across the sky. When a foxglove bends in still air, it is bowing to a passing fairy or other-worldly being.

The Legend of Knockgrafton

In Ireland the plant is fairy cap and also lusmore. A poor hunchback in the legend wears a sprig of the fairy cap in his hat at all times and is known by the name Lusmore. He encounters the fairies one night, joins their song in a manner that completes rather than interrupts it, and is rewarded with the lifting of his hump. A neighbour woman comes to learn his secret for her own son Jack. Jack is greedy, impatient, and cantankerous. He breaks into the fairy song rather than singing with it. The fairies do not lift his hump. They drag over Lusmore's discarded one and lay it onto Jack's back, doubling his burden, and he soon dies of it. The story is a warning to keep fairy time and fairy rhythm.

From Folk Cure to Cardiac Medicine

In 1785 William Withering recorded the foxglove as the active herb hidden inside a Shropshire wise-woman's family receipt for dropsy, a remedy of twenty or more herbs long kept secret and known for producing violent vomiting and purging while quietly drawing fluid from the body. Bruised, the herb yielded a juice he made into an ointment for the King's Evil. He went on to prescribe digitalis for asthma, shortness of breath, edema, dropsy, inflammation, ovarian cysts, gout, jaundice, weak pulse, consumption, swelling of the brain, enlarged liver, bladder stones, heart palpitations, paralysis, and a host of other ailments, many tied to heavy drinking or childbirth, with mixed results across leaves and root. The cardiac glycosides he was working with, digitoxin and digoxin, are still used today to treat heart failure and arrhythmia.

The Vegetable Drugs Committee

When drugs and nearly everything else fell short in Britain during the Second World War, a Vegetable Drugs Committee was formed to grow and gather foxglove from the public. Home gardeners, women's institutions, Boy Scouts, and Girl Scouts worked alongside doctors and botanists, raising and harvesting the plants that were then processed into digitoxin.

☠️
DEADLY POISON: Though known for healing properties (digitalis is used in heart medication), foxglove can cause serious problems, even death. Do not take internally. Lethal to cats. All parts of the plant are toxic.

Meaning When Gifted

When foxglove is gifted it carries the meaning of stateliness and youth.
