Source — Specific Uses

Use rowan branches in divining for water (as is done with witch hazel). Believed to protect against lightning. Magickal wands, rods, amulets, and charms are made from rowan wood. Rowan wands are for knowledge, finding metal, and divination. As an oracular tree, it often grows near stone circles.

Sacred to Brigid and the Muses as a symbol of divination and inspiration; also sacred to all fire goddesses — a fire tree because of its red berries, which are considered very magickal.

The Druids made fires of rowan wood and chanted incantations over them before battle to summon spirits. They also used rowan wattles spread on a newly flayed bull's hide as a last resort to force spirits to answer difficult questions.

⚠️ CAUTION: The seeds are POISONOUS — they contain cyanide.

Twigs tied as red-thread crosses carried or worn for protection; rowan planted by homes and sacred sites as warding trees.

Magickal Uses

  • Psychic enhancement: Carry wood; add leaves and berries to divination and second-sight incenses.
  • Healing and success: Add berries or bark to health, power, success, and luck sachets.
  • Protective amulets: Bind two twigs with red thread into a cross and carry for classic European protection.
  • Home and travel wards: Plant near the house; carry aboard ship to avoid storms; keep in the house to guard against lightning.
  • Grave guardianship: Planted on graves to prevent haunting. Rowan trees positioned throughout a cemetery, especially overlooking graves, are believed to serve as active guardians watching over the spirits resting there.

Etymology and Names

The rowan is a small tree closely identified with magic and spirituality in Northern lands. Its English name reveals its magical nature:

  • Related to the Norse "runall" meaning "a charm"
  • Rowan tree may also be understood to mean "rune tree"—rune staves were traditionally carved from its wood

Rowan is also etymologically connected to "alruna," the name given to ancient Germanic prophetesses and magical practitioners. Another nickname for rowan is "witch tree."

Norse Creation

In Norse myth the first woman was shaped from rowan wood, while the first man came from ash.

The Most Potent Rowan

Every rowan carries magickal power, but some trees are prized above others for protection. British tradition favors rowan growing beside standing stones or stone circles. Scandinavian tradition values rowan that sprouts from a cliff, a rock crevice, or another tree (above all another rowan) rather than from the earth. This is called flying rowan and is considered the most powerful of all.

Sacred Use in Northern Traditions

Rowan trees were so deeply embedded in the spiritual fabric of Northern lands that their use couldn't be prevented; instead it was redirected. The tree's sacred status is evident across Celtic and Northern European cultures:

Scottish Highlands

In the Scottish Highlands, use of rowan wood for any other reason but spiritual ritual was forbidden once upon a time. This prohibition underscores the tree's sacred nature and magical power.

Celtic Ritual Use

  • Celts in other regions made black dye for ritual robes from rowan's bark and berries
  • Rowan trees were planted around or near stone circles, marking them as part of sacred landscapes

Wales: Guardian of the Dead

In Wales, rowan trees were planted to guard and protect the deceased, serving as spiritual sentinels over graves and burial grounds.

Cattle Blessing Rituals

Cattle were driven through rowan hoops to:

  • Generate fertility
  • Break any malevolent spells
  • Offer protection

This practice demonstrates rowan's power to both bless and cleanse.

The Witch Tree Paradox

Rowan's most frequent modern magical usage is to prevent witchcraft. Many will tell you that it's called "witch tree" because it prevents witchcraft. In fact, it's more of a case of "it takes one to know one."

Rowan is one of those unusual plants that are simultaneously identified with witchcraft and also allegedly protect against it. Rowan may be understood as possessing the power of a witch so powerful that she can negate all other spells cast.

This dual nature—both witch's tool and witch's bane—makes rowan uniquely powerful in magical practice.

Respectful Harvesting

Like other trees (but even more so), it is important not to harvest any part of it without first asking permission (and giving the tree a chance to refuse), and then offering libations and gifts in return.

The rowan's sacred status demands ritual courtesy and reciprocity from those who would work with it.

Divine Associations

Brigid and Imbolc

Rowan is identified with Brigid and her festival of Imbolc. This connection links the tree to the Celtic goddess of fire, healing, poetry, and smithcraft, and to the early spring festival marking the return of light.

Thor and Virility

Rowan is identified with the Norse deity Thor. As one of his sacred plants, it was believed beneficial for ensuring virility. This association connects rowan to masculine strength, fertility, and the protective power of the thunder god.

Practical and Mundane Uses

Modern Protective Planting

To this day rowan is planted near homes for spiritual protection. This ancient practice continues, with rowan trees serving as guardians against malevolent forces and negative energies.

Dowsing Rods

The finest dowsing rods for locating metal are crafted from rowan. The tree's magical properties extend to divination and the location of hidden treasures or mineral deposits.

Intoxicating Beverages

Rowan also contributes to intoxicating beverages across Celtic lands:

  • Scottish Highlands: The berries were made into wine
  • Wales: The Welsh brew a rowan berry flavored ale
  • Ireland: The Irish have used rowan to flavor mead

These traditional beverages demonstrate rowan's role not only in ritual and protection, but also in celebration and communion.

Lady of the Mountain

The rowan stands for the second letter of the Ogham tree alphabet, Luis. At home in mountainous places and sacred to the Goddess, it is sometimes called the Lady of the Mountain, and also witchbane or witchwood, for rowans were held to guard against witchcraft and malevolent magic; the stake driven through a vampire's heart to kill it for good was meant to be of rowan wood. Yet the tree is said to grow where it is needed, often where witches make their homes, and witches themselves prized it, working its wood, berries, and leaves into spells and charms. Because of its magical power the wood is used for wands and for dowsing rods, and a particularly potent charm binds two twigs with red thread into a small cross, uniting the tree's protection with that of the Cross, hung over doorways and over a baby's cot to keep the infant from being stolen by fairies or other mischievous spirits. The rowan is an emblem of psychic power and a giver of divination: its berries hold an intoxicating quality, and the wine or juice pressed from them was favoured by the Druids in their shamanic work, while groves of rowan were sacred places for oracular rites, the more powerful in the presence of water. Its berries burn a bright blood red, so the tree is a sign of life, lending welcome colour through the Fall and sometimes clinging all winter, as one of its German names, Wintersteher, attests; Irish legend calls them the Berries of Immortality, though in truth they are poisonous to children.
