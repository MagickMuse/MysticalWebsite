Additional Notes

Yew wood scribes are used in rune candle work to memorialize a person or honor the dead. When carving runes for ancestral connection, remembrance, or spirit work, yew's associations with death and the otherworld amplify the working.

Yew is extremely poisonous, contributing to its associations with death and the otherworld. Its wood is hard and unyielding, making it excellent for crafting bows and wands of power. Yew is prominent in witchcraft lore and frequently found in graveyards across Europe, where it serves as guardian between the worlds.

  • Extremely poisonous; avoid ingestion and skin contact.

Everlasting Life and the Churchyard Yew

Among the longest-lived of all trees, some yews are reckoned to have stood for thousands of years, and for this the tree became a symbol of everlasting life. It grows in a strange manner, sending new stems down the outside of the old trunk so that the young is forever born from the old, which lends the yew its meaning of rebirth and regeneration; it even quickens into a fresh growth spurt at around five hundred years of age. So sacred was it that to a pre-Christian people the very ground it grew upon was holy, and to fell a yew was held both immoral and unlawful. This is why so many churchyards hold an ancient yew, the church having been raised on ground already sanctified by the tree, so that the new faith might be grafted onto the old; in time the tree's link with death overlaid its older meaning of life. Its poisonous berries were thought able to carry a soul into the spirit world, and its hollowing heart marked it as a thing belonging in part to a spiritual dimension. In our own day the yew has shown its kinship with life once more through taxol, a compound drawn from the tree and used against cancer. Its tough, enduring wood was shaped into shields and spears, and into the famed English longbow that won the day at Agincourt in 1415 against overwhelming numbers.
