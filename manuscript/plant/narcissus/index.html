Family safety warning. All members of the Amaryllidaceae family, especially the bulbs, have a profound effect on the nervous system, causing paralysis and in some cases death. The bulbs of Narcissus poeticus are more dangerous than those of the daffodil, being powerfully emetic and irritant. Even the scent of the flowers, when present in any quantity in a closed room, can produce headache and vomiting in some people.

A familiar garden flower of the Amaryllidaceae family, up to 50 cm high, with long sword-shaped leaves and very fragrant white flowers having a short yellow trumpet with a crisped red edge. Narcissus is the source of one of perfumery's most prized narcotic-floral absolutes.

Origin & Distribution

Native to the Middle East or the eastern Mediterranean region; naturalized in southern France. Cultivated extensively for its flowers. Only Holland and the Grasse region of France produce the concrete and absolute.

Folk Tradition

The name derives from the Greek narkao, meaning "to be numb," due to its narcotic properties. The Roman perfumers used narcissum, a solid unguent made from narcissus flowers, in the preparation of their elaborate fragrances. In France the flowers were once used for their antispasmodic properties, said to be useful in hysteria and epilepsy.

In India the oil is applied to the body before prayer in temples, along with rose, sandalwood, and jasmine. Arabian tradition recommends the oil as a cure for baldness and as an aphrodisiac.

Other Species

Two main types are produced in France: the cultivated or des plaines variety and the wild or des montagnes type. Narcissus is also closely related to the jonquil (N. jonquilla) and campernella (N. odorus), which are also occasionally used to produce an absolute, as well as to Daffodil (N. pseudo-narcissus).

Related Forms

See Narcissus Oil for the perfumery absolute and concrete produced from the flowers.

The Genus Narcissus

The genus contains more than fifty species of flowering bulbs and an endless number of hybrids, producing a vast array of color combinations and flower sizes. The Daffodil is the most common, its large, bright yellow blooms signaling that spring has arrived. Narcissus jonquilla, called simply jonquil or jonquil daffodils, is a close second and bears smaller double flowers in greater numbers. Paperwhites, frequently referred to as narcissus, are the most fragrant of the bulbs and are commonly forced in pebbles indoors around the holidays. Their name comes from their color and their paper-thin petals. All daffodils and jonquils are narcissus, but not all narcissus are daffodils or jonquils.

Magickal Uses

Love and beauty: Narcissus and daffodils are a powerful ingredient in spells for love and beauty. Exercise caution against going too far.

Self-love and healing: Use in rituals for self-love and healing.

Renewal and fertility: As spring flowers, narcissus are naturally associated with renewal and fertility.

Overcoming obstacles: Narcissus naturalize, returning year after year and spreading on their own. Work with their quality of resilience to overcome obstacles and find your place in the world.

Connection with the dead: The heavy scent carries a sedative quality and assists in the transition to the Underworld. Use narcissus to connect with those who have passed away.

Lore

The name comes from the Greek myth of Narcissus, son of the river god Cephissus and the water nymph Liriope. Warned from childhood never to look upon himself, he was kept away from mirrors and gazing pools. The nymph Echo loved him, was rejected, and faded away until only the whisper of her voice remained. Her dying whisper reached the goddess Nemesis, who avenged Echo by leading Narcissus to a spring where he saw his own reflection. He fell in love with the face staring back, took no food nor drink, and wasted away gazing at himself. Where his body sank into the ground, the narcissus flower bloomed the following spring. In some tellings the flowers spring directly from his corpse. The cup at the center of each flower is said to hold his tears.

A later literary tale recounts a different Prince Narcissus, orphaned and raised by a fairy named Melinette who gave him a ring of invisibility. He fell in love with Princess Potentilla as she sat by a brook weaving a garland of forget-me-nots. A jealous Enchanter conjured a flood of narcissus around her and tried to wed her in her sleep. Narcissus, aided by the fairies, arrived in time to rescue her.

Wreaths of the Fates and Eumenides

The Fates wore wreaths of narcissus. The Greeks twined the white stars of the odorous blossoms among the locks of the Erinyes, deities of revenge also called the Furies. Crowns of narcissus were woven in honor of these vengeful gods and placed upon the heads of the dead.

Cemetery Tradition

In the foothills of California, remote pioneer cemeteries left over from the Gold Rush hide among the pines. On many of these graves, bright yellow daffodils bloom every spring. In much of Celtic fairy mythology, the Otherworld and the Underworld are one and the same.

Persephone and the Underworld

A second Greek myth fixes the narcissus firmly as a flower of the Underworld. Persephone was gathering blossoms in a meadow when she stooped to pick a narcissus, and in that moment the ground tore open and Hades rose to carry her down into his kingdom. The flower suits the tale, for the narcissus is among the first to rise in spring, returning each year as though climbing back out of the hidden depths below. To call a person narcissistic, meanwhile, is to charge them with the vanity of the youth who loved his own reflection, one more meaning folded quietly into the bloom.

The Six-Pointed Flower

The wild narcissus bears six petals, read as the six points of a hexagram or six-pointed star, while the trumpet at its center stands for a seventh point that gathers and embraces the whole.

Safety

The flowers and bulbs are anticarcinogens and antibiotics, and the bulbs contain a growth inhibitor that has put narcissus under study in the management of cancer. The sap contains calcium oxalate, used in folk medicine to heal wounds and also an irritant. The flowers are considered poisonous; narcissus species are toxic and must never be consumed in cooking or teas. Narcissus shortens the life of other cut flowers when arranged with them.

Meaning When Gifted

When narcissus is gifted it carries the meaning of egotism and formality.
