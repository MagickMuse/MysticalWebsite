Hemlock (Conium maculatum) is a deadly plant associated with Saturnine banishing and perilous trance lore.

☠️
POISON: All parts of hemlock are fatally toxic. Do not ingest, burn, or handle. No practical use is safe or recommended.

Historical Lore

Hemlock's most infamous moment in history came in 399 BCE, when Socrates was executed by drinking a cup of hemlock mixed with wine. At the time, forced consumption of hemlock was a standard method of execution for those convicted of crimes in ancient Athens. Socrates had been accused of corrupting the young and of impiety.

The physician Dioscorides recorded that a poultice of hemlock applied topically was used to suppress the male libido, and that when applied to the breast of a young girl, it could interfere with lactation and halt breast development in those not yet sexually mature. These applications reflect an ancient understanding of hemlock as a plant that diminishes and suppresses physical drives.

In ancient Greece, hemlock was sacred to Hecate, goddess of the moon, darkness, and the liminal spaces between worlds. As a protector of witches, her dominion over hemlock helped cement the plant's deep association with the craft. Hemlock was also cultivated in some monastic gardens, where its ability to quell sexual appetite made it attractive to those committed to vows of chastity.

Symbolism

The very word hemlock summons the idea of poison, yet curiously, while it is deadly to humans it does no harm to domesticated animals. It is a pure and simple emblem of death. The phrase to drink hemlock has come down to us as a synonym for taking one's own life, an echo of the death of Socrates, whose final hours were set down by Plato. The poison was also smeared on knives and swords to ready them for killing, the venom heightening the blade's power as an instrument of death.

Witchcraft Associations

Hemlock appears as an ingredient in the traditional witch's flying ointment, a topical preparation historically used to induce altered states. The compound responsible is coniine, a volatile alkaloid present in both water hemlock and poison hemlock. Small amounts applied to the skin are said to produce a sensation resembling weightlessness or gliding through air. This effect, combined with the plant's hallucinogenic properties at near-toxic doses, explains its place in trance and flight lore.

Some folk accounts describe witches using hemlock as chicken feed, intoxicating the birds to make them easier to steal. The practice illustrates how intimately some practitioners knew the plant's effects across different species.

A Plant of Deception

Poison hemlock is one of the deadliest plants on earth and one of the most easily mistaken. Without its umbrella of small white flowers it can pass for wild parsley, parsnip, chervil, or Queen Anne's lace, and even with the flowers the resemblance is dangerously close. The reliable identification mark is the red splotchy mottling along the stem. Folk wisdom advises practitioners to remember the red as the stains of the blood of Socrates when seeking to recognize this plant in the wild. Poison hemlock is a biennial that blooms only every other year, so the flowers cannot be relied on as the sole guide. The crushed leaves give off a strong, foul, mouse-like odor that distinguishes hemlock from the harmless herbs it mimics. Those who do not know to crush and smell the leaves have been killed by sandwiches and salads gathered in good faith. One Scottish tailor died in 1845 after eating a sandwich his children had made from what they took to be an edible herb.

Names and Naming

The ancient Romans called poison hemlock cicuta until the mid-1500s, at which point Cicuta virosa became the designated name for the closely related and equally deadly water hemlock. Linnaeus assigned the current name, Conium maculatum, in 1736. In old flying-ointment formularies, water hemlock was often listed under the cover-name water parsnip. The North American conifer commonly called hemlock is a completely unrelated tree, a source of confusion when sifting through centuries of herbal and witch-craft lore.

The Eight Alkaloids

Hemlock has been used medicinally as a sedative and antispasmodic since the time of the ancient Greeks. It contains at least eight powerful alkaloids that act on the central nervous system, and in minute doses can magnify the effects of morphine. Modern research has also examined its anti-inflammatory potential. The same family of potent alkaloids appears in much weaker concentration in a number of other plants, including the carnivorous cobra lilies of the Sarracenia family.

Hemlock in the Flying Ointment

Hemlock is one of the three most poisonous plants growing freely in Europe, alongside Belladonna and Wolf's Bane, and it appears in the oldest formularies for the witches' flying ointment. In one surviving formula the four named ingredients are persil (parsley), water of aconite, poplar leaves, and soot. Scholars of the witch-cult tradition argue that the persil in this formula is almost certainly hemlock and not the harmless parsley it so closely resembles. Witches kept these recipes closely guarded for both magickal and practical reasons, and the true ingredients were often deliberately shrouded under cover-names that would not give the working away to outsiders.

The Witches' Cauldron of Macbeth

Botanists working with the famous cauldron scene of Macbeth have decoded the grotesque ingredient list as a coded plant inventory, a code that kept both practitioners and playwrights out of trouble. The likely translations are:

  • Eye of newt: mustard seed
  • Toe of frog: buttercup leaves
  • Wool of bat: holly or moss
  • Tongue of dog: hound's tooth (Cynoglossum officinale, also called hound's tongue, once used to treat dog bites)
  • Adder's fork: violet
  • Blind worm's sting: knotweed
  • Lizard's leg: ivy
  • Howlet's wing: Garlic, occasionally ginger

The Wrath of Tisiphone

The poet Ovid describes the punishment of Athamas and his wife Ino, who were targeted by Saturnia, daughter of Saturn, after Athamas gave her offense. In revenge she unleashed Tisiphone, one of the Erinyes, on the couple. Tisiphone descended robed in blood-red cloth, a serpent for her belt and a blood-soaked torch in her hand. She cornered them as they tried to flee and pulled two writhing venomous snakes from her hair, hurling them at Ino and Athamas. The snakes coiled around their victims without biting, stunning them instead with their noxious, smoky breath. Tisiphone then drew out the vials of liquid poison she carried, brewed from plants sprung from Echidna's venom and Cerberus's spit, boiled the mixture with fresh blood, stirred it with a stalk of hemlock, and poured it over the pair. To finish the working she ringed them in a circle of fire drawn from her bloody torch.
