Plant Grimoire

Herbal Allies for Ancestor Veneration

The ultimate source of all energy on this planet is the sun. That's not a metaphor; it's a physical truth with metaphysical implications. The plant kingdom is largely responsible for capturing this energy and making it available to the rest of the food chain through the process of photosynthesis. Animals acquire energy by eating plants, then are eaten by other animals.

Plants are born in the darkness of the earth, which is the realm of the ancestors. But they reach up toward the heavens and call down the divine energy of the sun. They bind the realms together. So it's no wonder that the axis mundi (which stretches from the underworld through the earth and up to the heavens) is often called the world tree. For this reason, plants deserve to be approached with respect. They are elders in this world, and each one understands profound mysteries of nature, including the mysteries of death, rebirth, and connection with the ancestors.

Plants as Complex Personalities

Books about plants and magick will often classify them according to broad categories of influence: this herb is good for love, that one is good for money. But what makes one money herb different from another? And how do some plants, like orris root, end up on lists for seemingly unrelated fields of influence, being prescribed for both finding love and improving divination?

Plants must be understood as complex personalities, just like the people in our lives. No one person is solely identified with one label. A father is also a lover, a musician, an accountant, and on one fateful night, a hero. Plants are the same way. Their qualities are rarely limited to one field of influence. Furthermore, which of those roles a plant is willing to play may vary depending on your personal relationship with it. Plants with whom a particularly strong relationship is developed may do things they won't usually do for others.

Plants are spirits, just like ancestors are. They have their own motives. They bring their own desires to the relationship.

Two Plants in the Same Field Are Not Interchangeable

The way any two plants work within the same field of influence will be different. Two prosperity plants are not necessarily interchangeable when doing work to draw in money.

Fava beans and mint are both plants that fall under the broad heading of money magick, but they are used in very different situations:

  • Fava bean is a charm against famine. It is a hardy plant that can withstand harsh, cold climates and grow in soil that other crops would fail in. Its magickal action is ensuring that no matter what happens, there will always be enough to eat, a powerful promise for anyone dealing with food insecurity. But for someone who needs to raise capital quickly for a business launch, fava may not be particularly helpful.
  • Mint, on the other hand, grows so fast it must be kept in a pot or it will take over an entire garden. The word is synonymous in English with the place where money is created, a large sum of money, and with something newly produced (newly minted) or in pristine original condition (mint condition). Mint seems to know a lot about fast money and mass production, making it a more appropriate ally when running a crowdfunding campaign to bring a product to market.

Working with Plants for Ancestor Veneration

When selecting herbal allies for ancestor veneration practice, consider not only a plant's general correspondences but also:

  • Its relationship to death, the underworld, or the ancestral realm in folklore and tradition
  • Its personal resonance: what does it evoke in you? What memories or sensations does it carry?
  • Its history with your ancestral lineages: what plants did your ancestors cultivate, use medicinally, or consider sacred?
  • The energetic distinction between plants with similar correspondences: what does this specific plant do that another cannot?

As with ancestors themselves, approach plant spirits with offerings, patience, and reciprocity. Build the relationship over time.

Mothers and Daughters

How a plant tends to grow, where it tends to grow, and what idiomatic expressions have developed around it over the years all give hints about the plant's magickal properties. But knowing how a species usually grows doesn't mean every individual plant within that species will grow that way.

Ross Heaven writes in Plant Spirit Shamanism that "in Peru, the mother spirit of a plant is the soul of its species. Each plant... contains its own soul, but it is the combined energy or essence of these leaves that forms the mother." We might extend this logic and call each physical specimen of a species a daughter. When we study plant species in books or from an herbalist, we are learning about the plant mothers. Sometimes daughters look or behave very differently from their mothers, but usually there is at least a little family resemblance.

So while it is important to know about plants, it is equally important to use your senses when harvesting them. You might correctly identify a plant with a strong reputation for healing in the wild, but if it is suffering from an infection, it is best to leave it be.

Historian David McCullough writes: "Insight comes, more often than not, from looking at what's been on the table all along, in front of everybody, rather than from discovering something new." The same is true of much magick as well as working with plants. You need to be willing to really look at a plant.

Paulownia: An Example

Paulownia (also known as empress tree or princess tree) is named for Anna Pavlovna of Russia, a queen consort of the Netherlands. In Japan, it is the emblem of the office of prime minister. The plant mother is strongly associated with power, either as a monarch or an elected official. Paulownia achieves domination by growing taller than surrounding plants and capturing most of the sunlight; shorter plants that also need full sun will die in its shadow, while plants that prefer shade will thrive in its place.

The Susun Weed Practice

Herbalist Susun Weed recommends the following practice for anyone who wishes to get to know a plant as a spiritual ally:

  1. Find the living plant and spend some time with it.
  2. Observe it. Ask it questions.
  3. Write down what you have learned directly from the plant.

The only way to fail this exercise is by writing down something learned from a book rather than from the plant itself. Whatever impression is received is completely valid.

Plants in Ancestor Veneration

The plants most useful for ancestor veneration practice tend to have a reputation for one or more of the following specialties:

  • Cleansing people, items, or spaces of miasma
  • Improving mediumship sessions by increasing psychic sensitivity and clarity
  • Healing spirits of the living and the dead

Most are easy to find. Many are also culinary herbs, so fresh sprigs can often be found at the grocery store, or potted plants can be purchased from a nursery or home improvement store. For dried leaves or flowers, seek a reputable source.

Wild Harvesting

If you prefer to wild harvest the plants used in magickal and devotional practices, make sure you know how to do so safely and ethically. It is often good practice to visit a particular spot over the course of at least one solar cycle before taking any plant matter from there. Many people harvesting plants for spiritual purposes engage in rituals such as:

  • Electing an auspicious moment to harvest
  • Asking whether the plant consents to having part of its body taken
  • Paying the plant with coins, tobacco, or a song

Form Matters

How you work with a plant will depend primarily on its nature. Some plants lose much of their potency when dried. Fresh jasmine, for example, smells heavenly and is strongly associated with psychic abilities; when dried, jasmine flowers have little scent. Some herbs can be taken as tea, like dandelion; others, like motherwort, are usually considered too bitter to drink as tea and are more commonly made into alcohol-based tinctures. Conversely, some herbs like nettle lose much of their nutritional value when brewed into a tincture, so it is better to take them as a tea or a stronger infusion. We honor the plants in part by learning the way in which each one likes to impart its wisdom.
