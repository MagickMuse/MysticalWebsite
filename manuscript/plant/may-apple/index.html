May Apple (Podophyllum peltatum) is a small North American perennial used as the primary substitute for European mandrake in money-drawing and protective work.

⚠️
POISON: All parts of the plant are toxic except the ripe fruit pulp. Do not ingest roots, leaves, or unripe fruit. For magickal use only.

Magickal Properties

Gender: Masculine

Magickal Uses

  • Mandrake substitute: Used as the primary American stand-in for true European mandrake (Mandragora officinarum), particularly in money-drawing spells and protective workings. Carries similar energy at a fraction of the rarity and cost.

What It Is — and What It Isn't

Mayapple is frequently sold or described as "American mandrake," but this is a misnomer. The two plants are entirely unrelated. True mandrake belongs to the Mandragora genus of the nightshade family; mayapple belongs to the barberry family (Berberidaceae). The resemblance ends at the name. Mayapple has a thin, creeping horizontal rhizome with no anthropomorphic shape — it bears no likeness to the famous forked, human-form root that made European mandrake the stuff of legend.

Botanical Description

Mayapple is a low-growing woodland perennial, typically no more than a foot or two tall, with large, deeply lobed leaves that spread like an umbrella. The "duck's foot" folk name refers to the distinctive shape of those leaves. In spring, a single white flower blooms beneath the leaf canopy; by summer it produces a small, lemon-yellow fruit. The ripe fruit pulp is edible, though all other parts of the plant are toxic.

Historical and Medicinal Lore

Mayapple was well known to Indigenous peoples of eastern North America long before European contact. It was used as a vermifuge (to expel intestinal parasites) and as an emetic (to induce vomiting), applications that reflect a sophisticated understanding of its powerful chemical action. The plant was later included in the British Pharmacopoeia, indicating that its medicinal use was formally recognized by European medical authorities.

Modern research has confirmed the potency of its active compound, podophyllotoxin, which has led to the development of pharmaceutical treatments for certain cancers — a reminder that even plants used magickally as stand-ins for legend can carry real and significant power of their own.
