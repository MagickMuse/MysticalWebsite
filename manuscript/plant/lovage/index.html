Lovage (Levisticum officinale) attracts love through fragrant baths and carries a long association with magick, earning it the folk name "the Maggi plant."

History

Legend holds that King Charlemagne was so fond of lovage in the early Middle Ages that he ordered it planted throughout his castle gardens. By the 1700s, root infusions combined with other herbs were used as a folk remedy for inflammation. The plant's nickname, the Maggi plant, reflects its dual associations with love magick and protective magick, and it has been widely cultivated across Europe and the United States for both its flavor and its magickal uses.

Characteristics

A tall perennial herb with culinary and medicinal applications, lovage produces dark green leaves frequently used as a seasoning for meats, soups, and stews.

Botanical Description

A large perennial herb of the Apiaceae family, up to 2 metres high, with a stout hollow stem and dense ornamental foliage. It has a thick fleshy root and greenish-yellow flowers. The whole plant has a strong aromatic scent.

Origin & Distribution

Native to southern Europe and western Asia; naturalized in North America. Cultivated in central and southern Europe, especially in France, Belgium, the former Czechoslovakia, Hungary, the former Yugoslavia, and Germany.

Folk Tradition (Medicinal)

A herb of ancient medical repute, used mainly for digestive complaints, oedema, skin problems, menstrual irregularities, and fever. It was also believed to be good for the sight. The leaf stalks used to be blanched and used as a vegetable or in salads. The root is current in the British Herbal Pharmacopoeia as a specific for flatulent dyspepsia and anorexia.

Other Species

Several related plants are also used to produce essential oils, including sea lovage (Ligusticum scoticum) and alpine lovage (L. mutellina).
