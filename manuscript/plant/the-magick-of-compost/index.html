Plant Grimoire

The Magick of Compost

Composting is one of the simplest and most powerful ways to return energy to the earth. Sometimes called "black gold," compost is decomposed organic matter: the natural result of death feeding new life. You can observe it on any walk through the woods: fallen trees, blanketed leaves, and spent annuals all slowly breaking down and becoming the bed for what comes next.

By composting food scraps and plant matter instead of sending them to a landfill, you participate in this cycle directly. It is death and life in a single process: decay becoming creation. For the magickally minded, compost is a living metaphor and a practical gift to the soil that will nourish your herbs.

When to Begin

The ideal time to start composting is around Samhain (October 31 - November 1), the witch's New Year. The harvest is home, the fields have been reaped, and the season of rest begins. There is a practical reason for this timing as well: organic material takes several months to break down, and starting in late autumn gives microbes enough time to transform it into rich compost by spring.

🌙
Honoring the Goddess

As you bring in the last of your herbs for the season, it is customary to leave an offering to the crone goddess in your garden as a gesture of gratitude and respect. The last apple of the harvest, a bundle of dried herbs, or a beautiful stone or crystal all make fitting gifts to honor the waning year.

What to Compost

  • Fruit and vegetable scraps
  • Coffee grounds and tea leaves
  • Eggshells (crush them to speed decomposition)
  • Dried leaves, grass clippings, and small branches
  • Spent herbs: leaves, flowers, stems, and roots left over from infusions or decoctions
  • Untreated wood ash (in small amounts)

What to Avoid

  • Meat and bones: attract rodents and scavengers
  • Dairy products: slow the process and attract pests
  • Animal waste: can contaminate the soil
  • Diseased plant material
  • Chemically treated wood or paper

Choosing a Site

Pick an outdoor location with good drainage and airflow. Avoid placing your compost under rain spouts or anywhere prone to runoff. Too much moisture will hinder the decomposition process. Think of it the same way you would care for a living plant: it needs air, balanced moisture, and a stable environment.

Compost Structures

There are several ways to contain your compost depending on the volume you expect to produce:

  • Open pile: the simplest option, suitable for small amounts
  • Wire bin: chicken wire stapled to lumber stakes, easy to build and customize in height
  • Tumbler: a commercially built container that is easy to turn, ideal for larger households or communities generating significant plant waste

A single person or small household may not produce enough scraps to justify a tumbler unless yard clippings are abundant. Match the structure to your needs.

Consecrating the Site

Before you begin, clear and consecrate the land. Rake or turn the surface over an area of roughly 3 square feet. Kneel and place your hands on the earth. Close your eyes and project your energy into alignment with the ground beneath you. Set your intention around gratitude and replenishment: the acceptance of both life's bounty and the death that follows. Whisper the following:

"IN DARKNESS WHERE THE SEEDS HAVE SLEPT
THE GODDESS WITH HER SECRETS KEPT
RETURNS, RETURNS, RETURNS, RETURNS

FROM DEATH TO LIFE
FROM DARK TO LIGHT.

SUBSIDE AND FALL, RETURN TO EARTH
AWAIT THE SPRING FOR YOUR REBIRTH
IN PERFECT LOVE AND PERFECT TRUST
THE EARTH HOLDS ALL, THE ASH AND DUST.
FROM WHAT HAS BEEN GROWS WHAT WILL BE
BY ALL THE POWER OF THREE TIMES THREE
AS I WILL, SO MUST IT BE."

Structure & Layering

Compost needs access to air to break down properly. Never store it in an airtight container. Layer your materials in alternating fashion:

  • Brown layer: external material gathered from the environment: dried leaves, chopped branches, and twigs. These release carbon as they decompose.
  • Green layer: internal material from your home: vegetable scraps, food waste, coffee grounds, and crushed eggshells. These provide nitrogen.

Alternate brown and green layers in roughly equal measure. Moisten dry materials with water before adding them. Both carbon and nitrogen are essential to the process.

Letting It Work

Cover your compost with a tarp to retain moisture and prevent it from drying out. Turn or agitate it periodically so the contents stay exposed to air. You can continue adding material over time. The pile will gradually shrink as it breaks down.

Finished compost is unmistakable: it looks like rich, dark earth. A properly managed pile will smell earthy and dank but should never have a rotting odor or attract pests. Depending on volume and conditions, the full process can take anywhere from a few months to a couple of years.

🪱
Vermicomposting

Another option is to use worms to break down your scraps. As they consume vegetable matter, they digest and excrete it as nutrient-rich humus that can go straight into your garden. The process typically takes about six months.

Indoor Composting

If outdoor space is not available, small-scale indoor composting is entirely possible. You will need a plastic or wood container with a snug-fitting lid, holes in the sides for air circulation, and holes in the bottom for drainage. Place it on a tray to catch moisture, and line the bottom with shredded newspaper. Keep the contents moist but not soaking.

A few tips for indoor composting:

  • A well-managed bin will not produce an unpleasant odor
  • Store it in a cool, dark place such as under a kitchen sink
  • Stick to fruit and vegetable scraps, herbs, tea, and coffee grounds. No meat or dairy

Community Composting

If composting at home is not practical, look into local options. Many farmer's markets and community gardens accept vegetable scraps for composting and are happy to keep organic matter out of landfills.
