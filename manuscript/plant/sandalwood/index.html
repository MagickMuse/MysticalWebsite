Sandalwood (Santalum album) is the small evergreen parasitic tree of the Santalaceae family whose dried heartwood and roots yield one of the oldest and most prized perfumery oils in the world.

Characteristics

A small evergreen parasitic tree up to 9 metres high with a brown-grey trunk and many smooth, slender branches. It has leathery leaves and small pinky-purple flowers. The tree must be over thirty years old before it is ready for the production of sandalwood oil.

Origin & Distribution

Native to tropical Asia, especially India, Sri Lanka, Malaysia, Indonesia, and Taiwan. India is the main essential oil producer, and the region of Mysore exports the highest quality oil. Some oil is also distilled in Europe and the USA.

Related Species

The Australian Sandalwood (Santalum spicatum, sometimes Eucarya spicata) produces a very similar oil, but with a dry-bitter top note, and is the principal ethical alternative due to strict export laws on Indian Sandalwood. The so-called West Indian Sandalwood, Amyris (Amyris balsamifera), is a poor substitute and bears no botanical relation to East Indian Sandalwood. Red Sandalwood (Pterocarpus santalinus) is another unrelated species sometimes used in similar magickal contexts.

Folk Tradition

One of the oldest known perfume materials, sandalwood has at least 4000 years of uninterrupted use as a traditional incense, cosmetic, perfume, and embalming material throughout the East. It is also a popular building material, especially for temples. In Chinese medicine it is used to treat stomach ache, vomiting, gonorrhoea, choleraic difficulties, and skin complaints. In the Ayurvedic tradition it is used mainly for urinary and respiratory infections and for acute and chronic diarrhoea. In India, sandalwood is often combined with rose in the famous scent aytar.

The Scent That Draws the Soul to the Gods

The sandalwood is a parasitic tree whose suckers fasten onto others to survive, and its most prized part is the heartwood, all that remains after termites have destroyed the rest, for it is this heartwood that holds the heavenly, ethereal aroma that defines the plant. Sandalwood oil is accordingly costly and is revered by Hindus and Buddhists alike, as well as by practitioners of aromatherapy. Hindus often place a smear of sandalwood paste on the forehead as an emblem of the third eye, and the scent is said to draw the soul closer to the gods. The oil is a chief ingredient in the incenses and perfumes burned in holy places such as temples, and the wood itself goes into the building of those same temples.
