Botanical Information

Lavandula angustifolia (Lamiaceae family). Masculine flowering herb, bush, and leaf originating from Mediterranean and Europe. Known as true lavender, spike, elf leaf, and nard.

History

Among the earliest recorded uses of lavender are those of Roman soldiers, who perfumed their bathwater and laundered their clothing with the wild-growing plant. The name itself derives from the Latin lavare, meaning "to wash." Beyond its cleansing associations, lavender carries an elemental connection to air.

Today lavender is most widely recognized as a fragrance in cosmetics, perfumes, and personal care products. Research supports the folk medicinal belief that lavender essential oil can be useful for treating anxiety and sleep disorders.

Characteristics

Lavender's evergreen leaves display a subtle bluish tint and grow between one and one and three-quarter inches in length. Flowers emerge on spikes measuring one and a half to two and a half inches. The plant requires full sun and well-draining soil to thrive. Its scent is simultaneously relaxing and uplifting, suitable for sleep pillows, aromatherapy blends, and essential oil preparations.

Herbal Lore

The most recognizable aromatic herb in magical practice. Multiple varieties exist (L. officinalis, L. vera, L. angustifolia, L. spika, L. stoechas, L. x intermedia) with differing properties, but all share core magical associations. Most famous for dream pillows, lavender should not be underestimated simply for its popularity and availability.

Historical use spans from ancient evil eye protection to Victorian love magic. In Renaissance times, lavender with rosemary preserved chastity. Victorian flower language translates lavender as "I am devoted to you." Victorian courtesans anointed themselves with lavender perfume to attract customers and announce their profession.

The first widely documented modern healing use traces to an incident in which a distiller, burned during extraction, splashed the fresh essential oil on the wound and observed rapid healing. Roman culture deeply intertwined lavender with love magic.

Ritual Uses

The flowers are widely used in perfumery, sachets, potpourris, and incenses. Egyptians and Romans used them in baths, and lavender remains a core ingredient in purification bath sachets. Added to healing sachets and sleep-inducing incenses, it is equally effective as a linen spray for night relaxation. It is a symbol of cleanliness and virtue; midwives traditionally burned lavender in preparation for childbirth.

For sleep and prophetic dreaming, place flowers in the pillowcase or add a few drops of lavender essential oil to distilled water and spray bed linens. Rosemary can be added to the blend to protect against nightmares. Anoint clothing with flowers or tuck them into drawers to attract love; paper rubbed with lavender becomes a powerful medium for love writing. Scatter lavender about the home to maintain peace and drive off cruelty, or add it to purification baths for cleansing. Worn or carried, it provides traditional protection against the evil eye. Its scent eases grief, lifts depression, and is said to be conducive to long life. Carry lavender to open pathways for spirit communication while maintaining protective boundaries. Place dried flowers beneath the bed of committed partners to encourage loyalty and intimacy.

Warm lavender essential oil in an aroma burner within the birth chamber to calm the mother's fears, welcome the newborn, and draw in gentle fae spirits. Bathing in lavender water just prior to childbirth is said to receive Hekate's blessings, as lavender is among her most sacred plants.

Ancestor Veneration & the Restless Dead

In traditional Neapolitan presepi (Nativity scenes), which feature a cast of up to seventy-two characters drawn largely from Neapolitan folk culture rather than the Bible, the washerwoman figure symbolizes the purification of souls in purgatory. The river she is usually found near recalls the famous rivers of Hades: the Styx, the Acheron, and the Lethe. Lavender thus carries strong associations with refreshment and indulgences offered for the restless dead.

To work with this symbolism: add a lavender tea to the water when washing clothes and pray to the ancestors to cleanse them of any influences causing anxiety or depression, especially the influence of ghosts.

Elves and Psychic Awareness

Lavender is associated with elves (hence the folk name "Elf Leaf") and enhances psychic awareness.

Lavender water is said to sharpen memory while stimulating psychic ability and retention. Bathe the head with it, using a cool compress or any preferred method, to assist in mastering complex occult systems such as tarot or the I Ching.

Security and Safety

Creates atmosphere of emotional safety and physical security. Gentle bug repellent keeps flying insects away outdoors.

Meditation and Centering

Induces meditative states and spiritual focus. Burns as incense to purify religious participants and sacred spaces before ritual work. Lavender's purple blossoms resonate with the crown chakra, making it particularly suited for workings of spiritual attunement and higher consciousness.

Lavandin (Lavandula x intermedia)

Lavandin is the hybrid of true lavender (Lavandula angustifolia) and spike lavender or aspic (Lavandula latifolia), also known as Lavandula hybrida, Lavandula hortensis, or bastard lavender. It is a larger plant than true lavender, with woody stems and flowers that may be blue like true lavender or greyish like aspic. Natural lavandin grows in the mountainous regions of southern France where both parent plants are found wild at different altitudes. It is mainly cultivated in France, but also in Spain, Hungary, Yugoslavia, and Argentina. Common cultivars include Grey Hedge, Silver Grey, and Alba. Lavandin yields more essential oil per harvest than either parent. Because it was unknown when A Modern Herbal was published, it has no long history of therapeutic use of its own; its properties combine those of true lavender and aspic.

Beauty & Skincare

Known as a premier herb for herbal beauty, lavender stimulates at the cellular level and is a key ingredient in preparations for dry or sensitive skin. Its antiseptic properties address acne and skin irritation, while its calming qualities work on the nervous system to reduce stress and restore radiance. Lavender oil is widely used in serums, toners, and facial steams.

Key themes for lunar workings: Love, purification, dreams, peace, longevity, protection, happiness.

Related Species

See Spike Lavender (Lavandula latifolia, the broad-leaved lesser lavender used to produce Spike Lavender Essential Oil) and Lavandin Essential Oil (the hybrid of true lavender and spike lavender) for the other commercial lavender oils.

Botanical Source Detail

True lavender (Lavandula angustifolia) is an evergreen woody shrub of the Lamiaceae family (Labiatae), reaching roughly 1 metre, with pale green narrow linear leaves and flowers borne on blunt spikes in striking violet-blue. The entire plant is intensely aromatic.

Distribution

Indigenous to the Mediterranean region and now cultivated worldwide. Major essential oil production centres include France, Spain, Italy, England, Australia, Tasmania, Yugoslavia, Turkey, Russia, Bulgaria, and Greece.

Other Species

L. angustifolia divides into two subspecies: L. delphinensis and L. fragrans. Synonyms for true lavender include L. vera and L. officinalis, alongside the common names garden lavender and common lavender. French lavender (L. stoechas) is a smaller shrub with dark violet flowers. Spike lavender and lavandin appear in their own entries. So-called cotton lavender (Santolina chamaecyparissus) and sea lavender (Statice caroliniana) belong to entirely different botanical families despite the shared common name.

Folk Medicine

Tradition praises lavender for "comforting the stomach," scenting linen, repelling insects, and serving as a cosmetic water and reviving yet soothing oil. Historic herbalists treated the essential oil and lavender spirit as restorative and tonic, prescribing it for faintness, nervous palpitations, weak giddiness, spasms, and colic. A few drops in a hot footbath were said to relieve fatigue. Applied externally, lavender preparations addressed toothache, neuralgia, sprains, and rheumatism, and in conditions of debility (hysteria, palsy, and similar disorders), lavender was regarded as a powerful stimulant.

Therapeutic Actions

Analgesic, anticonvulsive, antidepressant, antimicrobial, antirheumatic, antiseptic, antispasmodic, antitoxic, carminative, cholagogue, choleretic, cicatrisant, cordial, cytophylactic, deodorant, diuretic, emmenagogue, hypotensive, insecticide, nervine, parasiticide, rubefacient, sedative, stimulant, sudorific, tonic, vermifuge, and vulnerary.
