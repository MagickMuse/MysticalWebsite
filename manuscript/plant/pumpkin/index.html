Magickal Properties

  • Gender: Feminine
  • Powers: Prosperity, Fertility, Protection, Abundance, Health

From a single pumpkin come hundreds of seeds, which is why pumpkins are turned to in spells for prosperity and fertility. Carved into lanterns, they guard the home against the tricks of goblins and guide wayward trick-or-treaters back home. As a magickal food, pumpkin is an emblem of abundance, prosperity, and health.

Pumpkins are the food many people most associate with witchcraft. They are perceived as a magickal food: it is no coincidence that Cinderella's coach was a transformed pumpkin. Pumpkins are emblems of Halloween. Pumpkins, whether left whole or carved into jack o'lanterns symbolize Halloween for many; their image is incorporated into all sorts of Halloween memorabilia.

Pumpkins are the fruit of the Cucurbita gourd and are native to the Western Hemisphere. Although pumpkins may be boiled, baked, roasted or made into pie, 99 percent of all pumpkins sold are now used as jack o'lanterns.

Magickal Uses

A jack o'lantern is a hollowed-out pumpkin that has been carved to resemble a face. Pulp and seeds are removed and may be cooked and eaten afterwards although many people do discard them, only desiring the pumpkin's shell.

Most pumpkins are orange, however some are white. White ones have become popular recently and are used to create "ghostly" jack o'lanterns.

Jack o'lanterns are an Irish tradition and were first carved from turnips. However, Irish immigrants to the United States quickly realized that large round pumpkins were perfect for creating jack o'lanterns and turnips are now rarely used.

African Diaspora Traditions

⚠️ Yoruba religion, Haitian Vodou, and other African Diaspora spiritual traditions are closed practices and should only be engaged by those initiated within the originating lineages. Documented here for educational reference.

Pumpkins are also identified with African Diaspora spiritual and magickal traditions. Enslaved Africans recognized the pumpkins they encountered in the West as a type of gourd, popularly used as containers and magickal spell ingredients in Africa. Pumpkins were thus easily and naturally adapted.

Pumpkins are associated with Oshun, the Yoruba orisha of love, beauty, fertility, and magick. Orange is her sacred color; Oshun is said to cast her magickal spells with pumpkins, and pumpkins are identified as her children.

Taboo: Oshun's devotees or those to whom she has rendered assistance are forbidden to eat pumpkins, especially the seeds. (The injunction also extends to yellow and orange squash.)

In Vodou and other traditions, pumpkins are often hollowed out to serve as magickal lamps for divination and spellcasting. The pumpkin is treated as if similar to a cauldron. The hollowed pumpkins are filled with oil; cotton wicks are floated in the oil and lit.

Folk Medicine

Around the world, pumpkin is used to treat gastrointestinal diseases, aid digestion, and treat intestinal parasites, wounds, heart disease, urinary tract infections, and even cancer. The flowers are used in wound treatment and to address male infertility. Pumpkin also has a place in pet care, treating digestive disorders in dogs and cats and acting as an antiparasitic.

The Cucurbit Family

Pumpkins are cucurbits, the same plant family as squash and gourds, and they are native to the Americas. They store well throughout the winter months, which is part of why they have become a staple in cuisine and folklore around the world.

Gift of the Americas

⚠️ Pumpkins are a gift to the world from Indigenous peoples of the Americas who cultivated them and shared their knowledge with colonists and explorers. The cultural and ceremonial use of pumpkin within Indigenous traditions belongs to those communities.

Pumpkins were introduced to Europe after European contact circa 1492. Like the fast-growing vine itself, they quickly spread around the world, easy to grow and rewarding in yield.

Three Sisters

⚠️ The Three Sisters is a closed Indigenous farming practice. Documented here for educational reference.

Squash, beans, and corn are known as the Three Sisters, an Indigenous companion-planting practice that has produced stable crops for centuries. The squash grows along the ground and shades the roots of the corn, helping it hold moisture and nutrients. The beans vine up the corn, drawing support from the stalk and steadying it against the wind.

Cinderella's Coach

In the most famous of pumpkin tales, a godmother sends Cinderella to fetch the finest pumpkin from the garden. She scoops out the inside, leaving only the rind, strikes it with her wand, and the pumpkin is turned in an instant into a fine coach gilded all over with gold. An heirloom variety of C. maxima carries the name Cinderella pumpkin in honor of the story, deeply ridged like the carriage of the fairy tale. The variety was hybridized and grew popular in Paris in the 1880s, the same era that produced the most familiar telling of the tale.

Jack Pumpkinhead

Among the lesser-known characters of Oz is Jack Pumpkinhead. His form is made of rough sticks fitted together and dressed in ordinary clothes. His head is a pumpkin with a face carved upon it, set on top of a sharp stake that serves as his neck. Jack is active, good-natured, and a general favorite, but his pumpkin head is likely to spoil with age. To secure a good supply of heads, he grows a large field of pumpkins and lives in the middle of it, his house being a huge pumpkin hollowed out. Whenever he needs a new head, he picks a pumpkin, carves a face upon it, sets it on the stake of his neck, and throws the old head away as of no further use.

Jack o'Lantern Origins

Setting candles inside carved, hollowed vegetables, now a Halloween custom, was originally meant to protect against wandering, malicious spirits and ghosts. The practice still works, especially when the jack o'lantern is made with conscious intent. Because pumpkins are the subject of many spells themselves, they are well suited to guarding against malevolent magick. The original Irish jack o'lantern was carved from a turnip.
