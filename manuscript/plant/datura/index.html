Source Book Notes

Planet: Sun · Element: Fire · Basic Powers: Scrying, Visions, Prophecy

Shamanic incantations: Inhaling the fumes is a shamanic aid to incantations and an ingredient in scrying incense.

Romani visions: It is said that the Romani smoked the leaves for visions.

Priests of Apollo: The priests of Apollo at Delphi used datura in a drink to receive prophecies.

Gamblers' clairvoyance: Tradition says gamblers once nibbled the seeds to become clairvoyant and dream about the outcome of a night of gaming.

Alkaloids: This herb contains the same alkaloids as nightshade.

Ancient names: Early Arabian names were datora and tatorsh. Early Sanskrit writings refer to this drug as dhurstura and unmata.

Chinese surgical preparation: Old Chinese herbal medical texts state that if equal amounts of datura and Cannabis sativa were gathered in the seventh and eighth moons of the lunar year, dried in the shade, then pulverized in wine, drinking the resulting preparation would allow surgery and cauterization with little or no pain.

History

Thorn apple originates from the region of the Black Sea and the Caspian Sea, where it likely spread by attaching its spiny, sticky seed pods to animal fur. It does not appear to have been known in Europe prior to the Renaissance, and was brought to the Americas so early by colonists that some botanists initially mistook it for a native plant.

Characteristics

Thorn apple is an annual that thrives in full sun and needs shelter from wind. It is both easy to cultivate and easy to identify: its flowers are white and funnel-shaped, and its fruit, the "apples," are roughly the size of a walnut and densely covered in spines.

Datura (Datura spp.) is a widely distributed family of herbaceous shrubs with fragrant, trumpet-shaped flowers and spiny seedpods.

⚠️ EXTREME DANGER: Datura is deadly poisonous. All parts of the plant are toxic and potentially fatal. Do not use internally or handle without extreme caution.

Botanical Overview

Datura names a widely distributed family of herbaceous shrubs with fragrant, trumpet-shaped flowers and usually spiny seedpods. There are approximately 20 species of datura growing around the world. Common folk names include angel's trumpet, devil's trumpet, devil's herb, horn of plenty, Jimsonweed and thorn apple.

Many datura species have beautiful white flowers that bloom only at night, closing during the day, and thus the plant possesses a profound lunar affiliation. Datura is cultivated for these beautiful flowers and various species remain staples of California's botanical nurseries.

Geographic Distribution

Most species of datura are indigenous to the Western Hemisphere, however several originated in Eurasia, most probably in the region around the Caspian Sea. It is abundant in Russia from the Black Sea regions through Siberia, but thrives everywhere with the exception of very cold regions.

Datura is used ritually wherever it is found, including widespread regions of Africa, China, India, Mexico, and the North American southwest. Remains of seeds and seedpods found within a ritual context in South Texas, together with remains of other psychotropic plants, have been dated back to 2000 BCE.

Magickal and Ritual Uses

Datura is used to:

  • Hex and to break hexes
  • Induce sleep and visionary dreams
  • Communicate with other species, especially birds
  • Enter other realms, especially that of the after-life
  • Sorcery and love magick: Thorn apple has long been used as a sorcerous ingredient and in workings of love magick, its potency and danger lending an edge of mystery and power to any working it enters.
  • Devotional use: It holds sacred associations for worshippers of both Kali and Dionysus, gods of transformation, ecstasy, and dissolution of boundaries.
  • Flying ointment: Thorn apple is one of the traditional ingredients in the witch's flying ointment, used to facilitate astral travel and altered states of consciousness.

Datura is deadly poisonous. Its active ingredient is hyoscyamine, which is similar to atropine but has a more powerful effect on the peripheral nervous system, causing powerful hallucinations.

Thorn Apple in Western Magic

Thorn apple is the form of datura most familiar in Western magic. Thorn apple (Datura stramonium) has been known in Europe since the sixteenth century, apparently introduced by the Romany who are believed to have been instrumental in datura's spread through Europe.

The Notorious Jimsonweed

The most notorious datura may be Jimsonweed, which is a corruption of Jamestown Weed. There are two versions of how this species of datura acquired that name:

Version 1: In 1676, soldiers were sent to Jamestown in the Virginia colony to quell a local rebellion and somehow accidentally ate the plant. This was followed by an 11-day period of delirium. The news spread throughout the colonies and Britain and the name stuck.

Version 2: The plant was named in honor of the first place in the American colonies where datura was deliberately cultivated for medical use.

Historical Medicinal Use

Once upon a time datura was an important medicinal incorporated into treatment for madness, melancholia, and seizure disorders. It's no longer used as such; the toxicity rate was too high and safer replacements have been developed. In 1968, the United States banned the sale of over-the-counter preparations containing datura.

Folk Medicine

Every species of datura carries strong narcotic tropane alkaloids: scopolamine, atropine, daturine, and stramonine. Atropine and scopolamine are used in surgery to reduce saliva production. Atropine treats muscle spasms, scopolamine treats nausea, and daturine treats abdominal and digestive complaints and has been used to address symptoms of Parkinson's disease. Daturine is also used for pain control alongside opioids, a combination known to the ancient Greeks, who paired datura and deadly nightshade with opium poppies as a surgical anesthetic. Many Indigenous peoples have long used datura's pain-relieving medicine within their own traditions. The hallucinogenic alkaloid meteloidine appears in quantity only in some species. Datura stramonium has been used for centuries in Ayurvedic medicine to treat a range of ailments, including asthma. Studies have shown that D. metel carries anti-inflammatory properties, and the alkaloid stramonine is being explored as a treatment for depression.

Brugmansia, the Sister Plant

Brugmansia was originally classified as datura, since the two plants share many characteristics, including flower formation and the same tropane alkaloids that induce hallucinations. The split between the two genera was not made botanically official until the 1970s, and many home gardeners still call brugmansia datura to this day. In mild climates like the San Francisco Bay Area, brugmansia grows into a heralding tree whose massive ivory bells hang down and release an intoxicating scent at twilight.

A Victorian Curiosity

A possibly apocryphal tale holds that Victorian and Edwardian ladies, seated in conservatories filled with exotic plants from across the British Empire, would set their teacups beneath a datura so that the moisture dripping from it would lend strange and rather pleasant sensations. They may have been startled to learn that the effects came from the plant's psychotropic chemicals. Datura is reputed to have caused more deaths by poisoning than any other plant, and an overdose can turn the mental journey into a fatal, physical one.

A Worldwide Sacred Plant

Datura has been used as a sacred and medicinal plant in cultures across Asia, Africa, Europe, and North, Central, and South America. It is used in rituals to access the spirit world and to seek guidance for matters of the head, the heart, and the health of both individuals and communities. It is also used in coming-of-age rituals to mark the transition from one phase of life into the next. Archaeobotanical evidence shows datura in ritual use for at least three thousand years in the American Southwest, and longer still in Mexico, Central America, and South America.

Momoy and the Chumash

⚠️ Chumash sacred plant tradition is a closed practice and should only be engaged by those within Chumash culture and lineage.

Datura is part of the Chumash creation story, in which the plant is personified as an elder woman called Momoy, who transformed into the plant after the great flood. She represents a bridge between the spiritual world and the modern one. Her use is reserved for Chumash shamans, who may enter a trance through her to diagnose disease and commune with the spirit world.

Rain, Dreams, and Protection in the Pueblos and Desert Southwest

⚠️ Zuni, Mohave, and Yuma sacred plant practices are closed traditions and should only be engaged by those within the originating cultures.

Zuni priests use datura in rituals for rain. Datura is also used among the Mohave, Zuni, and Yuma as a dream aid and for protection.

A'neglakya and A'neglakyatsi'tsa

⚠️ Zuni sacred plant tradition is a closed practice and should only be engaged by those within Zuni culture and lineage.

The Zuni hold that datura was once a brother, A'neglakya and A'neglakyatsi'tsa, who lived deep in the Underworld. They often visited the outer world, each wearing a garland of datura flowers, and would roam and observe and then return home to tell their mother all they had seen and heard. One day they met the Divine Ones, who asked how they were. The siblings answered that they were happy, for they had gained knowledge that allowed them to see ghosts, put people to sleep, and spot thieves. The Divine Ones decided they knew too much and banished them back to the Underworld. Datura flowers grew up in the spot where they had stood, and the Divine Ones called the flowers a'ntglakyn after the boy. These flowers bloom white but are also tinged with yellow, blue, and red, the colors of the four cardinal points.

Jamestown Weed

Sixty-seven different tropane alkaloids have been identified in Datura stramonium, also known as Jimson weed or Jamestown weed. The Jamestown name comes from an early colonial mishap, when British soldiers used datura leaves as salad greens and were thrown into delirium and illness. An 1813 letter discussing Jamestown weed records that condemned men of firm character carried it in their pocket to anticipate the guillotine, since it brought on the sleep of death as quietly as fatigue brings the ordinary sleep, without struggle or motion.

The Thorny Apple of Peru

A 1597 herbal called Datura stramonium the Thorny Apple of Peru and described it as having a drowsy and numbing quality, resembling in effect the Mandrake. The same herbal recommended it as a salve for inflammations and burns.

Datura in the Greek Mysteries

Datura was likely one of the plants that produced the visions at the Oracle of Delphi, and it may also have been in Circe's arsenal of plants used to intoxicate and enchant.

At Least Nine Species

There are at least nine recognized species of datura, including D. metel, D. innoxia, D. wrightii, and D. stramonium.

Zombie Cucumber

⚠️ Haitian Vodou is a closed practice and should only be engaged by those within the originating tradition.

In Haiti and elsewhere in the Caribbean, Datura stramonium is called zombie cucumber, for the stupor it can cause on consumption and for its role as one of the key ingredients in the ritual process of zombification.

Not the Wild Cucumber

The name thorn apple, sometimes written thornapple, is given to datura for its thorny flower pods. A species of wild cucumber native to North America also produces thorny fruits and is sometimes called thorn apple, though it belongs to the gourd family and is quite edible. The telltale difference is that one plant grows on a stalk and the other on a vine. When in doubt, leave it out.
