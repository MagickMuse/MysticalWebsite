Mistletoe (Viscum album; Phoradendron spp.) is an all‑purpose classic for protection, love, fertility, health, exorcism, and lightning ward.

History

Revered by the ancient druids for its rare ability to bloom in the depths of winter, mistletoe was considered among the most sacred of herbs. Its role in Yuletide celebrations carries forward to this day in the English custom of kissing beneath a sprig of mistletoe alongside the gathering around a Yule log.

Characteristics

Mistletoe is a parasitic epiphyte, growing only upon other living trees and never in soil. Many distinct varieties exist, and their properties can differ substantially. The berries are toxic and should never be consumed, though the leaves have long appeared in folk medicine for a wide range of purposes, from calming anxiety to supporting cancer care. Mistletoe is identified by its waxy, white berries and small evergreen leaves that range from pale yellow to deep green.

Necromancy

Mistletoe is known as the golden bough, a key believed to unlock the doors between the realms of the living and the dead. Holding a branch of mistletoe during necromantic workings is said to provide inspiration, protection, and success.

Geographic Distribution and Sacred Status

Mistletoe is native to a region stretching from Northern Europe to Northwest Africa and east all the way to Japan. Wherever it is found, mistletoe is considered holy, sacred, powerful, and magickal.

A Plant That Wasn't a Plant

Mistletoe is unique. It was understood as a plant that wasn't a plant, a sort of magickal plant. Mistletoe doesn't grow in Earth; it's a parasite that attaches itself to trees and eventually may kill them. (Identification of mistletoe with witches wasn't always meant positively. Other inferences were also intended.)

Mistletoe's poisonous berries look like tiny golden full moons. In German, these berries are known as "witch's berries."

Sacred to Greeks, Romans, and Celts

Mistletoe was a sacred plant of the Greeks and Romans as well as the Celts. Observers documented Druid rituals involving mistletoe, especially golden mistletoe that grew on sacred oaks.

The Druid Harvest Ritual

Mistletoe was never permitted to touch Earth. Druids harvested it with a golden sickle, and the plant was caught in white linen cloths as it fell. The mistletoe was held to be the seat of life of the oak, and as long as it remained uninjured nothing could kill or even wound the host tree. The harvested mistletoe was then used in rituals of protection and blessing.

Diana and Freya

Mistletoe was sacred to Diana, Queen of Witches, and also to the Germanic goddess Freya. Mistletoe was described as Freya's plant; its presence conferred love and fertility blessings.

The Death of Lord Balder

The sacred Norse myth concerning Lord Balder revolves around mistletoe. The beautiful god died after being struck by mistletoe, the only thing that could harm him.

His mother, the goddess Frigg, desperate to save her son, extracted oaths from everything on Earth not to hurt him: iron, stone, water, fire, plants, trees, animals, diseases, and poisons all swore. But Frigg neglected to ask mistletoe, considering it too small, young, and weak to be of consequence. The trickster Loki discovered this oversight and fashioned a weapon from mistletoe that killed Balder. In the fuller telling the weapon was a spear of mistletoe wood, and Balder's light had shone so brightly that his death plunged the world into winter. The white berries are said to be the tears Frigg wept over her son, and when the Gods at last relented and restored Balder to life, they decreed that mistletoe should never again deal death but bring only love into the world.

Mistletoe on the Ash

Mistletoe is most often paired with the oak, but it will also grow on certain species of Ash. One such mistletoe, Viscum fraxini, has been shown to be an effective treatment against colon cancer.

The Strange Life of the Golden Bough

Mistletoe grows only in the air, never in the ground, and that alone marked it as a plant nearer to the Heavens than to Earth. It is carried from tree to tree by birds, themselves seen as messengers of the Gods: the mistle thrush eats the sticky berries and plants them where they are excreted, and when the birds wipe surplus seeds from their beaks against the bark, those seeds lodge in the crevices and take root. An older belief held that mistletoe sprang up wherever lightning had struck a tree. Though it is most often imagined on the oak, it grows just as readily on old apple trees, among others, and in the bare winter branches its untidy bundles look like birds' nests scribbled into the canopy. Its pearlescent berries were read as drops of life and seed, which is why the plant became a token of fertility.

Modern Christmas Tradition

The romantic Christmas tradition of kissing beneath the mistletoe has ancient roots and is the rare vestige of Pagan tradition acknowledged and embraced by mainstream Christianity. People first hung mistletoe indoors to drive off evil spirits, and the kiss beneath it is a remnant of its old potency as a fertility charm, reinforced by the tiny x, the mark of a kiss, found on the underside of each berry. Even so, mistletoe never settled into the Church as easily as holly and ivy did. It remained an uncomfortable reminder of pre-Christian power and was banned from many churches, even as it was sometimes called lignum sanctae crucis on the strength of a claim that the cross of Christ had been cut from mistletoe wood, an unlikely story to anyone who has handled its fragile stalks.

Meaning When Gifted

When mistletoe is gifted it carries the meaning of "kiss me," of affection, of difficulties, and it is regarded as a sacred plant of India.
