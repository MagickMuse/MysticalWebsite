Hawthorn (Crataegus laevigata) is a thorny tree sacred to rites of spring, famed for both fertility and chastity workings.

History

Throughout western Europe, hawthorn has long been regarded as a tree of magick. It carries associations with the heart, love, and desire, and its flowers are traditionally believed to carry an aphrodisiac fragrance. Medicinally, the flowers, leaves, and berries were used as a tonic supporting circulation and helping to regulate blood pressure -- binding hawthorn's connection to the heart across both the physical and magickal realms.

Characteristics

Hawthorn is a small to medium-sized tree with distinctly thorny branches, capable of reaching heights of 30 feet or more. Its thorns measure approximately an inch in length. The tree blossoms in May, making it a traditional herald of Beltane. Spring flowers give way to the bright red berries that follow, which persist on the branches through winter.

Magickal Properties

Gender: Masculine (source: Hot)

Deities: Cardea, Olwen, Blodeuwedd, Hecate (+ Flora, Hymen)

Powers: Protection, Fertility, Chastity, Fishing Magic, Happiness

Ritual Uses

Hawthorn was once used to decorate May poles and is entwined with Witchcraft lore and fairy faith.

Magickal Uses

  • Fertility and weddings: Incorporated into spring weddings to increase fertility.
  • Chastity: Leaves placed beneath the mattress or around the bedroom to enforce chastity or celibacy.
  • Fishing luck: Carried on a fishing trip to ensure a good catch.
  • Joy and protection: Worn or carried to lift sadness and promote happiness; protects against lightning and bars ghosts from the home. Romans placed hawthorn in cradles to guard infants.
  • Fairy lore: Sacred to the faeries and part of Britain's "Oak, Ash and Thorn" triad. When hawthorn grows alongside oak and ash, the site is considered sacred ground where faeries are known to gather.
  • Beltane rites: Hawthorn played a central role in Beltane observances. To sleep beneath a hawthorn tree at Beltane was traditionally believed to open the way into the faerie realm.
  • Portals and wishes: Hawthorn is associated with thresholds, doorways to magickal realms, and the granting of wishes. Petitions written on ribbons and tied to the branches were believed to be carried by the wind into the faerie realm, where its inhabitants might choose to intercede on behalf of the petitioner.
  • Love spells: The bark and berries of the hawthorn are traditional ingredients in love spells and workings of the heart.
  • Word origin: The word "haw" is an old term for hedge.

Source Book Notes

  • May Queen and Goddess of Flowers: Hawthorn is sacred to the May Queen and the Goddess of Flowers.
  • Symbol of chastity: Also a symbol of chastity.
  • Temple cleaning: All temples were cleaned out in May and the sacred images ritually washed and decorated.
  • Hawthorn wand: The Goddess is supposed to have carried a hawthorn wand to work spells; some witches also use this wood for wands of power.
  • Fairy tree: Known as the fairy tree — it blooms only around the first of May.
  • Unlucky indoors: The flowers are white and fragrant but it is unlucky to cut them and bring them into the house.
  • Boundary hedge: This tree has been planted as boundary hedge in Britain since at least 1750.
  • Fermented berries: In autumn the berries ripen and fall; they ferment on the ground and birds that eat them can act drunk.
  • Erotic scent: The blossoms have a strong scent said to be erotic to men, but the scent repels bees.
  • Symbol of white magick.

Fairy Warning

⚠️
According to Celtic tradition, hawthorn is one wood that may not be cut without dire consequences. The species belongs to the fairies, who feel very protectively towards it. Unless you are an experienced practitioner of fairy magic, find a fallen branch or choose a different wood.

Additional Notes

Hawthorn wood is excellent for wand-making. Hawthorn enhances creativity and Witchery skills in magickal practice.

The May Tree and the Glastonbury Thorn

The hawthorn, or May tree, is a sacred and holy tree wherever it stands, and in the Celtic Ogham alphabet it carries the letter H, or Huath. It is as useful as it is holy: cut back into hedgerows its thorns sharpen into a fine barrier for livestock, its berries feed the birds and make syrups, preserves, and wines, and its nourishing leaves earned it the nickname of the bread and cheese tree. Tolerant of other plants growing close about it, the hawthorn encourages a wealth of life around it. Its standing as a tree of protection owes to more than its thorns, for there was an old belief that it guarded against fire as well. Each year a woven hawthorn globe was brought into the house as insurance against fire of any kind, and the next year it was replaced, the old one burned and its ashes scattered on the fields to ensure a healthy harvest.

The white flowers of the May blossom open in late spring, each with five petals and five matching sepals set like a star, so that, like all of the rose family to which it belongs, the bloom is a natural pentagram. Heavy with an almost narcotic scent, the flowers open at Beltane when the sap rises in plant and beast alike, marking the hawthorn as a tree of fertility and sensuality and a natural focus for the fertility rites of the season. Though it stands for frivolity and mirth, a contrasting legend clings to it. Joseph of Arimathea, resting on Wearyall Hill in Glastonbury, leaned on his staff and pressed it into the ground, where it took root and sprouted into a thorn tree, and a Christian chapel rose upon the spot. This holy tree, the Glastonbury Thorn, is said to blossom at Christmas, and a sprig is sent to the Queen each year, though the tree that stands now is only a distant descendant of its legendary parent.
