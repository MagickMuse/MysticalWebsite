Source — Specific Uses

The tincture of the capsule is known as laudanum. The capsule and flowers contain opium, a strong, addictive hypnotic and sedative; also a pain reliever and antispasmodic. Overdoses can be fatal. Best used in charm bags for prosperity and abundance.

Magickal Properties

Powers: Fertility, Love, Sleep, Money, Luck, Invisibility

Ritual Uses

Pods used for dream questions; gilded heads worn for wealth.

Morpheus and the Sleep of Ceres

Morpheus, the Greek god of sleep, counts the poppy's opiates among his attributes, as does Demeter in her double guise as goddess of the harvest and of death. One story tells that the god of sleep made the poppy for Ceres when grief for her lost daughter Persephone left her too sleepless to make the corn grow; once sleep had restored her, the fields flourished again, and to this day it is counted good luck to see poppies growing in a cornfield.

A Flower of Remembrance and Grief

The poppy became the flower of remembrance for those who died in the two World Wars, and around Remembrance Day in November paper poppies are sold to aid bereaved families, the flower chosen because it grew in such profusion in the fields of Flanders. The bond between the poppy and grief for fallen warriors is far older, though: in the Iliad, written in the eighth century BC, a dying warrior is likened to a drooping poppy.

Leaves Beneath the Pillow

The leaves of the poppy serve as a tool of divination; placed beneath the pillow, they are said to give the sleeper dreams of what is to come.

Magickal Uses

  • Sleep and dreams: Use seeds and flowers in sleep mixtures; ask a written question placed in a seed pod and sleep upon it to dream the answer.
  • Funerary rest: Wild poppy seeds scattered throughout a cemetery encourage the dead to sleep peacefully and deeply, drawing on the poppy's deep associations with sleep and the underworld.
  • Fertility and luck: Eat or carry seeds to promote fertility and attract luck and money.
  • Love: Add seeds to food or sachets to induce love.
  • Invisibility charm: Soak seeds in wine for fifteen days; drink daily for five days while fasting (traditional lore).

Additional Notes

Use legally obtainable poppy only; observe all local laws and safety.

Meaning When Gifted

When a poppy is gifted it carries the meaning of eternal sleep, oblivion, and imagination.

  • Red Poppy: Pleasure
  • White Poppy: Consolation
  • Yellow Poppy: Wealth and success

⚠️ LEGAL CAUTION: Opium poppies (Papaver somniferum) are potentially poisonous and, depending where you're located, almost definitely illegal. Attempting to order them from seed catalogs may attract unwanted attention from law enforcement, even if your intentions are purely ritual or magickal.

Species and Morphine Content

There are many species of poppies, however only two contain morphine in any significant amount: the opium poppy and the wild setaceous poppy (which may be the root ancestor species of all poppies). Opium poppies were perceived as the most powerful and magickal of the species for obvious reasons.

Magickal Substitutes

When discussing historical medicinal use, only true opium poppy (Papaver somniferum) is indicated. However, when discussing ritual or magickal use, any of the many other poppy species may substitute:

  • Vivid red corn poppies are the most popular substitute
  • They were also dedicated to Demeter (as are opium poppies) and serve similar magickal function
  • Papaver strigosum or "witch poppy" is another red substitute

Countless Seeds and Fertility

Poppies contain a tremendous amount of seeds; they are literally countless. Because of this, poppies are among the flowering plants most associated with human fertility. They are the floral equivalent of a pomegranate. As such, they were associated with the generative powers of the Earth and sacred to powerful goddesses.

Ancient Use as Aphrodisiac, Intoxicant, and Medicine

Opium has been used as an aphrodisiac and intoxicant since the Stone Age, however ancient forms of usage were different than those of modern times. The ancients didn't have the technical capacity to isolate chemical constituents as is done today. There was little concept of "recreational drugs." Instead opium poppies were understood as unique, sacred, both beneficial and dangerous and as packed with magick power as with seeds.

Cultivation History

  • Poppies were cultivated by European Neolithic cave dwellers
  • They traveled from Northern Europe to the Mediterranean during the later Neolithic period, following the amber trade route
  • By the Bronze Age, opium poppies were well distributed throughout the Eastern Hemisphere and used medicinally, as a food source, and as an intoxicant

Helen of Troy's Nepenthe

The most ancient form of use was an infusion, either opium poppy-infused water or wine. Helen of Troy's elixir of forgetfulness, nepenthe, is believed to have contained opium as well as those other witches' plants, mandrake and henbane.

Sacred to Demeter and the Eleusinian Mysteries

Poppies are sacred to Demeter and closely identified with her most sacred rituals, the Eleusinian Mysteries. It is believed, although no longer verifiable, that opium played a part in the mysteries. The Greater Mysteries, held in early autumn, lasted nine days, commencing with preliminary ritual bathing and culminating with a night of sacred revelations within Demeter's temple at Eleusis.

Initiates literally drank the kykeon, a potent beverage that may have been psychotropic. Theories of its composition include ergot fungus (from which LSD derives) or opium poppies, or perhaps both, blended with wine, barley, and mint. Those initiated into the mysteries, who experienced the kykeon and the visions it provided, were reputedly changed forever. They no longer feared death and now knew the secret of a happy afterlife.

Goddess Associations Across Cultures

Hathor and Isis (Egypt)

  • Hathor is depicted wearing a necklace of opium pods
  • Isis is shown holding poppies in her hand

Baba Yaga (Slavic Tradition)

Baba Yaga's home is surrounded by a fence topped with skulls. Within the fence grows a wild profusion of plants sacred to her, including poppies. Poppy seeds are considered among her most sacred, and she is petitioned with offerings of bread topped with seeds.

Nyx and Thanatos (Greek Tradition)

  • Nyx, primordial spirit of night, carries a bouquet of poppies
  • Thanatos, Lord of Death, wears a garland of poppies on his head

Hermes and Mekone

Hermes makes his home in Mekone, which literally translates as "poppy town." This is the city where he tricked Zeus into accepting bones and fat as offerings rather than meat.

Asklepios: Dreams and Healing

Asklepios, spirit of healing, used poppy extract to induce the sacred healing dreams sent to those who slept within his temple precincts. These dreams provided diagnostic information and sometimes even miraculous cures.

The Witch's Flying Ointment

Poppy is among the plants cited in historical descriptions of the witch's flying ointment, documented in sources from the 1500s and 1600s. Its inclusion alongside hemlock, belladonna, henbane, mandrake, monkshood, and thorn apple reflects the poppy's long association with altered consciousness, sleep, and the boundary between waking and dreaming states. The sedative and hallucinogenic properties of opium made it a natural candidate for preparations intended to produce visions and sensations of flight or spirit travel.

The connection is echoed in older lore as well: Helen of Troy's nepenthe, the elixir of forgetfulness described in the Odyssey, is believed to have contained opium alongside mandrake and henbane — the same trio that appears in flying ointment accounts.

Poppy Fields of World War I

Following World War I, which devastated European battlefields, the first plants observed blooming amidst the devastation were vivid red poppies. This inspired the custom of selling artificial poppies to benefit veterans. Many Christmas cards from the World War I era feature borders of red poppies.

The fields of red poppies in The Wizard of Oz evoke the poppy fields of World War I, in which the characters fall into enchanted sleep.

Etymology: Somniferum and Somnus

The opium poppy's botanical name, Papaver somniferum, derives from Somnus, the Roman Lord of Sleep. The name reflects the plant's primary historical use and magickal property: the induction of sleep and dreams.

The Discovery of Morphine (1803)

In 1803, a German pharmacist named Friedrich Sertürner successfully isolated morphine from opium. This was a groundbreaking achievement: morphine was the very first alkaloid ever isolated from any plant. This discovery revolutionized both pharmacy and chemistry, opening the door to the isolation of countless other plant alkaloids and the development of modern pharmaceutical science.

The True Meaning of "Narcotic"

The term "narcotic" is frequently misused in modern parlance. Technically, a narcotic is anything that induces sleep or stupor. The word derives from the Greek narkoun, meaning "to benumb." Opium is the quintessential narcotic in this original sense—it was used for millennia specifically to induce healing sleep, relieve pain, and provide respite from suffering.

Modern misuse of the term to mean "illegal drug" obscures the plant's ancient and legitimate role as a sacred medicine and dream inducer in magickal and healing practices across cultures.

Cultivation History

Opium has extremely ancient associations with human beings. Although its origins remain shrouded in mystery, opium poppies have been cultivated since that old time immemorial; no wild population exists. Some poppies may escape from a field and wander; abandoned poppy fields will thrive; but basically opium poppies live where people plant them.
