Garlic (Allium sativum) is a pungent bulb with powerful culinary and magickal properties, known for its strong protective qualities.

History

Garlic stands among the oldest recorded medicinal and culinary plants, with evidence of its use reaching back to the earliest periods of human history. The bulbs, along with the stems and leaves (known as scapes), have been cited in more than 125 distinct treatments across cultures and eras.

Characteristics

A member of the lily family, garlic is cultivated primarily for its bulbs, which have been applied to a wide range of ailments from blood pressure problems to digestive complaints. The most potent medicinal benefits are obtained when the bulbs are consumed fresh or preserved through freeze-drying in enteric-coated capsules, which protect the active compounds through digestion.

The Two Garlics

Two Allium species sit at the heart of garlic lore. Allium sativum is the large, segmented bulb most familiar from kitchens and from cinema wreaths. Its native range runs across south and central Asia and reaches into northeastern Iran. Allium ursinum, the wild garlic, is also known as ramsons, Buck Rams, or Buck Rampe. It is broad-leaved, grows on moist meadow banks, smells of onion when crushed or bruised, and bears clusters of pearly white blossoms with sharp petals. Despite the wreathed bulbs in our movie imagination, it is the wild ramsons that turn up most often in the oldest vampire wards, a sign of how ancient the practice is. Allium species and their counterparts also grow throughout the Americas.

The Spear and the Plant

The English word garlic likely derives from the Anglo-Saxon gar, meaning spear, joined to le'ac, meaning plant. The naming honors the spear-like leaf and points to the older sense of garlic as a piercing, warding thing, a planted weapon set at the threshold against what would do harm.

Garlic Against the Vampire

No plant is more bound to vampire lore than garlic. The reputation runs back further than the gothic novel that fixed the image in modern minds. The classic working uses the wild ramsons rather than the cultivated bulb. The fresh flowering tops are rubbed across every opening of a room. Windows are latched, then the sashes are rubbed so that every breath of air entering carries the scent. The door jamb is treated above, below, and at each side. The fireplace surround receives the same. A wreath is woven of the flowers or cloves and placed at the throat of the threatened sleeper. The wreath stays on through the night. The window stays shut until morning. Silence is part of the rite, and obedience to the seal is part of the silence. See Garlic Wreath at the Sickbed.

Mars and the Spear-Leaf

Garlic is ruled by Mars. It is the planted weapon, a botanical spear, a plant whose work is forceful and direct. This martial rulership underlies its use against active threats, its association with soldiers who carried cloves into the field, and its modern reputation as an aphrodisiac of the lust-rousing rather than love-binding variety. Garlic does not soften the heart. It heats and arms.

A Family of Wardens

Garlic does not work alone. Its near relatives, onion, leek, chive, and shallot, share its protective and discouraging quality. Planted along the borders of garden beds, the Allium family discourages insects in the literal sense and also serves a magickal purpose, warding the garden as it wards the kitchen. A magickal garden of any size benefits from a generous border of Allium.

Fire Cider

Fire cider is an old folk medicine experiencing a revival. The vinegar is made by steeping a great many cloves of garlic together with onion, horseradish, ginger, and cayenne in apple cider vinegar for several weeks. Honey is added when the steep is strained. Taken by the teaspoon at the first sign of a cold or flu, it sharpens immune response, opens the sinuses, and is widely regarded as a domestic ward against winter illness. The recipe is closely tied to garlic's reputation for warding off germs as much as ghosts. See Fire Cider.

The Welsh Leek

The leek, Allium ampeloprasum, is the close cultivated cousin of garlic and a plant of unlikely grandeur. We picture soup before we picture knighthood, but the leek is the emblem of Wales, sacred to Saint David, and worn as a badge of high office in Welsh chivalric tradition such as the Knights of the Leek. The same Allium force that wards the bedroom also marks the standing of the wearer.

Healing and the Kitchen

Garlic stands among the most enduring kitchen medicines on the planet. A clove swallowed weekly through the cold months, a fine mince steeped in warm water and taken to clear a sinus infection, a daily clove added to the cooking pot, all are traditional household applications. Modern research confirms the older sense, noting reductions in total cholesterol and in LDL cholesterol with regular intake, and pointing to lowered risk of gastrointestinal and other cancers across the broader Allium family of onion, leek, and chive.

Medicinal Uses

Garlic is one of the oldest documented medicinal plants on earth, with evidence of its use in healing stretching back thousands of years across nearly every major culture. It is classified as an alterative herb, meaning it supports the body's ongoing maintenance and filtration functions rather than targeting a specific condition. Its primary medicinal properties are antimicrobial, cardiovascular, and immune-supporting. Allicin, the compound responsible for garlic's characteristic smell, is a potent natural antimicrobial active against a range of bacteria and fungi. Regular consumption has also been associated with improved circulation, reduced blood pressure, and lower cholesterol over time. As an alterative, garlic works best as a consistent presence in the diet rather than a short-course remedy.

Folk Tradition

The Talmud holds that garlic increases the sexual potency of the man. Yet during the Greek festivals sacred to Athena and Demeter, women ate garlic so that the vow of abstinence was easier to keep, perhaps owing to the plant's pungency. Like its relative the onion, garlic was believed to ward off snakes, a belief likely rooted in its genuine power against infection. Shepherds in the Carpathian mountains still rub their hands with garlic before milking their ewes, a symbolic rite grounded in fact, since the plant's antibacterial action checks the spread of disease. In Borneo, garlic was held to have the power to recover lost souls.

Botanical Description

Garlic is a strongly scented perennial herb up to 1.2 metres high, with long, flat, firm leaves and whitish flowering stems. The bulb is made up of several cloves pressed together within a thin white skin.

Origin & Distribution

Garlic is said to have originated in south-west Siberia and then spread to Europe and Central Asia. It is naturalized in North America and cultivated worldwide. Major oil-producing countries include Egypt, Bulgaria, France, China, Germany, and Japan.

Related Species

Closely related to the wild or wood garlic (Allium ursinum), also known as 'ramsons'. There are also many other wild species with similar but less pronounced properties.

Documented Medicinal Tradition

Garlic has been used for thousands of years for respiratory and urinary tract infections; digestive disorders and infestations; skin eruptions; heart disease, high blood pressure, and arteriosclerosis; as well as epidemics and fever. It was used in the First World War to prevent gangrene and sepsis. In China it is applied to diarrhoea, dysentery, tuberculosis, diphtheria, hepatitis, ringworm, typhoid, and trachoma, among others. It is listed as specific in the British Herbal Pharmacopoeia for chronic bronchitis, and its properties have been attested by modern experimental and clinical research.
