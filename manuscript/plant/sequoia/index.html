Sequoia (Sequoiadendron giganteum; coast redwood Sequoia sempervirens) names the two redwoods, the largest and tallest trees in the world. The greatest giant sequoias are given names of their own, such as General Grant and General Sherman, and are the biggest single living organisms on Earth; General Sherman, the outright champion, weighs some 1,500 tons, holds a volume of 55,040 cubic feet, and rises above 274 feet, while the tallest coast redwoods, more graceful than the grizzled giants, have reached 400 feet.

Folk Tradition

Inevitably these trees have become symbols of longevity, though not of survival, for the coast redwood was logged so heavily (it is said San Francisco was built from it) that, although now protected, little more than three per cent remains of the original forest that once spread five hundred miles from Monterey to Oregon. No single culture has claimed the redwoods as the epitome of the World Tree, yet they are likely candidates for that sacred standing: so tall their crowns are sometimes lost in cloud, the upper reaches of the coast redwood cannot draw water from the soil and live instead by drinking the sea fogs rolling off the Pacific. They are nearly impossible to photograph whole, so that usually we see only their feet, with a small human figure dwarfed for scale. In Britain the giant sequoia was named Wellingtonia for the first Duke of Wellington, Britain's most famous soldier, and the Americans, unimpressed by the attempt to claim their greatest tree, answered by naming it Washingtonia for their first president.
