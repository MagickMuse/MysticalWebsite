The essential oil is among the most hazardous in the trade and should not be used on skin or in home aromatherapy.

Cassia (Cinnamomum cassia) is a close relative of true cinnamon, often sold under the cinnamon name in the United States. Its bark carries a stronger, more pungent flavor than Ceylon cinnamon.

Characteristics

A slender evergreen tree growing up to 20 metres high in the wild, with leathery dark-green leaves and small white flowers. In commercial production it is usually cut back hard each year so that it grows as a multi-stemmed bush, easier for harvesting bark, leaves, and twigs.

Origin & Distribution

Native to the southeastern provinces of China. Found to a lesser extent across Vietnam and southern India (Cochin), where it is also cultivated for spice and oil production.

Related Species

Cassia is closely related to but distinct from Cinnamon (C. verum, also called Ceylon Cinnamon), which produces a sweeter, more delicate bark. Both belong to the Lauraceae family. Several other regional Cinnamomum varieties are also distilled commercially for essential oil. Cassia is the harsher, hotter of the two and the one most often substituted in American grocery cinnamon.

Folk Medicine

Cassia bark has been used medicinally in much the same way as Ceylon cinnamon, mainly for digestive complaints: flatulent dyspepsia, colic, diarrhoea, and nausea. It is also reached for in cases of common cold, rheumatism, kidney complaints, and reproductive trouble. The powdered bark is current in the British Herbal Pharmacopoeia as a specific remedy for flatulent dyspepsia or colic with nausea.

Cassia is used extensively as a domestic spice in Asia.

Magickal Uses

Cassia shares most of the magickal properties of cinnamon but with a more intense energy due to its stronger aromatic profile.

For spirituality: Burned to raise spiritual vibrations and connect with higher realms.

For success: Added to incenses and sachets to attract success in all undertakings.

For protection and healing: The dried bark was often burned in temples and private homes for protection and healing. It can be used as a substitute for cinnamon in perfumery, potpourris, and incense.

For spirit work: The warming, stimulating scent is believed to attract spiritual entities and heighten psychic awareness.

Substitution

Cassia and cinnamon can generally be used interchangeably in magickal work, though cassia has a more intense energy.

The Tree of Life

To the Chinese the cassia is the sacred Tree of Life, the World Tree that grows everlasting in paradise, and it is said that whoever eats its fruit becomes immortal. That paradise is placed high in the mountains of Tibet, at the very source of the Yellow River.
