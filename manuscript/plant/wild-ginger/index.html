Wild Ginger (Asarum canadense), also called Snakeroot or Indian Ginger, is a low-growing North American woodland herb of the Aristolochiaceae family valued in folk medicine and as the botanical source of a warm, gingerlike essential oil.

Botanical Description

An inconspicuous but fragrant little plant standing no more than 35 cm high, with a hairy stem and two glossy, kidney-shaped leaves rising from a creeping rootstock. The solitary bell-shaped flower is brownish purple on the outside and creamy white within.

Origin & Distribution

Native to North America, especially North Carolina, Kansas, and Canada. The essential oil is produced in the USA mainly from wild-growing plants.

Related Species

Wild Ginger should not be confused with the so-called Virginian Snakeroot, Serpentaria (Aristolochia serpentaria), which belongs to the same botanical family but contains asarone and is considered toxic. The common name Snakeroot is also applied in other plant families to Snakeroot (Polygala senega), an unrelated North American medicinal herb.

Folk Tradition

Wild Ginger has been employed for centuries in folk medicine, though it is now little prescribed. It was traditionally used for chronic chest complaints, dropsy, rheumatism, and painful bowel and stomach spasms. It was also regarded as a valuable stimulant in cases of amenorrhoea and colds, and was taken to promote a copious perspiration. The Snakeroot name, at least for the Virginian variety, derives from its use in helping the body combat nettle rash, poison ivy, and some snake bites. Recorded actions include anti-inflammatory, antispasmodic, carminative, diuretic, diaphoretic, emmenagogue, expectorant, febrifuge, stimulant, and stomachic.

Safety

The essential oil distilled from the dried rhizomes and roots is non-toxic, non-irritant, and non-sensitizing, but should be avoided during pregnancy.
