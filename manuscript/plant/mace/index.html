Mace (Myristica fragrans) sharpens the second sight and quickens the mind. It belongs to the Myristicaceae family.

Characteristics

Mace is the bright red, netlike aril or husk that surrounds the nutmeg shell and seed inside the fleshy fruit of the nutmeg tree, an evergreen reaching up to 20 metres high with greyish-brown smooth bark, dense foliage, and small dull-yellow flowers. When dried, the husk turns orange-brown.

Origin & Distribution

Native to the Moluccas and nearby islands. Cultivated alongside Nutmeg in Indonesia, Sri Lanka, and the West Indies, especially Grenada.

Related Species

Mace and nutmeg come from the same tree, Myristica fragrans. East Indian mace from Indonesia and Sri Lanka is considered superior to the West Indian variety produced in Grenada.

Folk Tradition

Mace is widely used as a domestic spice in both East and West, and has been used for centuries as a remedy mainly for digestive and kidney problems. In Malaysia it is used during pregnancy to strengthen and tone the uterine muscles. A small quantity of mace oleoresin is also produced by solvent extraction and is used in colognes and perfumes, especially men's fragrances. Recorded actions include analgesic, anti-emetic, anti-oxidant, antirheumatic, antiseptic, antispasmodic, aphrodisiac, carminative, digestive, emmenagogue, gastric secretory stimulant, larvicidal, orexigenic, prostaglandin inhibitor, stimulant, and tonic.

Safety

Mace contains more myristicin than nutmeg, and large doses can produce nausea, stupor, and tachycardia, with very large quantities being hallucinogenic. Mace is probably less safe to use than nutmeg, especially the West Indian variety. Use in moderation, and with care in pregnancy.
