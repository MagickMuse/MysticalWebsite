Juniper (Juniperus communis) is an ancient purifier, guardian, and strength‑giver.

Ancient Lineage

Junipers belong to the cypress family, a botanical line that reaches back roughly 250 million years to the age of Pangea. Because juniper existed before the continents separated, the same species, not merely cultivars, can be native to lands now divided by entire oceans.

Berry Constituents and Medicinal Use

Juniper berries are among the oldest medicines on record. They carry several active compounds, including α-Pinene, myrcene, and limonene, and they ease stomach pain and nausea while lending gin its signature flavor.

Patience, Resilience, and the Meditative Smoke

In pre-Christian times the juniper bush was believed to harbor spirits, and offerings were made to it to propitiate them. The plant became a symbol of patience, for its berries, which are in truth tiny cones, take three years to ripen. Because juniper grows happily in hilly, windswept places where little else thrives, it is likewise a symbol of resilience. Buddhist monks burn juniper wood in their sacred temple fires; though it burns poorly, it gives off an aromatic smoke said to aid both meditation and inner vision.

Navajo Postpartum Tea

⚠️ The following practice is sacred to the Diné (Navajo) and should only be practiced by those within the originating culture. The Diné prepare a tea from juniper berries to help restore a woman's strength after childbirth.

Sacred to the Furies

The Greeks consecrated juniper to the Untitled and burned it in their honor as a way of appeasing them. Juniper berries were also burned during funerals to keep malignant forces from approaching the dead.

Virgin Mary's Shelter

An Italian tradition of hanging juniper during the winter holidays traces back to the flight from Bethlehem, when Virgin Mary, fleeing the soldiers of King Herod with the infant Jesus, took shelter among juniper's branches.

Warding Door, Window, and Cradle

Juniper wood burned in the home brings good luck and breaks hexes. Sprigs may be brushed over doorways and windowsills, and laid near a baby's cradle, to ward off sickness and turn away harm.

Magickal Uses

  • Holy tree: A holy tree in Northern Europe and Siberia, used in shamanic incense along with wild thyme. Ripe blue berries used as medicine — known to be antibacterial, antiseptic, and antifungal.
  • Demon ward: Over hundreds of years considered very effective against all demons.
  • Lover charm: String mature berries for a charm to draw lovers.
  • Culinary recipe: Berries used in seasoning venison or wild duck — mix with lemon, one bay leaf, and 4–5 juniper berries; parboil in beef stock; baste wild meat while cooking.
  • Home and threshold: Juniper hung at the door protects against evil forces and persons.
  • Exorcism and cleansing: Burn in exorcism rites; a sprig protects its wearer from accidents and attacks by wild animals, guards against ghosts and sickness.
  • Love and potency: Added to love mixtures; the berries are carried to increase male potency.
  • Psychic powers and uncrossing: Carried or burned to help the psychic powers and to break hexes and curses; also drives off snakes.

For the deceased: Burning juniper berries at a gravesite during funeral rites wards off malicious and mischievous spirits, creating a protected space for the deceased and the mourners.

Safety: Not recommended for internal use if pregnant. Do not use long term (max 4–6 weeks at a time).

Safety and Common Use

Juniper is not poisonous. Every year, juniper trees are safely brought into the home at Christmas. Its berries are found among the spice aisles of food markets, not to mention as the primary flavoring in gin. Juniper is a component of many over-the-counter medical preparations, bath oils, herbal, and cosmetic products. However, it is not safe for use by pregnant women or by those actively attempting to conceive.

Distribution and History

Juniper is an evergreen widely distributed throughout the Northern hemisphere, with a long history of use in magick, midwifery, herbal medicine, and the brewing of intoxicants. Like those other small trees, elder and rowan, juniper is simultaneously identified with witchcraft and an alleged guard against it.

Ancient Western Asian Goddesses

Long ago, in Western Asia, juniper was associated with Lady Asherah and her daughters Anat and Astarte. Like those renowned, powerful goddesses, juniper is associated with birth, death, and prophetic ability.

Women's Herbal Ally

Juniper earned its fame as an herbal menstrual regulator. For women who lacked periods or whose cycles were irregular, preparations of juniper could jump-start them onto the road to regularity and fertility. However, timing and need are everything: juniper was also famous as a contraceptive and herbal abortifacient.

Depending upon one's perspective, juniper was understood as a woman's stalwart botanical ally, providing whatever she needed, or as an evil tree under the dominion of Satan.

Love Spells and Contraception

Juniper is included in many love spells and aphrodisiac formulations. When administered to men, frequently in the form of a potion, it allegedly increases sexual prowess and irresistibility. When administered to women, especially if in the form of a bath or douche, as in the Hoodoo formulation known as Hot Mama for instance, it may be understood as being intended to have contraceptive effects. (There is as yet no conclusive scientific information verifying or disputing this historic folkloric use of juniper, although pregnant women are strongly cautioned against its use.)

Clairvoyance and Disease Protection

Juniper's magical uses aren't limited to love and reproduction: it was also burned to stimulate clairvoyance and for protection from disease. (The volatile oils released by the burning wood provide an antiseptic effect.)

Even after World War I, French hospitals burned juniper branches alongside lavender to minimize rates of infection and the spread of disease. Juniper wood was traditionally included in Samhain bonfires to stimulate clairvoyance among those who inhaled its fumes.

Ward Against Witchcraft?

Juniper was also believed by some to ward off witchcraft. If one understands "witchcraft" to be synonymous with malevolent magick, then the belief makes sense; however it remains ironic, because by the late Middle Ages and beyond in Central Europe, juniper had become almost exclusively associated with witches and midwives.

Savin: The Potent Subspecies

Juniper became famous for its use as an herbal contraceptive and abortifacient at a time when such things were against the law and believed by many to set one literally onto the road to Hell. This was particularly true of the subspecies Juniperus sabina, popularly known as savin or sabin, which grows in Alpine regions and Central Europe and is a particularly potent menstrual regulator. The phrase "giving birth under the savin" was a common euphemism used during the Middle Ages for induced miscarriage.

Savin Bushes as Code

An eighteenth-century traveler visiting Swabia (now in modern Germany) writes that savin bushes served as a code, providing clear messages that couldn't be spoken aloud. Savin bushes in a garden signaled that the garden belonged to either a midwife or a barber, who at the time also frequently offered abortion services. Desperate people stole entire bushes or raided them for materials. Local authorities, on the other hand, periodically destroyed savin bushes as preventive measures.

Eventually in many Central European areas, the only savin bushes to be found were discreetly hidden among other plants on private property. Bushes on public property had all been destroyed. If the botanicals don't exist, women can't use them.

Lost Knowledge and Danger

Do not be in a hurry to plant savin bushes or at least not for personal use. They are beautiful, fragrant, powerful plants. However, although juniper bushes survive, the skilled practitioners who knew their secrets did not. Like the other plants in this section, administration required an experienced practitioner with a skillful hand. The wrong dosage, by a very slight margin, could cause disaster.

The Juniper Tree: A Grimms' Tale

The Brothers Grimm story The Juniper Tree recounts the tale of a woman who, unable to conceive, makes a Pagan-influenced sacrifice under her juniper tree. She conceives, but late in pregnancy (too late; she dies) she develops a desperate craving for juniper berries. The story is mysterious and indecipherable unless one understands the implications of that tree and those berries, inferences the Grimms' sources would have immediately understood.

The Juniper Tree, Retold

Once there was a good man who seemed always to be followed by misfortune. He and his wife longed for a child, and one winter day she stood in the courtyard beside a fragrant juniper tree, paring an apple, when she cut her finger. Her blood fell into the snow, and she sighed and said, "If only I had a child as red as blood and as pale as the snow." Within a month she found she was pregnant, and she brewed tea from the juniper's berries to keep her nausea at bay. The tree comforted and steadied her throughout the pregnancy, and one day she said to her husband, "If I die, bury me beneath this tree." Eight months later she gave birth to a beautiful boy, pale as snow with ruby red lips. She looked once upon her child, and then something turned, and before another moment passed, she died. With great sorrow her husband buried her beneath the juniper tree.

In time the man remarried, and he and his new wife had a daughter. The stepmother grew terribly jealous of the boy, though the brother and sister adored one another. One day the daughter asked her mother for an apple. The mother kept the apples locked in a great iron chest, for apples were a rarity in that country and the cold iron kept them fresh. She gave one to her daughter, and then the girl asked for another to give to her brother. Just then the boy was coming up the lane home from school, and a terrible thought seized the stepmother. When he stepped inside, his sister told him that mother had an apple for him too. Hungry, he asked politely. The stepmother told him to reach into the chest and take one for himself, and as he leaned in she slammed the lid down and chopped off his head, which rolled among the shining apples.

Terrified of being found out, she propped the boy upright, tied his head back on with a little handkerchief, and put an apple in his hand. When the little sister came and asked her brother for an apple, he gave no answer. The mother said, "Just give him a little shove," and when the girl did, the head fell off. The mother cried, "Oh, what have you done to your brother!" The little girl was hysterical, and the mother said, "We must hide this, or you will be caught." Then, wickedly, she cut him into pieces and cooked him into black puddings, salted by his sister's tears, which would not stop falling.

When the father came home, the stepmother told him the boy had gone to stay with his uncle for several weeks. Though surprised, he accepted the excuse, sat down to dinner, and ate the black pudding, throwing the bones beneath the table. The sister ate nothing. When her parents had left the kitchen, she gathered every one of her brother's bones into a silk handkerchief and carried them out to bury beside his mother beneath the juniper tree. At this the tree began to shift and stir, and a mist swirled about it, and suddenly she felt as if her brother were with her again. A little bird in the branches saw it all.

The bird flew to the windowsill of a cobbler's shop and sang:

My mother she killed me,

My father he ate me,

My sister she cries in sorrow,

As I lie below,

Stick, stock, stone dead.

The cobbler was enchanted but could not be certain he had heard the song aright, and asked the bird to sing it again. "I will sing if you give me those pretty red shoes you are making," said the bird. The cobbler gave them over, and the bird sang the song again.

Next the bird flew to the watchmaker's windowsill and sang the same eerie song. "Such a beautiful song," the watchmaker cried, "but I did not catch the words. Sing it again, little bird." "I will sing again if you give me that gold watch and chain," said the bird. The watchmaker obliged, and the bird sang once more. Then it flew off, the red shoes clutched in one claw and the gold watch and chain in the other.

The bird came to a mill where three millers were chiseling a great millstone. Perched in a nearby tree, the bird sweetly sang:

My mother she killed me,

My father he ate me,

My sister she cries in sorrow,

As I lie below.

Stick!

At this, one of the men set down his tools and looked up.

Stock!

The second miller set down his tools and looked up.

Stone!

The third miller set down his tools and looked up.

Dead!

All three cried in unison, "Oh precious bird, please sing that song to us again!" The bird replied, "I will sing it again if you put that millstone round my neck." The men obliged, and the bird sang the song one more time and flew away.

It returned home and landed on the roof where it could see the juniper tree from its perch. It dragged the millstone against the tin of the roof. The wicked stepmother said, "Oh, how it thunders!" The little girl ran outside to look for lightning in the sky, and as she ran the bird dropped the red shoes at her feet. Again the bird rattled the millstone against the roof, and again the stepmother said, "It thunders!" This time the father ran out, and the bird dropped the gold watch and chain. The little chain caught around his wrist, and he caught the watch in his hand. Delighted, he cried, "Ah, what wonderful gifts the thunder brings us!" The stepmother looked out the window and saw her stepdaughter with shiny red shoes and her husband with a gold watch. The bird dragged the millstone once more across the tin roof. "What gifts will the thunder leave for me?" she wondered, and ran out the door. Just as she did, the bird dropped the millstone on her head, and it killed her.

Hearing the commotion, the father and his daughter ran out and saw the woman dead on the ground, and a great deal of smoke and fire rising up around the juniper tree. And there, also, was the brother, and they were together again at last.

Intoxicating Beverages

Juniper's role as a component in intoxicating beverages remains today:

  • It was once used to flavor whiskey
  • Genevrette is a French beer-like drink brewed from equal parts barley and juniper berries
  • The Dutch alcoholic beverage known as jenever eventually evolved into the English potion, gin

Botanical Description

An evergreen shrub or tree of the Cupressaceae family, growing up to 6 metres high, with bluish-green narrow stiff needles. It bears small flowers and little round berries, which are green in the first year and turn black in the second and third.

Folk Tradition (Medicinal)

The needles and berries have a long traditional history of use. Juniper is used medicinally for urinary infections such as cystitis and urethritis; for respiratory problems such as bronchitis, colic, and coughs; and for gastro-intestinal infections and worms. It helps expel the build-up of uric acid in the joints and is employed in gout, rheumatism, and arthritis. It is current in the British Herbal Pharmacopoeia for rheumatic pain and cystitis.

Other Species

In Yugoslavia, an oil is produced from the fruit and twigs of J. smerka, less rich and sweet than that of common juniper. Various other species include J. oxycedrus (which produces Cade Oil, see Cade), J. virginiana (which produces Virginian Cedarwood Essential Oil, see Virginian Cedar), and J. sabina (which produces savin oil, also discussed in this entry's Savin section above).

Related Oils

See Juniper Berry Essential Oil (steam distilled from berries; the preferred aromatherapy form) and Juniper Needle Essential Oil (steam distilled from needles and wood; often adulterated, source recommends avoiding).
