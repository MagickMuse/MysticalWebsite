Mabon

Thistle is one of the most powerful protective and strengthening herbs in magickal practice, revered by ancient Celtic peoples and Norse practitioners. A bowl of thistles placed in a room strengthens the spirits and renews vitality of all within it. Wizards in England traditionally selected the tallest thistle in a patch to use as a magickal wand or walking stick.

Some of the most beautiful things worth having come wrapped in a crown of thorns. If the plants that harm are often the plants that heal, then a plant covered all over in tiny little swords must do wonders.

Magickal Uses

  • Strength and Vitality: A bowl of thistles placed in a room strengthens the spirits and renews the vitality of all within it. Carry thistle or part of a thistle for energy and strength.
  • Garden Protection: Grown in the garden, thistles ward off thieves. Grown in a pot on the doorstep, they protect against evil.
  • Personal Protection: A thistle blossom carried in the pocket guards its bearer from harm and negative influences.
  • Lightning Protection: Thrown onto a fire, thistles deflect lightning away from the house during storms.
  • Hex-Breaking: To break spells cast against you, wear a shirt made of fibers spun and woven from thistle. Stuff hex-breaking poppets with thistles.
  • Exorcism: Thistles strewn in homes and buildings exorcise evil spirits and negative entities.
  • Healing and Love: Used in healing spells, and when men carry thistle, they become better lovers.
  • Spirit Communication: To call spirits, place thistle in boiling water, remove from heat, and lie beside it. As steam rises, call spirits and listen for their answers.
  • Magickal Tools: Wizards used the tallest thistle as magickal wands or walking sticks for enhanced power.

Botanical Family

To count as a thistle, a plant carries spiny prickles along its leaves and often its stems. Most thistles have an urn-shaped flower head whose petals run from purple to pink to yellow, and then erupt into a downy seedhead carried on the wind. Most, though not all, thistles belong to the Asteraceae family. The common name spans a wide range of genera and species. Their range stretches from the Highlands of Scotland to the shores of the Pacific.

Notable Species

  • Spear Thistle (Cirsium vulgare): Native to Scotland and often called the true Scottish thistle. It is the most likely candidate for the thistle Mary, Queen of Scots, incorporated into the Great Seal of Scotland, and it appears on Scottish coins and as the emblem of the Order of the Thistle.
  • Scottish Thistle (Onopordum acanthium): Chosen by Sir Walter Scott in 1822 as Scotland's national emblem for the visit of George IV. An introduced species rather than a native.
  • Blessed Thistle (Cnicus benedictus): One of the most commonly used thistles in medicine.
  • Carline Thistle (Carlina vulgaris): Heals wounds, acts as an antiseptic, and treats urinary disorders such as UTIs and cystitis.
  • Barnaby's Thistle (Centaurea solstitialis): Native to the Mediterranean. Its pollen was discovered by archaeobotanists at a Neanderthal gravesite, pushing the bond between people and thistle back at least sixty thousand years.
  • Sow Thistle (Sonchus oleraceus): Native throughout Europe and western Asia. Considered a gift from Hekate.
  • Cobweb Thistle (Cirsium occidentale): Native to the Pacific Coast of California. Its spiny leaves carry a pronounced silver pattern, and its downy seeds drape like cobwebs.
  • Cirsium horridulum: A related species. ⚠️ The Seminole craft blowgun darts from this plant. This use is sacred to the Seminole and should be honored as a closed cultural practice belonging only to those within the originating culture.

Medicinal Uses

Cirsium thistles are antifungal, antibacterial, detoxifying, cleansing, diuretic, and anti-inflammatory. Externally they treat bruises, burns, and bites. Some thistles, especially in the root, carry mild analgesic action.

⚠️ The Saanich, Cowichan, and other Island Salish peoples peel the taproots and eat them raw or cooked. The Saanich place thistle leaves in the bath, holding that the plant's sharpness drives away negative forces. These are closed Indigenous practices, sacred to the originating cultures and not to be taken up by outsiders.

The Order of the Thistle

The Order of the Thistle is named the greatest order of chivalry in Scotland and is believed to have been established in the late fifteenth or early sixteenth century during the reign of James III. The order recognizes sixteen knights as well as Scottish men and women who have given to the country through public service or other contributions. Its motto, Nemo me impune lacessit, translates as "Nobody provokes me with impunity," which sounds like exactly what happens if you mess with a thistle.

Every other year the knights gather for the Thistle Service. They process in full regalia of green velvet robes and white-plumed hats to the Order's chapel in Edinburgh. The biennial schedule mirrors the plant itself, since thistles are biennials that bloom every other year.

The Thistle's Defense of Scotland

In the thirteenth century, a party of Norse invaders crept toward a Scottish camp at night, intent on a sneak attack. They removed their shoes to approach in silence and stepped barefoot onto the spiky thistles. Their cries of pain woke the clansmen, who rose and defeated the invaders. Scotland has held the plant as a defender ever since.

The Sow Thistle's Hidden Treasure

The sow thistle is a gift from Hekate. When you see one, the words "Open sow-thistle" spoken in the manner of "Open sesame" will reveal any concealed or hidden treasures in the immediate vicinity.

The Thistle of Mecklenburg

A German tale from Mecklenburg names a barren patch of ground that marks where a murder once took place. A strange thistle grows there. Each day at high noon the plant sprouts human arms, hands, and heads, and when one dozen heads have grown, it vanishes. Anyone who looks at this botanical horror risks illness or paralysis. Perhaps that is why no one searches for it too hard.

What Happened to the Thistle

A fairy tale of the same name opens in the well-kept garden of a grand old mansion, resplendent with rare trees, plants, and flowers. The gardens are much admired, and visitors sometimes come for them alone. The thistle is ignored by every passerby until a beautiful woman visiting from Scotland recognizes it and elevates it to its rightful place of honor. Near the fence between garden and meadow stood an uncommonly large and fine thistle, strong and full enough to earn the name thistle bush.

Melancholy, the Fall, and the Hidden Nut

The thistle has long been held able to banish melancholy, whether through its sweet scent or through the wine in which its flowers were once steeped to make the remedy. Scripture casts it in a darker light, for when Adam and Eve are driven from Eden into the wilderness, it is among the thorns and thistles that spring up there, a sign of mankind's fall from grace. Yet even this prickly emblem of hardship keeps a kindness hidden, for the spiny flower heads of many thistles can be cut open to yield a tasty and nourishing nut.
