History

Peppermint as we know it emerged in 1696 when a hybrid of spearmint spontaneously appeared in a field in England, giving rise to one of the most widely used herbs in the world. Its first formal entry in the London Pharmacopoeia came in 1721, though it is believed ancient Egyptians were already using it centuries earlier to settle upset stomachs. Mints have been cultivated since ancient times in China and Japan, and evidence of a type of peppermint has been found in Egyptian tombs dating from 1000 BC.

Characteristics

Peppermint is notoriously difficult to grow from seed and does not breed true, meaning seedlings will not reliably resemble the parent plant. As a result, it is most commonly propagated through cuttings. It can reach up to three feet in height and prefers moist conditions, growing best near ponds and streams. It does not tolerate dryness. The plant has a tendency to spread and benefits from being moved every few years. Peppermint is distinguished from spearmint by its menthol content, which gives it its characteristic sharp, cooling quality. Two main forms are recognised: white peppermint, with green stems and leaves, and black peppermint, with dark green serrated leaves, purplish stems, and reddish-violet flowers. It is widely used as a digestive aid and flavoring, and appears in everything from gum and toothpaste to tea.

Origin & Distribution

Originally a cultivated hybrid of Mentha viridis and Mentha aquatica, peppermint was being propagated in England before the seventeenth century. It is now naturalised throughout Europe and America and is cultivated worldwide. The essential oil is produced mainly in France, England, America, Russia, Bulgaria, Italy, Hungary, Morocco, and China.

Related Species

Several chemotypes of peppermint exist. Other species in the mint family include spearmint, apple mint, pennyroyal, water mint, and pineapple mint, some of which are also used to produce essential oils. Peppermints grown in northern regions, including the Mitcham peppermint, are considered of superior quality.

Folk Tradition

Peppermint has been used extensively in Eastern and Western medicine for indigestion, nausea, sore throat, diarrhoea, headaches, toothaches, and cramp. It remains current in the British Herbal Pharmacopoeia for intestinal colic, flatulence, common cold, vomiting in pregnancy, and dysmenorrhoea. Recorded actions include analgesic, anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, antiphlogistic, antipruritic, antiseptic, antispasmodic, antiviral, astringent, carminative, cephalic, cholagogue, cordial, emmenagogue, expectorant, febrifuge, hepatic, nervine, stomachic, sudorific, vasoconstrictor, and vermifuge.

Botanical Information

Mentha × piperita (Lamiaceae family). Masculine herb originating from Europe and Northern Africa. Also known as Brandy Mint and Lammint. Bright green leaves with crisp scent link to money and clarity.
