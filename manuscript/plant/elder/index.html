Elder (Sambucus canadensis, S. racemosa) is a shrub or small tree with compound leaves and clusters of small white flowers.

🌿
Not All Parts Are the Same: Elder is tagged as flower, fruit, herb, and tree, and each part carries distinct properties and safety considerations.
  • Flowers: Used in healing rituals, sleep sachets, fairie blessings, and teas. Gentle and well-suited for internal use.
  • Berries (ripe): Carried for protection; used in prosperity spells and food preparations. Ripe fruit is generally safe internally. Unripe berries are toxic.
  • Leaves: Used in prosperity spells and green magick, but not recommended for internal use.
  • Bark, roots, and wood: Used for wands, amulets, and incense for spirit contact. Not for internal use. Do not burn wood. (See warning below.)

If a recipe or ritual calls for "elder," check which part it specifies before proceeding.

Magickal Properties

  • Powers: Exorcism, Protection, Healing, Prosperity, Sleep
  • Deities: Crone

Folk Names

Ellhorn, Elderberry, Lady Elder, Pipe Tree, Bore Tree, Bour Tree, Hylder, Hylantree, Eldrun, Hollunder, Sureau, Black Elder

Source Book Notes

Druid blessings and curses: The Druids used elder to both bless and curse by scattering the leaves to the winds in the four directions.

Sacred to: The Great Goddess, Venus, and the Summer Solstice.

Summer Solstice fairy sighting: Tradition says that standing under an elder at Midsummer enables one to see fairies.

Narcotic odor: Its odor is said to be narcotic and vision-inducing.

Elder wands: Elder wands are used for evoking and exorcising evil spirits.

Musical instruments: The branches can be made into panpipes and flutes.

Warding: Elder is said to ward off bad luck, thieves, and lightning.

Ritual Uses

The elder has long been thought to possess the power to bless or curse. Elder wands are used by witches in many magickal operations since the wood is protective yet quite potent for magickal workings. Elder is associated with wand-making.

⚠️
Never burn elder wood. This is a longstanding prohibition in folk tradition.

Magickal Uses

For protection and exorcism: Hang elder over doors and windows to keep evil from the house. Elder bushes planted in the garden protect it from sorcerers and lightning. The berries, when carried, protect against evil and negativity.

For prosperity: Elder leaves and berries are used in prosperity spells, and the flowers in healing rituals.

For blessing: To bless a person, place, or thing, scatter elder flowers to the four winds in that spot. This ancient blessing ritual should be done with reverence.

For sleep: Elder flowers placed under the pillow induce sleep, and the berries worn as a charm protect the bearer against rheumatism.

For a peaceful death: Keeping elder twigs, leaves, or berries in the home is said to ensure a happy and peaceful death for all who live there.

The Witch Tree

The elder, known as the "witch tree," is powerfully identified with witchcraft, the magickal arts and goddess-oriented Pagan tradition.

Threshold Tree: Portal Between Realms

The elder is a threshold tree: it serves as a portal that allows souls to pass between realms. Ghosts, spirits, and elves can pass into the mortal realm via elder trees and bushes but, remember, one-way signs don't exist in the magickal, shamanic world. portals where you can access other realms.

In order to establish contact with other realms, try burning elder bark, blossoms, roots, and wood as incense. (If gathering botanical material yourself, remember to ask for permission from the plant first and always to leave a gift in return.)

Sacred to Hulda (Mother Holle)

The elder is sacred to the Germanic deity, Hulda, known affectionately as Mother Holle. Hulda was once an extremely prominent, important deity, so entrenched in people's hearts that, unlike some other spirits, she was never entirely banished. Unable to completely eradicate her, local Christian authority dubbed her a Queen of Witches, with the immediate implication that all who were devoted to this queen were witches themselves.

In Denmark, the elder tree itself is called Frau Hylle or Hyllemoer, Danish for Mother Holle or Frau Holle. In Anglo-Saxon it was known as Hylder or Hylantree. As part of Hulda's ritual, circle dances were performed around the tree.

Home of Family Ghosts

For the ancient Germans and Slavs, old elder trees, especially those that nestled close to a house, were the home of family ghosts. Because they are threshold trees, elders are often incorporated into funeral rites.

  • Heathen Frisians buried their dead underneath elders
  • In England, grave-diggers traditionally carried elder wood so as to protect them from any malevolent ghosts lingering in the graveyard
  • In other areas, it was customary for the driver of the hearse to carry a whip made of elder wood

Witches in Disguise

Perhaps because of associations with Hulda, elder became identified with witchcraft. Elder bushes personified witches. It was believed that witches could transform into elders as surely as they could assume the form of a cat. One single solitary elderberry bush popping up in an unexpected spot might actually be a witch in disguise, another reason to treat the elder with respect.

Perhaps in the spirit of that old saying "it takes one to know one" elders are also powerful agents used to ward off malevolent spells.

Protective Burial Traditions

Elders have a powerful reputation as protective trees, especially for fending off malevolent witchcraft. Traditional protective practices include:

  • Fingernail parings, hair or teeth are buried beneath elders so as to prevent their use in malevolent spells
  • Afterbirths of calves and foals are buried beneath elders so that neither the new-born animals nor their mothers can be bewitched (These practices may also be simultaneously understood as offerings to Mother Holle, the elves or the ancestors)
  • Elder wood is carved into amulets to prevent unwanted enchantment
  • Green elder branches were buried in a grave to protect the dead from witches and evil spirits

Elder "Blood"

Elder is incorporated into many spells, especially those for love and protection. A nickname for elder sap is "blood." Sap was understood as literally the blood of the tree, in the same way that bark is its skin and leaves the tree's hair. Because the elder was believed to house important spirits, to embody Hulda's essence or to even be a witch in disguise, elder "blood" was potentially incredibly powerful, more powerful than most sap.

Northern European spells that cite "blood" as an ingredient may, in fact, be requesting elder sap instead.

Physical Characteristics

Elder is a short, bent, crooked tree that never grows very tall, hence the constant confusion between whether it's a bush or a tree. It is not a tall, forbidding, imposing tree. Unlike other witchcraft plants, elderberries are not toxic but tasty and nutritious, as anyone who's had elderberry preserves or wine can attest.

A Friendly Tree

Unlike so many other witchcraft plants, elders are friendly plants; they're understood as a tree that likes people and is by nature helpful and affectionate. (Spirits residing within may or may not be as friendly and benevolent: Frau Holle and the elves both possess reputations for volatility, although this may be in response to defamation and loss of respect and offerings.)

The Christian Dilemma

Elder's roots among spiritual traditions of Northern Europe and its associations with spiritual entities and ancestral spirits were so powerfully entrenched that it created a dilemma for ascendant Christian authority.

Attempts were made to either:

  1. Taint the tree as evil and diabolical, thus to be shunned by all righteous people, or
  2. Incorporate elder into Christian tradition, so that its use could continue under proper auspices

Both methods were historically tried.

Christian propaganda also claimed that elder repelled witches. Apparently no one told Hulda or her witches, who use elder in abundance. The claim was likely a twist on the long held magickal wisdom that elder repels evil. Hulda herself is among the leaders of the Wild Hunt, the reveling procession of spirits and witches.

Source of Light

In the days before the easy availability of wax and hence cheap candles, elder was a source of light.

Funerary Uses

In Jewish folk tradition, planting an elder cutting on a grave serves as a spiritual barometer. If the plant flourishes, it is read as a sign that the soul is at peace and content in the next realm.

Magickal Teas

Elder flower can be brewed as a magickal tea to receive Fairie blessings, honor or invoke the Goddess, support healing, attract prosperity, and promote rest. Drink 1 to 2 cups only during the spell event itself.

Native Range

Sambucus species are native to North America and most of Europe. With more than one variety in cultivation, the tree thrives easily in many climates without overtaking the garden, erupting in hundreds of fragrant white blooms followed by deep purple berries rich in antioxidants and vitamin C.

Medicinal Uses

Nearly every part of the tree carries medicine. The berries, generally cooked into a syrup and sold in drugstores worldwide under the name Sambucus or elderberry, treat coughs, colds, and fevers. The flowers are taken in infusion for bronchitis, coughs, and other respiratory complaints, and an infusion of the blossoms is also used to brighten the skin. An ointment made from the leaves or flowers calms irritated skin and is applied to bruises, burns, and sprains. The elder is antiseptic and antiviral, which is why folk wart cures using elder branches have a basis in plant chemistry. The unripe berries are toxic and must never be consumed in any form.

Compassion and the Sight of Magickal Beings

Beyond protection from evil spirits and curses, elder is said to reveal magickal beings to humans and to bring compassion to those who work with it. Witches turn to elder to commune with nature and to walk alongside the fairy and elemental spirits.

Hyldemoer, the Elder Mother

The dryad-like spirit who dwells within the tree is known by many names. In Denmark she is Hyldemoer, the Elder Mother, or Hylde-qvinde, the Elder Woman. She guards the medicine of the tree and avenges any injury done to it. Picking the flowers, harvesting the berries, or interfering with any part of the elder without her explicit permission is a serious taboo. The herbalist tradition holds that every garden, however small, should have at least one elder in it, for all the other herbs grow under her protection.

Asking the Elder Mother for Permission

Before touching berries, bark, flower, wood, or even a discarded twig, make a sincere spoken request of the Elder Mother. One Danish custom is to ask aloud, wait long enough to see whether the tree objects, and if no refusal comes, spit three times before harvesting only what you need. If you are uncertain whether permission was sought before an elder cutting reached your hands, leave it alone.

The Tale of the Elderbush

Hans Christian Andersen, who grew up in Denmark where the Elder Mother's name is woven into the language itself, preserved her in a fairy tale variously translated as The Elderbush or Elder-Tree Mother. A little boy catches cold after splashing through puddles on his way to school, and his mother puts him to bed with a pot of elder tea. A jolly old neighbour with no wife or children sits beside the bed and tells the boy to look into the teapot, for there is a story inside it. As the boy looks, the lid lifts of its own accord, white elderflowers spring up one by one, and long boughs grow out of the spout and the body of the pot until a great elder tree fills the room. In the heart of the tree sits a kindly old woman in a dress so green and trimmed with so many white blossoms that it is impossible to tell whether she wears cloth or leaves. She is the Elder Mother, stepping out of the medicine the child has been given.

The Russian Twig Cure

In Russia, elder twigs were thought to carry toxins and illness out of the body. The cure called for the sufferer to take a twig, push it silently into the ground without speaking a word, and walk away. The next person to lay eyes on the twig was believed to take the illness with them. The custom is included for the folkloric record, not as a recommendation.

Wart Charms

Two old elder wart charms survive. In the first, you rub an elder branch across each wart, cut a notch into the wood for every wart treated, then bury the twig. As the wood decomposes underground, the warts dissolve from the skin. In the second, the fresh shoot of an elder branch is rubbed directly on the warts and then buried. The remedy is grounded in the tree's antiseptic and antiviral properties.

Never Build with Elder

Elder protects the garden and the home, but it must never be used to build either, nor any of the furnishings within them. Folk tradition is full of stories of sleepers waking bruised and pinched in the night because a bedpost, a cradle, or a piece of furniture had been made from elder wood without the Elder Mother's permission, and she had taken her revenge while they slept.

Trolls and Fairies on Midsummer's Eve

Standing beneath or near an elder on Midsummer's Eve opens the eyes to trolls and fairies as they pass by in procession. Elderflower water and elderberry wine are both used in Midsummer rites, carrying the tree's revealing power into the cup.

Samhain Wards

At Samhain, elder branches are hung over doors and windows to turn aside the many evil and mischievous creatures abroad on that night. The tree that reveals the unseen on Midsummer's Eve is the same tree that holds the unwanted out at the year's other great threshold.

The Ogham Letter Ruis

The elder appears in the Ogham tree alphabet as its fifteenth letter, called Ruis. A prolific tree, content in poor soil and dense shade, it rarely attains the height or girth of which it is capable; an elder with a girth of two meters is an unusual sight.

Broomsticks, Crowns, and the Hollow Stem

Like elm, elder wood was held proper for the making of broomsticks, and crowns of elderwood were worn at Samhain to heighten the chance of otherworldly communication, both heard and seen. In England no elder might be felled without first seeking the leave of the Elder Mother, here called "Old Gal"; whoever injured the tree was said to see it gush blood and turn back into the human shape of the witch within, who would then bear the very scars that had been dealt to the wood. Because the soft pith pushes so easily from the surrounding stem, children have long made the hollow sticks into peashooters and adults into bellows for blowing smouldering embers into flame, a use preserved in the Anglo-Saxon names eldern, meaning "hollow," and aeld, meaning "fire."

Meaning When Gifted

When elder flower is gifted it carries the meaning of zeal.
