Hazel (Corylus avellana; C. cornuta) is a wise tree of luck, wishes, protection, and inspired craft.

Native Range

Hazel grows across the Northern Hemisphere, with strong presence in North America, Europe, and Asia. Corylus maxima is the filbert.

Medicinal Use

Hazel can ease stomach complaints, nausea, flu, and other common cold symptoms. The flowers are the part most often used, brewed as a tea.

Sacred to the Dead

Hazel trees are sacred to the dead. Their wood is favored for wands used to invoke spirits and to work any magick that involves passage between realms. They are also protecting trees, raised to keep evil or brutish spirits out of the fairy realm, which makes them equally fitted to guard a home.

Cinderella's Hazel Tree

In the Grimm Brothers' telling of Cinderella, hazel carries the heart of the story. Cinderella's mother had died, and her father remarried a woman who was unkind and had two rude daughters. When her father set off for market, the stepsisters asked for new gowns and jewels. Cinderella asked only for the first branch that knocked against his hat on the road home. As he rode back through a green thicket, a hazel twig brushed against him and struck the hat from his head. He broke it off and brought it to her.

Cinderella took the twig to her mother's grave, planted it there, and wept so freely that her tears watered it. The branch grew into a handsome tree. Three times a day she sat beneath it, weeping and praying, and a little white bird always settled in its branches. Whatever Cinderella wished for, the bird threw down to her.

When the king's festival began and the stepsisters left her behind, it was the hazel tree, not a fairy godmother, that clothed her. She went to her mother's grave and said:

Shiver and quiver, little tree,
Silver and gold throw down over me.

The bird sent down a gold and silver gown and slippers embroidered with silk and silver. She returned again the second night, and a third, the dresses more splendid each time and the last pair of slippers all of gold. On the third night the prince smeared pitch on the staircase to catch her, and one golden slipper stuck fast. He vowed to marry only the woman whose foot fit the shoe.

When the prince brought the slipper to her house, the first stepsister cut off her toe to force her foot inside, and the second cut a slice from her heel. Each time the prince rode away with the false bride, two pigeons in the hazel tree called out:

Turn and peep, turn and peep,
There's blood within the shoe,
The shoe it is too small for her,
The true bride waits for you.

Each time he looked down, saw the blood, and brought the false bride back. Only when Cinderella herself slid her foot into the slipper did it fit, and the pigeons on the hazel tree sang:

Turn and peep, turn and peep,
No blood is in the shoe,
The shoe is not too small for her,
The true bride rides with you.

At the wedding, just before the stepsisters entered the church, the pigeons pecked out their eyes. The hazel that grew from a daughter's grief stood through it all as protector, oracle, and tree of the dead, vouching for the true bride and punishing the false.

Magickal Uses

  • Sacred to the Nine Muses: Hazel is a symbol of concentrated wisdom, knowledge, and poetic eloquence, sacred to the Nine Muses.
  • Druidic heralds: Druidic heralds carried hazel wands. Hazel is the most powerful tree for general magick wands, symbolizing white magick and healing.
  • Divining: Forked hazel branches are used for divining buried treasure, water, thieves, and murderers.
  • Caryatid: The caryatid is a type of nut fairy or nymph who gives poetic inspiration.
  • Anti-lightning: The wood serves as an anti-lightning charm.
  • Protective circle: Draw a circle around yourself on the ground with a hazel twig when outside and in need of magickal protection.
  • Luck and bridal blessing: String nuts and hang in the house for luck; gift to a bride for good fortune.
  • Wisdom and fertility: Nuts eaten to gain wisdom and increase fertility; often eaten before divination.
  • Protective circles: Draw a circle in the dirt with a hazel twig to protect yourself or a plant.
  • Wish crown and invisibility: Weave a crown of hazel twigs for wish magic; hazel crowns were also worn to induce invisibility.
  • House protection: Twigs in window frames guard against lightning; three hazel pins driven into the house protect from fire.
  • Wands and dowsing: Excellent for all‑purpose wands; forked branches used for divining hidden things. Hazel twigs and forks cut on Midsummer's Eve are specifically recommended for divining purposes.

Additional Notes

Hazel is associated with Thor, Brigid, and Apollo and serves as a European symbol of wisdom. The hazel nut was considered food of the gods in Celtic tradition. Hazel is prized for wand-making and enhances Witchery skills and magickal abilities.

The Tree of Knowledge

For the Celts the hazel was the Tree of Knowledge, its nuts held to contain great wisdom, and in Druidic lore it is bound to the Salmon of Wisdom, which won its knowing by eating the magical hazel nuts that fall in autumn. Coll, the old Irish word for hazel and the name borne by early Kings of Ireland, is also its name in the Ogham alphabet. So sacred was the tree that, like the apple, it was once punishable by death to fell or harm it, and hazel branches were laid with the dead to grant them wisdom in the afterlife.

Favouring damp ground, the hazel was tied to water, and its wood makes the best dowsing rods for finding hidden wells and springs, and seams of minerals besides. In Cornwall such rods of coppiced hazel were prized for sniffing out tin, a coppicing craft known in Britain since the Bronze Age. Because harming the tree was forbidden, the wand had to be cut in a set and ritual manner with a sickle, and only at dawn on a Wednesday, the day of Mercury, the god who rules the hazel. Held the tree of witches and of the gods who ruled the sea, the hazel was further bound to water, and woven hazel wishing caps were worn by sailors and fishermen to win those gods' protection. Its nut, long a good food, is a symbol of fertility: in parts of Germany the bride handed out hazel nuts three days after the wedding to show the marriage was consummated, and the nuts served in divinations to glimpse a future marriage partner. Like the willow, the hazel inspires poetry and song, perhaps for the sound of the wind sighing through its soft leaves.

Meaning When Gifted

When hazel is gifted it carries the meaning of reconciliation.
