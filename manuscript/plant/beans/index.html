Beans

Notes/Warnings
Beans not used for food may be poisonous. Most Phaseolus species contain phytohemagglutinin (PHA), a protein that is toxic in large quantities when raw; thorough cooking neutralizes it, and a pinch of wood ash added to the cooking water helps. Fava beans (Vicia faba) carry a separate, severe risk for people with G6PD deficiency (favism).

Beans (Phaseolus spp.) are leguminous crops with powerful protective and fertility properties in magical practice.

Magickal Properties

Planet: Mercury

Powers: Protection, Exorcism, Wart Charming, Reconciliations, Potency, Love

Ritual Uses

Some beans' flowers are white, and hence sacred to the Goddesses of old Europe. Beans are always sacred to harvest goddesses. According to tradition, only the highest-ranking priestess in Scotland could either plant or cook the bean. Beans, along with pork, were offered to Cardea in Rome on June 1st. They are also associated with the Underworld and the dead; in ancient Rome they were distributed and eaten during funerals.

Magickal Uses

For protection: In general, beans were used in classical times (and still are) as a charm against evil sorcerers. Put a bean in your mouth and spit it out at the person. Dried beans are carried as an amulet against negativity and evil magick. They are also used in rattles to scare away spirits, especially those who have entered into bodies and made people sick.

For exorcism: To avert evil spirits, say very quickly three times in one breath: "Three blue beans in a blue bladder. Rattle, bladder, rattle."

For reconciliation: If a couple is quarreling, the woman should carry three lima beans strung on a silk thread for two days. The couple will quickly smooth over their difficulties.

For potency: Beans help cure impotency if they are carried or eaten. This is due to the fact that they resemble testicles.

For love magick: A woman should place seven beans of any kind in a circle on the ground. Next, she must have the man of her choice step into the circle or walk over it. If this can be done, he will be attracted to her. (But this might also be manipulatory.)

For wart removal: Rub a dried bean on each wart during the waning Moon. As you do this say: "As this bean decays, So wart, fall away!"

⚠️ Safety Note: Beans not used for food may be poisonous.

Ghost Repulsion

Scattering beans around the perimeter of a property is said to deny ghosts entry for up to a year. If any beans take root and grow into plants, the banishing effect may extend even longer. An older practice rooted in ancient Greece involves spitting or throwing beans directly at a ghost to drive it away.

Additional Notes

Fava Beans

Italian folklore is full of interesting magical references to beans:

  • Beans were spat in the directions of ghosts for purposes of exorcism
  • Beans were believed to serve as containers for unborn human souls, thus serving as fertility symbols, and incorporated into fertility rituals
  • Beans were also sometimes a tabooed food

Beans thus were the seeds of life and death.

Although beans in general are perceived as a magical food by many traditions, these Italian traditions didn't just refer to any beans: many modern beans derive from the Western Hemisphere and, like tomatoes, were unknown in Europe pre-Columbus. The beans incorporated into Italian traditions are fava beans.

Death and Rebirth Symbolism

Fava beans (Vicia faba) are traditionally associated with death and rebirth. Ancient Romans served them at funeral banquets. This tradition still survives in Italian witchcraft and spiritual practices. At midnight on October 31st, for instance, bowls of fava beans are placed outdoors for the spirits; they are then buried in Earth after sunrise on November 1st.

Favism: A Sacred Danger

Fava beans were understood as a magically potent, potentially deadly food and for good reason: a condition known as "favism" is common throughout the Middle East and the Mediterranean. There is a genetic predisposition toward this condition, however it is triggered by the consumption of fava beans. Once upon a time, favism was considered to be an allergic reaction to fava beans. It is now known to be caused by a deficiency in an enzyme (glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase).

Favism is a type of anemia caused by hemolysis (destruction of healthy red blood cells). Victims are predominately male; almost all victims are of Middle Eastern and Mediterranean descent. Symptoms include intense fatigue, nausea, vertigo, and dark-orange colored urine. The condition is usually temporary but seasonal, corresponding to the sprouting of fava beans in the early spring. Thus favism is among the first signs of spring in that region. Favism can be fatal if an attack is sufficiently severe: approximately one in twelve cases proves fatal.

Historical Cultivation

The oldest known fava beans were found in an archeological dig in Nazareth and date from c.6500 BCE but are believed to have been wild plants. Widespread cultivation of fava beans is believed to stem from the third-millennium BCE; they were widely cultivated throughout the Middle East, Mediterranean, and North Africa.

Fava beans were a mixed blessing. Although they were potentially deadly to a sizable percentage of the population, they have also been found to protect against malaria, once among the primary causes of death in the region.

Ritual Traditions

Saturnalia and King Cakes: In ancient Rome, one single fava bean was baked within one of the ritual cakes of the Saturnalia. Whoever found it was crowned Lord of the Saturnalia. This tradition survives in the King Cakes of New Orleans' Mardi Gras, although instead of a fava bean, the token is a tiny baby doll.

St. Joseph's Beans: Italian immigrants to New Orleans brought other fava-bean traditions with them. On the Feast Day of St Joseph, dried, roasted fava beans are blessed, thus transforming into special St Joseph's Beans or Lucky Mojo Beans. Allegedly someone who carries one of these lucky beans will never want for money.

Feast of the Epiphany: The Romans spread fava bean rituals throughout their Empire; some continue to evolve. In France, the Feast of the Epiphany, also called the Feast of the Kings, is occasion for mass consumption of the "galette of the kings"—a flat, round pastry filled with almond paste baked with one trinket concealed inside. This lucky charm is called a fève (fava bean in French) because once that's what served as the trinket. Whoever finds the fève becomes king or queen for the day. (Galettes are sometimes sold with paper crowns.)

In the late nineteenth century, small porcelain figurines began to replace the traditional bean. Fèves are now collectors' items with the rarest commanding high prices. Some collect them for value or novelty but others for their magical aura as a charm. Fèves range from the traditional, like four-leafed clovers or horseshoes to the unusual—Harry Potter figurines or porcelain tiles depicting positions from the Kama Sutra.

Protection Against Striges

Ovid and Petronius recommended a ham and bean soup to antidote effects of the striges, including loss of male sexual vigor.

Jack and the Beanstalk

For the last two hundred years and more, the bean has held a place in the cult of fairy tales that few other plants can rival. The center of that hold is a single English tale, in which an impoverished widow sends her only son Jack to market to sell the family's last cow, and Jack instead trades the cow for a handful of magic beans. In some versions he meets a wizardly old man on the road; in others, a butcher who simply tricks the silly boy. The mother, when she hears, despairs, but Jack rises early the next morning and plants the beans in the garden anyway, muttering that his mother says they are nothing more than common scarlet runners. By dawn the beans have risen into a stalk that pierces the cloud line. Jack climbs it, finds a kingdom in the sky belonging to a giant, and brings down a bag of gold, a goose that lays golden eggs, and a singing harp. He cuts the stalk behind him. The story is older than its first published version, and the bean it names by species, the scarlet runner, ties the fairy tale directly to a real climbing plant grown in cottage gardens. See the page on Jack of the Beanstalk for the legendary figure himself.

The Scarlet Runner

The scarlet runner bean (Phaseolus coccineus) is native to the highlands of Mexico, Guatemala, Honduras, Nicaragua, and Panama. It was first domesticated in Mexico around 2500 BCE, with archaeological traces of its wild form going back roughly seven thousand years. It reached England in the early seventeenth century by way of an English botanist who traveled the world collecting plants for a queen's garden, and from there it spread across European cottage gardens. Anyone who has planted scarlet runners knows how fast they run: they climb anything they can reach and bloom vivid red flowers that give way to long pods. Inside the pods, the beans come in white, black, red, purple, and mottled deep-purple variations, jewel-like enough to make any child trade a cow.

The Tepary Bean

The tepary bean (Phaseolus acutifolius) is native to the American Southwest and northern Mexico and is among the most drought- and heat-tolerant crops on the continent. It can produce a harvest where almost no other bean would survive, and for that reason it has been an essential cultural and nutritional crop for many Indigenous nations of the region.

Coyote and the Milky Way

⚠️ A Diné (Navajo) story belonging to the originating culture; recorded here with respect, not as the practitioner's own. In one telling, Coyote is running with a bag full of tepary beans when he trips, and the beans scatter across the sky, becoming the Milky Way. In another telling, Coyote, impatient with the careful work of the First People who were placing the stars in the sky one by one out of the bag, snatched the bag and flung the whole thing into the heavens at once. Either way, the long, scattered band of stars overhead is, in this telling, a bean spill. See the entity page for Coyote.

The Three Sisters

The Indigenous companion-planting method known as the Three Sisters is a teaching about harmony made into a garden. Corn rises tall and gives the bean a stalk to climb. The bean, like every legume, draws nitrogen from the air through its roots and leaves a portion in the soil for the corn to feed on. Squash or pumpkin sprawls along the ground at the foot of both, shading the soil and keeping it cool and damp, and discouraging animals from grazing on the young plants. Eaten together, the three become a complete protein: corn is rich in vitamins but lacks amino acids; beans are full of the amino acids the corn does not have. See the ritual page for the Three Sisters Companion Planting method.

Words for Bean

Nearly every Indigenous language in the Western Hemisphere has a word for bean. Among the Anishinaabe (Ojibwe), the bean is called mashkodesimin, mashkodiisimin, miskodiisimin, or miskojiimin. Among the Ani'-Yun'wiya' (Cherokee), it is tu-ya. In Spanish, it is frijol or frijoles. In French, haricot. The word follows the bean across every soil it has ever been planted in.

Beans for the Dead

The ancient Greeks served beans alongside lettuces at funeral repasts, the meal eaten after a burial. The ancient Romans threw beans into the fire on the Lemuria, their May festival for the dangerous dead, and the head of the household walked the rooms barefoot at midnight spitting beans behind himself to redeem the family from haunting. The Italian fava-bean lineage carries the same logic forward into modern practice. Beans are food the dead can eat, and food that the living can put into the hands of the dead in exchange for safe passage.

Goddesses and Fruition

Beans are sacred to harvest goddesses across many lineages, and in old European practice the white-flowered varieties especially were associated with the goddesses of the land. The bean is a fertility symbol because it is full of stored life: a single seed will grow into a vine, and that vine into a season's worth of food. Carried as an amulet, it is one of the oldest portable protections in folk practice.

Divination and Oracles

Beans have been used in oracular practice for as long as they have been grown. They have been cast and read for yes-or-no questions, sorted by color for choices, drawn from a pouch as lots, and counted out for numbers. The fortune-teller's bean and the bean ritual for the solo fortune-teller listed in this page's rituals are two surviving examples; the love-oracle of seven beans is another. Approach bean divination as you would any cast lot: ask one clear question at a time, agree with yourself on the meaning of each outcome before you cast, and accept the answer the beans give you.

Bean Flower Water for the Skin

The distilled water of garden bean flowers has long been used as a wash for the face and skin, said to clean spots and ease wrinkles. To make it, gather fresh bean flowers in the early morning, cover them with clean water, and either distill the water or steep the flowers as a tea, strain, and store cold. Apply with a clean cloth.

Bean Flour for Wounds and for Nursing

Ground into a fine flour and mixed with water into a paste, bean flour serves as a poultice for swelling and inflammation around wounds, and is a traditional aid for engorgement or mastitis in nursing mothers. Apply the paste warm in a clean cloth across the affected area, leave on until the cloth cools, and renew as needed.

Kidney Stones and the Green Husk

The green husk of the bean pod, distilled in water, has been used in folk practice as a treatment for kidney stones. The same water, taken plainly, is a gentle diuretic.

A Word on Toxicity

Most beans of the Phaseolus genus contain a protein called phytohemagglutinin, which is toxic in large amounts when raw and causes nausea, vomiting, and gastric distress. The protein is destroyed by thorough cooking, especially in water with a pinch of wood ash added, a practice known to Indigenous cooks of the Americas long before any European herbal noted it. Soak dried beans, drain, and boil for at least ten full minutes before reducing to a simmer. Never eat raw beans of any variety. The separate, severe risk of favism with fava beans is noted in the warnings section above.

Bean Blossoms

Beans are magick plants, and Jack and the Beanstalk is only the most famous of the tales celebrating their power. The dried seed is used most often, but the beautiful, fleeting blossoms are also full of power. They can't be bought from a florist and must be grown at home.
