Muin

Grape (Vitis vinifera) is sacred to ecstatic rites and household plenty. The vine carries prosperity, fertility, and abundance in equal measure, and its fruit, raisins, and wine are among the oldest offerings laid on human altars. Where the grape ripens, the year ripens with it, and dreams come into fruition under its cluster.

Magickal Properties

Planet: Moon

Deities: Dionysus, Bacchus, Liber Pater, Semele, Hera, Zeus, Ninkasi, Hathor

Powers: Prosperity, Fertility, Abundance, Garden Magick, Mental Powers, Money, Ecstatic Devotion

Magickal Uses

Garden fertility: Paint or place images of grapes on garden walls to ensure fertility.

Fertility and mental power: Eating grapes or raisins increases fertility and strengthens mental powers.

Money magick: Place grapes on the altar during money spells.

Altar offering: Grapes, raisins, and wine are appropriate offerings to deities of the vine and the harvest; lay a cluster on the altar at the new moon or before any working aimed at increase.

Ritual consumption: A single grape eaten with intention can be a small but potent act of devotional magick; chew slowly and name the blessing you are asking the vine to carry.

The Wine That Came West

The first grape wine that we can name came out of the Caucasus and Iran and made its long way west, reaching Greece around 4500 BCE. From there it spread along every shoreline and over every hill where vines could be coaxed to root. The story of grape magick is therefore also the story of human travel, the slow drift of cuttings from one valley to the next, and the persistence of a single plant whose fruit could be both fresh food and lasting cellar.

Dionysus, Bacchus, and Liber Pater

The vine belongs first to Dionysus, son of Zeus and Semele, whose chariot is hung with grape leaves and whose breath, in one telling, can turn salt air to red wine. To the Romans he is Bacchus, god of wine, agriculture, fertility, and merrymaking, and to another corner of the Roman pantheon he is Liber, or Liber Pater, who carries the same gifts under a third name. Greek and Roman tellings braid these three together until they are nearly indistinguishable, all of them gods of revelry and of the chaos that revelry sets loose.

The Death of Semele and the Valley of Nysa

Dionysus came into the world through grief. Hera, jealous of her husband's lover, convinced Semele to ask Zeus to appear in his full thunderous glory; bound by an oath he had already given, Zeus revealed himself and Semele burned to nothing in the same instant. Before she was lost, Zeus rescued the unborn child and carried him away to the Valley of Nysa, where nymphs raised him out of reach of Hera's wrath. In that hidden valley, Dionysus learned to plant and tend the vine and taught the farmers around him how to coax wine from the harvest. The grape, in this story, is grown out of loss and into joy.

The Pirates and the Dolphins

Pirates once mistook the young Dionysus for a wealthy prince and seized him for ransom. When they tried to bind him to the mast, the ropes fell away of their own accord, and one of the crew, panicked, cried out that the boy must be a god. The rest scoffed until Dionysus blew a long breath across the deck, filling the sails and the boards with blood red wine. The crew flung themselves into the sea, and as they struck the water they were changed into dolphins, who swam away from the haunted ship. The story marks the vine as a power that does not suffer mockery: wine can flood a hull as easily as a cup.

The Bacchanalia and the Saturday Night to Come

The Roman festival of Bacchus, the Bacchanalia, was held in mid-March as an unbridled celebration of lust, drink, and divine madness, raised to ritual status. The Roman authorities tried to ban it and punish its followers, and yet the underlying impulse, the carving-out of one night where ordinary rules give way to dance, drink, and abandon, has never really been suppressed; it lives on in every weekend's release. Where wine flows past the point of decorum, the Bacchanalia is still being celebrated under another name.

Women and the Wild Cult

Dionysus, like the alcohol he gave, could rouse his followers to frenzy and riot. Women in particular were said to be drawn into his orbit, sometimes leaving children, households, and ordinary obligations behind to follow the god into the forest as Maenads. The vine, in this aspect, is a plant of dissolution as much as of plenty; it loosens what has been too tightly bound, for good and for ill.

The Fox and the Grapes

A fox once strolled through an orchard on a summer morning and saw a beautiful cluster of grapes ripened high on a vine trained over a tree. He leapt for them, fell short, drew back further, leapt again, and again, until at last he had to give up. He walked away with his nose in the air, declaring that the grapes must have been sour anyway. The little fable preserves a piece of grape wisdom that has nothing to do with wine: it is easy to despise what you cannot get, and the vine is happy to teach this lesson to anyone willing to be honest about their own reaching.

California Wild Grape

Several species of Vitis are native to North America. Vitis californica grows wild across California, and Vitis riparia ranges from central and eastern Canada down through the central and eastern United States. The Indigenous peoples of California have long worked with the wild grape as food, medicine, and craft material: a decoction of the steeped roots as a blood purifier, a root tea as emetic and cathartic, root fiber woven into baskets among the Hupa, stem bindings for basket rims among the Maidu, leaves laid in earth ovens by the Miwok, vine ties and baby-basket hoops shaped by the Southwestern Pomo. These traditions belong to those communities and are noted here only as cultural context; they are not for outsiders to practice.

Ninkasi of the Vine

Long before Dionysus tended his first row in Nysa, the Sumerians knew Ninkasi, goddess of intoxicating fruit and grapes in particular. One of her seven children grew into the goddess of beer, and the family between them held dominion over every drink that fermentation could yield. Where the grape is honored as a sacred crop rather than a casual snack, Ninkasi is the elder name at the head of the table.

Twelve Grapes at Midnight

A Spanish tradition, now practiced across the Spanish-speaking world, calls for eating twelve grapes in time with the twelve strokes of midnight on New Year's Eve. Each grape stands for one of the months ahead, and each swallow seals luck and prosperity into that month while turning bad luck away. The custom is small, fast, and beautifully effective: a year's worth of magick in the time it takes a clock to finish striking.

The Talking Grapes

A Hungarian tale tells of a king who set out for market and asked his three daughters what they wished him to bring home. The eldest asked for a gown of gold, the middle for a gown of silver, and the youngest for talking grapes, a smiling apple, and a tinkling apricot. The king found the gowns but not the fruits, and on the road home his carriage stuck fast in the mud. A pig offered to free the carriage in exchange for the youngest daughter, and the king, panicked, agreed.

The pig came to the palace the next day to claim what had been promised. The king tried to send down a peasant girl in silks, but the pig was not fooled; he tried again with his own daughter dressed in rags, and the pig was not fooled by that either, and carried her away by wheelbarrow to a pigsty. She cried herself to sleep on the straw and woke the next morning in a palace of fairy work, attended by maids. In the garden she met a handsome young man who told her everything she saw was hers, and there she heard the grapes calling to her to pick them and eat, saw an apple smiling, and heard an apricot tinkling like a bell. The prince had been bewitched into a pig, and only a woman who wished for talking grapes could break the spell. They lived happily and abundantly ever after, which is what the vine has always wished for those who learn its name.
