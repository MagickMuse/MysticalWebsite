Related Species

The genus Cedrus includes several closely related true cedars that share much of Lebanon Cedar's energy and scent. The most commercially important cedarwood oil today comes from Atlas Cedar (Cedrus atlantica), believed to have originated from the Lebanon cedars themselves. The Himalayan Deodar Cedar (C. deodara) is a third close relative whose oil is chemically very similar.

The trees sold as Texas Cedarwood and Virginian Cedar are actually Juniperus species, botanically and chemically distinct from true Cedrus even though they share the cedarwood name.

Historical & Sacred Uses

Used by ancient Egyptians, Babylonians, Greeks, and many others as an offertory incense burned during sacrifices. Sacred to Astarte and associated with the Summer Solstice. Several Native American peoples burn cedar to repel evil spirits and attract good ones. The tree represents sovereignty, power, and longevity. Sacred to Jupiter.

Fresh cedar branches are used as brooms in purification and exorcisms of a temple or ritual area. Cedarwood oil was used by the ancient Celts and others to preserve trophy heads. Red cedar needles and oil are used in perfumery, and the sawdust in incense making.

Groves of the Gods

As oak groves were held sacred in ancient Greece and across Britain, so cedar groves were sacred in their native Middle East and North Africa, where like oracular rites were performed. The Epic of Gilgamesh names the cedar woods as a dwelling place of the gods. Long-lived, towering, dark, and imposing, the Cedar of Lebanon in particular reads as a symbol of longevity and nobility, while its tough and durable wood makes it an emblem of incorruptibility and purity. Its timber raised the Temple in Jerusalem. Like all evergreens it speaks of immortality, and it is counted among the trees said to have furnished the Cross, so that Christ might share in the tree's incorruptibility and purity. The great forest that once mantled Mount Lebanon has now dwindled to a few scattered stands.

In Rome the statues of the gods were carved from cedar, its qualities fitting for so sacred a use, and its resin preserved the heads of celebrated enemies, an echo of the older Egyptian embalming, for which the antiseptic, sweet-scented oil made cedar an apt ingredient and a symbol of rebirth. The Assyrians held that the secret names of the gods were written upon the cedar itself, and they hung its sticky, egg-shaped cones as charms to keep demons at bay. Its fragrant wood is burned as incense, and among Jewish people it is burned to mark the New Year.

A classic temple wood across cultures; strong, clean smoke for rites and house‑clearing.
