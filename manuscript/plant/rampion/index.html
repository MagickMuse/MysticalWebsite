Rampion is a meadow and garden plant whose long history is tied as much to the supper table as to the witch's garden. The name Rapunzel is the German word for this plant, and the Grimm Brothers took it for the heroine of their best-known fairy tale, in which a pregnant woman's craving for rampion seals her child's fate.

Characteristics

Rampion grows in lush clumps of emerald green leaves, sending up slender flower spikes hung with small, star-shaped, pale purple bellflowers. The root is somewhat radish-like in texture and taste.

Medicinal Uses

Rampion is edible from leaf to root. It acts as an anti-inflammatory and has been used to bring down fevers. The leaves carry a strong supply of vitamin C, and the roots have been used to help regulate blood sugar. As a leafy green it is a good source of calcium and iron, which may be why pregnant women in folk tradition were said to crave it so fiercely, since anemia is common in pregnancy.

Magickal Properties

  • Powers: Invoking fairies and nature spirits, love, attraction

Rampion is an unsung hero of the meadow and garden. Magickally it is used to call in the gentler fairy and nature spirits, and it can be added to love spells. The roots are carved into small amulets to draw the eye of a would-be lover. As a binding love charm it is gentler and less powerful than mandrake.

Rapunzel in the Witch's Garden

The Grimm Brothers' tale turns on this plant entirely. A pregnant woman looks down from her window into a witch's walled garden and sees a bed of rampion so green and fresh that she is seized with a craving she cannot shake. She tells her husband she will die without it. He scales the wall at twilight and brings her a handful, and she devours it as a salad. Her craving only grows, and on his second trip the witch catches him. He pleads his wife's hunger, and the witch agrees to give him all the rampion he wants in exchange for the child the woman is carrying.

The baby is born, the witch claims her at once and names her Rapunzel, and at twelve the girl is shut into a tower in the woods that has no stairs and no door, only a high window. The witch climbs up Rapunzel's long braids when she calls, "Rapunzel, Rapunzel, let down your hair." A prince hears her singing, learns the trick, and visits in secret, bringing her skeins of silk to weave into a ladder. Rapunzel lets slip how much heavier the witch is to pull up than the king's son, and in fury the witch cuts off her hair, casts her into a far desert, and waits for the prince. He climbs the braids, sees the witch, leaps from the window into brambles that blind him, and wanders the woods for years until he stumbles on the desert where Rapunzel lives. Her tears fall into his eyes and restore his sight, and they return to his kingdom together.

Whether the witch knew the pull of the rampion all along and used it to seduce a woman she already knew was carrying a child is a question the tale leaves open.

Gateway Between Worlds

Though rampion was largely cultivated as a vegetable crop, it carries quiet mystical weight. There is a belief that the plant is a gateway between worlds. In an old Calabrian story, a young girl pulls a rampion up out of the earth and finds a staircase that descends into the realm of the fairies.

Offering to Apollo

The ancient Greeks treated rampion as a funereal vegetable and made offerings of it on golden plates to Apollo. For all its humble cousinship with the radish, this is in truth an enchanted plant.
