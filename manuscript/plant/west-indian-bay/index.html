West Indian Bay (Pimenta racemosa) is a tropical evergreen tree of the Caribbean. Its aromatic leaves are the source of bay rum.

Characteristics

A wild-growing evergreen tree reaching about 8 metres in height, with large leathery leaves and small aromatic fruits. The whole tree carries a warm, spicy fragrance reminiscent of cloves, cinnamon, and allspice rolled together.

Origin & Habitat

Native to the West Indies, with the finest essential oil produced on Dominica. The tree thrives in warm, humid coastal forests across the Caribbean.

Related Species

West Indian Bay is part of a small family of aromatic trees:

  • Anise-scented Bay and Lemon-scented Bay: regional varieties of Pimenta racemosa whose oils carry dramatically different chemistry, dominated by anethole or citral rather than eugenol.
  • Allspice / Pimento (Pimenta dioica): a close cousin often grown in the same groves. The dried fruits of both are sometimes blended to make household allspice.

Despite the shared common name, West Indian Bay should not be confused with Bay Laurel (Laurus nobilis), the Mediterranean culinary herb, nor with Bayberry or Wax Myrtle (Morella cerifera), the wax-yielding North American shrub. The three plants are unrelated and have very different uses.

Folk Tradition

West Indian Bay tree groves are often interplanted with allspice, and the dried fruits of both are sometimes pounded together for kitchen spice. The tree's most famous gift, however, is bay rum: a hair and skin tonic made by distilling the fresh leaves in rum. Long valued as a fragrant, scalp-stimulating lotion, bay rum has been a barbershop staple for over a century, particularly recommended for greasy or lifeless hair.
