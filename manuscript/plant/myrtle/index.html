Myrtle (Myrtus communis) is a classic Venus herb for enduring love, youth, fertility, peace, and prosperity. It belongs to the Myrtaceae family.

Characteristics

A large bush or small tree with many tough but slender branches, brownish-red bark, and small sharp-pointed leaves. It bears white flowers followed by small black berries. Both the leaves and the flowers are very fragrant.

Origin & Distribution

Native to North Africa, myrtle now grows freely all over the Mediterranean region and is cultivated as a garden shrub throughout Europe. The essential oil is mainly produced in Corsica, Spain, Tunisia, Morocco, Italy, Yugoslavia, and France.

Related Species

Myrtle belongs to the same large aromatic family as eucalyptus and tea tree. Other plants known as myrtles include bayberry or wax myrtle (Myrica cerifera) and the Dutch myrtle or English bog myrtle (Myrica gale), both used in herbal medicine, although their essential oils are said to be poisonous. Myrtle should not be confused with iris, sometimes called 'myrtle flower,' or with calamus, which is also known as 'myrtle grass' or 'sweet myrtle.'

Folk Tradition

The leaves and berries have been used historically for 'drying and binding, good for diarrhoea and dysentery, spitting of blood and catarrhous defluctions upon the breast.' Dioscorides prescribed myrtle for lung and bladder infections in the form of an extract made by macerating the leaves in wine. The leaves and flowers were a major ingredient of 'angel's water,' a sixteenth-century skin care lotion. Recorded actions include anticatarrhal, antiseptic for urinary and pulmonary use, astringent, balsamic, bactericidal, expectorant, regulator, and slightly sedative.

Magickal Properties

Deities: Venus, Artemis, Aphrodite, Hathor, Astarte, Ashtoreth, Marian

Powers: Love, Fertility, Youth, Peace, Money

Magickal Uses

  • Love kept and renewed: Worn to preserve love; added to all love sachets and spells, especially to keep love alive and exciting.
  • Fertility balances: Worn to increase fertility, yet traditionally also worn by brides at weddings to avoid conceiving too quickly.
  • Youthfulness: Myrtle wood carried preserves youth; a cup of myrtle tea drunk every three days preserves youthfulness (must be kept up without fail).
  • House blessing: Grown on each side of the house, myrtle brings love and peace; lucky in window boxes when planted there by a woman.
  • Money: Used in money spells.

Love, Death, and the Goddess

Myrtle is counted among the plants of true power, and a wand cut from myrtle wood is said to let its bearer speak with the spirit world the shrub is bound to. The plant carries a double meaning, standing at once for death and for love, and it is sacred above all to Aphrodite. When the goddess first came ashore at Paphos she is said to have hidden her beauty behind a myrtle bush, just as her Roman counterpart Venus sheltered behind the same shrub from the satyrs who pursued her. The flower's five petals mark it as the Goddess's own.

In ancient Greece myrtle carried frank sexual meaning, sharing its name with a word for the female genitals, and the shape of its bud was likened to the vesica piscis. From this it grew into the flower of love and of lovers and found its way into bridal bouquets, a custom the Church long frowned upon as a coarse and pagan relic. An old wedding tradition has the bride pass a cutting of myrtle from her own bouquet to her bridesmaids, so that they too may find love and so that the love between bride and groom will never die.

Because it joins love and death in a single emblem, myrtle came to stand for a love able to outlast death itself. Those who suffered the ache of unrequited love, or who died unmarried, were said to have earned the right to a sprig of myrtle laid upon the grave, and with it the hope of finding love at last in the Afterlife.

Meaning When Gifted

When myrtle is gifted it carries the meaning of love and joy, and it serves as the Hebrew emblem of marriage.
