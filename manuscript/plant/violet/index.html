History

Violets have been cultivated for over two thousand years. The word violet traces back to the Greek ion, itself derived from the name of one of Zeus's mortal mistresses, weaving the flower into the oldest threads of Greek myth.

In classical mythology, it was while gathering violets that Persephone, daughter of Demeter the earth mother, was abducted. Hades arose from a fissure in the ground, so enamored of the maiden goddess that he took her as his queen without consent. The loss sent Demeter into a grief so profound that all plant life on earth withered and died. Such was the destruction that Zeus himself intervened, petitioning Hades for Persephone's return. Because Persephone had eaten pomegranate seeds during her captivity — unknowingly binding herself to the realm of the dead — she was permitted to return to the living world for only part of the year. The seasons themselves are the by-product of that arrangement: spring and summer belong to the reunion of mother and daughter; autumn and winter mark Persephone's return to the underworld.

The violet, then, is a flower of threshold and transformation — it was present at the moment the world changed, when death and fertility were first joined together, and when the agricultural cycle of the earth was set irreversibly into motion.

The Shrinking Violet

For all its tiny size and its modesty, growing low to the ground and hiding among the long grasses, which gives us the phrase "shrinking violet," the flower fairly sparkles out from its hiding place. The violet was sacred to Aphrodite, goddess of love, but its very modesty and humility later made it a fitting emblem of the Virgin Mary, all the more so because the little plant is self-pollinating and so serves as a reminder of the Virgin Birth. Mohammed called the violet a symbol of the truth of his teachings, since it spreads quickly and flowers twice a year.

A Flower of Death

The scented violet, Viola odorata, carries a marvelous perfume and enters into perfumes, love charms, and potions. The violet is also bound to death and the spirit world, said to grow in the fields where Persephone walked before the ground swallowed her, and its purple color is itself a symbol of death.

Characteristics

Prized for their color, fragrance, and both culinary and medicinal value, violets prefer cool, damp conditions and should be planted in early spring or late August to establish roots before summer heat or winter frost. They bloom from April through May and again in late autumn. Medicinally, violets have been used to ease headaches, constipation, and coughs, and applied topically to nourish the skin.

Medicinal Uses

Parts used: leaves and flowers. Violet has been used as an emollient and a laxative.
