Cabbage (Brassica oleracea) is a leafy vegetable with tightly packed leaves forming a round head. Wild cabbage grows in England and France, while the widely cultivated cabbage of most gardens today traces its native range to the Middle East. In lore and old botanicals, cabbage is sometimes called coleworts, a term that can also gather in kale and other related brassicas.

Magickal Uses

For marital luck: Cabbage should be planted in the garden the first thing after a couple has been married, if they wish to have good luck in their marriage and garden.

Housewarming blessing: Bring a fresh cabbage plant into a new home as a blessing and to draw prosperity to the household and garden.

Flight and fairy magick: Cabbage stalks and stumps were favored vessels for fairy travel and the practice of flight.

Folk Medicine

Cabbage carries fiber, potassium, and vitamins C and K, and acts as an anti-inflammatory. It also lowers blood pressure. Topically, cabbage treats a wide range of ailments including bruises and sprains. An old remedy lays cabbage leaves on the chest of a nursing mother suffering from clogged milk ducts, known as mastitis. Boiled cabbage in one dose will open the body, while the second dose will bind it. Cabbage juice taken in wine was used to treat the bite of an adder. Red cabbage holds an especially high concentration of anthocyanins, with thirty-six varieties of these antioxidants identified in a single head, and crushed cabbage leaves have been applied topically for bruises, sprains, inflammation, mastitis, and rheumatic pain. The ancient Greeks turned to cabbage as a remedy against intoxication from the fruit of the vine.

Halloween Divination

In Scotland and parts of Ireland, cabbages are used for divination on Halloween night. The plant is pulled from the ground, and the roots reveal the figure and nature of the future partner. In Scotland, young women are blindfolded and pull cabbages on Hallowe'en to learn the size and shape of their husbands to come.

Babies in the Cabbage Patch

The old saying that a baby is in the cabbage patch, or that a sweet-faced child was found in a cabbage patch, anchors the plant to fertility and abundance. Cabbages thrive nearly anywhere, and that wild generosity is part of why they carry the current of fertility.

Flight and the Good People

A cabbage stalk or stump was a favored means of transport for the fairy folk, who would travel on them through the air. In Irish fairy legend, a gardener named Crowley was thought to be in cahoots with the Good People and grew exhausted each day because every night the fairies carried him on a night journey atop one of his own cabbage stumps.

The Donkey Cabbage

In an old fairy tale, a weary hunter, in some versions a soldier, walks home hungry and happens upon a garden of nothing but cabbages and vegetables. After a few mouthfuls he sprouts four legs, a thick head, and two long ears, finding himself transformed into a donkey. The juicy salad still tastes good to him in his new shape, so he keeps eating, until he reaches another kind of cabbage that returns him to human form. He then uses the magickal cabbages to trick a lovely maiden and a witch alike into the same donkey shape, teaching each of them a lesson. As with the apple tales, the cure for an unwanted donkey form is more cabbage.

The Cabbage to the Sky

In a Russian fairy tale, an old husband and wife each plant a cabbage-head. The wife's, set in the ash-hole, withers entirely; the husband's, set in the cellar under the cottage floor, grows up through the floor, then through the ceiling, then up to the sky, the old man cutting holes with his hatchet as it climbs. He follows the stalk into a heavenly land of pie, cake, and stew and eats his fill. He returns and tells his wife, who climbs into a sack so he can carry her up by his teeth. She grows impatient on the way and asks if they are nearly there; when he answers her, the sack falls from his mouth, and the wife with it. He finds only her shattered bones inside. A fox happens by, offers to cure her, and tells the old man to heat the bathhouse, bring her there with a bag of oatmeal and a pot of butter, and then stand outside without looking in. The fox makes a pudding of her bones, eats her all, and runs off, leaving the old man in poverty. For gardeners, the practical thread is that wood ash from the hearth helps control cutworms and cabbage maggots, the common pests that damage the crop.

Findhorn and the Forty-Two-Pound Cabbage

The spiritual community of Findhorn in northern Scotland became known for growing oversized vegetables in sandy, inhospitable soil, including reports of forty-two-pound cabbages and sixty-pound broccoli plants, sixty-five vegetables, forty-two herbs, and twenty-one fruits in conditions that should not have supported them. Their success was attributed to working in partnership with fairies, devas, and elementals, with one founding member said to keep ongoing council with the Elf King and other highly ranked members of the fairy kingdom. Findhorn continues today as a spiritual community offering retreats focused on reconnecting with nature.

Saintly Fare

Certain saints were said to subsist almost entirely on cabbages. In an effort to outdo Saint Julian Sabas, who lived on bread and salt water, Saint Macarius of Egypt ate only a few cabbage leaves every Sunday.

Cool-Season Crop

Cabbages thrive in mild climates and can be grown year-round where summers stay cool, or in early spring and fall where summers are hot. Along with broccoli, cauliflower, and Brussels sprouts, cabbage is a cool-season brassica. It became a staple of Irish cooking long before the potato arrived in Ireland in the late 1500s, when cabbages and turnips kept tables fed. Salad greens such as radicchio, endive, lettuce, arugula, and kale share the same cool-season preference and can be grown on a similar schedule to the brassicas.
