Red Clover (Trifolium pratense) is a sacred woman's herb of purification, luck, and Triple Goddess devotion, whose three-lobed leaf has long represented divine trinity.

History

Red clover is cited by both Pliny the Elder and the herbalist John Gerard. Its therapeutic reputation shifted over the centuries, but in the early nineteenth century it was prescribed for conditions ranging from syphilis and psoriasis to eczema and anxiety. The distinctive shape of its three-lobed leaves, meeting at a single shared point, has long been read as a symbol of divinity, whether the maiden-mother-crone trinity or the father-son-spirit.

Characteristics

Considered a woman's herb, red clover is a perennial that grows up to sixteen inches tall and bears pinkish-purple flowers from late spring through early fall. It is widely foraged and grows abundantly in the wild. Medicinally, it is frequently cited as a remedy for menopausal symptoms including hot flashes and breast tenderness.

Magickal Properties

Powers: Protection, Money, Love, Fidelity, Exorcism, Success

Magickal Uses

  • Greek names: The Greeks called clover meli (honey) and lotos (clover), reflecting its sweetness and sacred nature.
  • For youth and beauty: The three-leaved clover is used in rituals designed to help one look youthful and fair.
  • Military avoidance: Four-leaved clovers are carried to avoid military service.
  • Earth offering: Remember to leave something in payment to the earth if you take an entire plant.
  • Healing: Use in healing workings; all four elements are present in clover, making it a powerful all-purpose herb.
  • Protection: Clover is carried to protect against evil and hexes. The three leaves represent the Triple Goddess or the Holy Trinity, providing divine protection.
  • Money: Red clover is added to prosperity baths and sachets to attract wealth and financial success.
  • Love and fidelity: White clover is used in love spells. Wear clover to be faithful in love and to ensure your partner's fidelity.
  • Four-leaf clover: The rare four-leaf clover is extremely lucky and protective. It enables one to see faeries, detect witches, avoid military service, and preserve sanity. Each leaf represents fame, wealth, love, and health.
  • Success: Carry clover when going to court or business dealings for a favorable outcome.
  • Otherworld: Clover, especially the four-leaf variety, opens pathways to the Otherworld and the realm of the fae.
  • Purification and cleansing: Red clover infusions are used to cleanse ritual tools and sacred space. Brew the herb as a tea, allow it to cool, and decant it into a spray bottle for an easy and effective space-clearing mist.
  • Triple Goddess and crone: Red clover is sacred to the crone aspect of the Triple Goddess. Its triple leaves embody the three faces of the goddess and make it appropriate for workings honoring wisdom, endings, and transformation.

Names and Etymology

The Latin name Trifolium translates as three leaves. The English word clover is believed to derive from the Anglo-Saxon cloefer. Shamrock comes from the Irish seamróg, pronounced sham-rog, sham-rouge, or sham-raug depending on the dialect. Some hold that a shamrock has three leaves only and that the four-leaf form belongs to the clover proper, yet folk usage runs both ways, and the four-leaf shamrock, Seamrógna gCeithre, appears throughout Irish writing. The plants sold around Saint Patrick's Day in the United States as shamrocks or Irish clover are often not Trifolium at all but oxalis, which can share the common name wood sorrel.

Trifolium Species

The genus Trifolium contains more than three hundred species. Several are native to Ireland, including Trifolium dubium, T. repens, and T. pratense. Trifolium stoloniferum is one of several species native to the United States and is currently endangered.

Medicinal Uses

Clover has been brewed for centuries as a tea for bronchitis, colds, and coughs. Topically it has been applied to a host of skin conditions, including psoriasis and insect bites. The challenge in modern foraging is finding clover that has not been treated with pesticides or other agricultural chemicals.

Protection Against Curses

Clover protects against curses and evil enchantments and lets the bearer see threats clearly. It is carried in love and protection spells, and it enables the wearer to see sorcerers, witches, and cunning folk for who they truly are.

The Sign of the Trinity

Three is a powerful number in witchcraft. The shamrock has been read as the holy trinity of Father, Son, and Holy Ghost, and equally as the triple face of Mother Nature in maiden, mother, and crone. The four-leaved clover carries its own current of luck, love, hope, and faith. Its possessor was once believed to gain a host of supernatural gifts: luck in gambling and racing, immunity from being cheated in a bargain or deceived, and a shield against witchcraft. The clover must be kept and kept close. Bragging about it, sharing it, or giving it away diminishes its power.

The Symbolism of the Leaves

The clover stands for protection, fertility, and abundance. Brought into the home or worn on the body, it serves as a charm to keep witches away, and its three distinctly heart-shaped leaves add to its benevolent reputation. The shamrock, the form of clover bound up with all things Irish, was known as the shamrakh in Arabic countries, where it symbolized the triple aspect of the Goddess. Early Christian missionaries such as Saint Patrick used both shamrock and clover to illustrate the three-in-one nature of the Trinity.

Each leaf count carries its own meaning. The four-leafed clover or shamrock holds all the symbolism of the number 4: finding one means the bearer can see faeries and witches, recognize evil spirits, and gain protection from them. A rare five-leafed clover, spotted only by the sharp-eyed, carries the symbolism of the number 5, which joins the feminine 2 with the masculine 3 and so signifies marriage and engagements.

Scotland, Wales, and the Romans

Ireland is not the only country to revere clover. Scotland, Wales, and England all hold similar beliefs in its powers in both three- and four-leafed form. The ancient Romans considered it a sign of protection and luck.

The Milkmaid's Four-Leaf Clover

A Cornish tale tells of a young milkmaid coming back from milking with a pail so heavy she could barely lift it. She stooped down and gathered grass and clover into a pad for her head to balance the load. The moment she touched the clover, she found herself surrounded by hundreds of little people dipping the clover blooms into her milk and drinking from them, and gathering buttercups, foxgloves, and convolvulus blossoms beneath the cow's udder to catch the last drops. When she made it home, her mother told her she must have laid a four-leaf clover on her head.

Mary's Shamrock and the Tin Can

An Irish tale tells of a poor widow whose only son was dying of plague with not a drop of milk to wet his tongue. At the court a lady gave her a tin can that would never empty so long as she or her son lived, on the condition that she keep the secret. She was told to drop a morsel of Mary's shamrock, a four-leaved shamrock, into the milk and give it to her son. He rose well within the hour. The widow walked the villages with the can, and no one she gave a drink to went away unhealed.

The Elfin Knight of the Scottish Moors

A Highland tale tells of a lone moor haunted by an Elfin Knight who appeared once every seven years and carried off any mortal who crossed the ground. Two young earls, St. Clair and Gregory, rode out to hunt there. St. Clair remembered that one who wore the Sign of the Blessed Trinity was safe from the Knight's spells, so he bound a leaf of clover to his arm with a silken scarf. Gregory mocked him and trusted his bow. Only St. Clair could see that the rider on the moor did not touch the ground but was carried by great pinions like a bird's. Gregory chased the Knight across burn and moss into a circle of dancing eldritch goblins, drank from an emerald goblet of heather ale that never ran low, and fell as if dead. St. Clair, shielded by the trefoil, kept vigil through the freezing night. He paced nine slow circles around the enchanted ring, walked through it without speaking, lifted the goblet from the Elfin King's marble table, and broke the enchantment. The bog vanished, and the two friends woke on solid ground.

A Resilient Field Plant

Trifoliums are incredibly resilient plants, thriving in poor soils, growing in a wide range of conditions, and bouncing back from long periods of dormancy. Clovers and micro-clovers are increasingly mixed into lawns as an alternative to water-hungry turf. Micro-clovers give a lush, lawn-like appearance with almost no maintenance, and clover lawns produce blossoms that feed bees and other pollinators. Some species have become invasive in the United States, so cultivating species native to your growing zone is the safer choice.

First Nations Uses

Many Indigenous peoples of the Americas regard local Trifolium species as a food and medicine source. ⚠️ This is a closed practice. The leaves are primarily eaten raw and the roots boiled. The roots of wild clover, Trifolium tridentatum and other Trifolium species, are an important food for some of the Pacific Northwestern First Nations, and used as food and medicine for the Southwestern Pomo of northern California. As medicine, a decoction of the blossoms is drunk to reduce vomiting.
