Plant Grimoire

Tree Magick

Since ancient times, mythology and legend have spoken of magickal trees. Early Greek myths held that certain trees could predict the future. The Druids considered trees sacred and performed rituals in oak groves. The Celts believed the sacred World Tree connected the upper, middle, and lower worlds. The Buddha gained enlightenment beneath a Bodhi tree.

Trees are the pillars of the natural world. They anchor the ground and seem to hold up the sky. They form the backbone of the green witch's practice.

Working with Wood

Although witches and herbalists tend to focus on herbs, they also work with wood when they want to create stability or permanence:

  • Wands and staffs - Traditionally crafted from wood
  • Protective amulets - Made from sticks and twigs
  • Talismans - Carved from rounds cut from branch cross-sections
  • Ritual fires - Sacred woods combined, particularly at Yule

Depending on your purpose, you can work with the bark, leaves, and/or inner wood of trees.

Fresh Wood vs. Deadfall

When you use fresh wood, you capture vibrant life energy, which may be exactly what a particular ritual or spell calls for. Deadfall is wood the tree has shed as no longer useful, and it may not carry the same vibrant charge. The choice depends on your personal view and the nature of the working.

🌳
Harvesting Etiquette: If you plan to cut fresh wood, first ask the tree for permission. If you sense the tree is receptive, proceed with care and respect. Make an offering in return, just as you would when harvesting any other plant material. Thank the tree before you leave.

The Sacred, Living Tree

It is not hard to see why the tree is revered the world over as a sacred, living being. Trees are not only beautiful but benevolent, giving food in nuts, fruits, and syrups, medicines (aspirin first came from willow bark), and timber to raise houses, ships, and carriages as well as weapons and tools. They have a darker office too, for their wood made the gallows and their branches the place of execution.

The tree holds all the elements within it. Its roots reach into the Earth that nourishes it; its rising sap is the water element, the dew of Heaven and the tree's own lifeblood; its leaves and branches stand high in the air; and fire is both struck from its rubbed sticks and fed by its wood. Greater still is its work of breathing, for the tree processes the air, and to destroy the rainforests is to cut a vital organ from the body of the world. The greatest forests, like the coastal redwoods of northern California, raise their own atmosphere about them. By the Doctrine of Signatures, the old teaching that a plant carries the qualities of what it resembles, the meaning is plain: a bare branching tree looks like a great upturned lung, and the trees are indeed the lungs of the planet.

The Spirit of the Tree

Little wonder that our ancestors believed each tree held its own powerful spirit, a belief that has never truly faded. To bring an evergreen into the home at Christmas is a living echo of it. In pre-Christian Britain trees were honoured at the Beltane festivals, dressed with ribbons and danced about; the Romans banned the rite, but it returned in the maypole. Before felling a living tree our forebears thought it needful to appease its spirit, and the custom has by no means died out. The Druids are remembered above all for their worship of trees, and their very name shares a root with the word for oak, the tree they revered most. Yet tree worship has been kept the world over. At Dodona the birds in the sacred trees carried messages from the Gods, and the trees were the focus of a divination called phyllomancy, in which meaning was read from the rustle of leaves in the wind.

The World Tree

Every people has held its own most sacred tree, what we might call its World Tree. For the Scandinavians it is the great ash Yggdrasil, which binds the cosmos together; the Siberians venerate the birch, the Germans the lime they called the Linden, the Buddha found enlightenment beneath the Bodhi tree, and the sage Lao Tzu was said to be born beneath a plum.

The idea of a World Tree, a giant tree growing through the cosmos to link the Heavens, the Earth, and the Underworld, is wholly universal, a primal archetype akin to the Axis Mundi and the Omphalos. It speaks not only of a striving toward the Heavens but of the endless turning of life, death, and rebirth, and the birds in its high branches are the souls of beings yet unborn. Its symbolism reaches far: it stands for the family, the Kabbalah itself is pictured as a tree, and in alchemy the World Tree appears as a naked woman crowned with sprouting, fruiting branches, wands in her hands, the Sun and Moon at her right and left.

The inverted tree, its roots in the air, is a sacred image now largely forgotten. It is set in the centre of the Green Man Maze in Wales and carries the teaching of the Upanishads that the whole Universe is an upside-down tree, its roots in the Heavens and its branches embracing the Earth. The same picture appears in the Kabbalah, in the ten spheres of the Sephiroth and the twenty-two paths that join them.

The Christmas Tree

Among the happiest of all tree symbols is the Christmas tree, now bound to the Christian feast of the birth of Christ though its roots run back into far older pre-Christian custom. The old pagan practice of bringing a living tree indoors in the depths of the dark months and dressing it with candles and trinkets was carried to Britain by Prince Albert, husband of Queen Victoria, and the idea swiftly took hold. To bring evergreen boughs and branches from the cold outside into the warmth within was a piece of sympathetic magick, meant to call back the spring and the growing season. The timing mattered greatly: the tree should not come in before Christmas Eve, and all the greenery had to be down and the tree gone by Twelfth Night on the sixth of January, a reckoning the department stores, with their artificial trees set out from September, no longer keep.

The tree is by tradition an evergreen, most often a spruce or a fir, and it stands for immortality and everlasting life, while the lights strung upon it promise that even in the darkest season the lighter days are near at hand. The northern Yule had its echoes in the south, where the Roman savior god Atys, or Attis, lived a story that closely mirrors that of Christ. Born on the twenty-fifth of December to a virgin mother, he was sacrificed for the sake of mankind, slain beneath a conifer, and lay three days and nights in the tomb before his resurrection. His priests, the dendrophori or tree bearers, would choose a conifer from a sacred grove to bring indoors in memory of the death and resurrection of Atys, who is himself linked with Apollo, the Sun God.

The Three Blessed Fruits

In the Buddhist tradition three fruits are honoured together as the Three Blessed Fruits, each standing for one of the Three Great Blessings: the citrus for happiness, the peach for longevity, and the pomegranate for fecundity.
