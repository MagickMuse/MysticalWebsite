History

Valerian has been in use as a calming agent for well over a thousand years. Its root produces a notably strong odor, which has given rise to the legend that it was the substance the Pied Piper used to lure the rats from Hamelin — its pungent scent reportedly being highly attractive to rodents.

Characteristics

A tall perennial herb, valerian has a hollow stem and blooms with either white or reddish flowers. It is also known as garden heliotrope. The medicinally active parts — the rhizome and root hairs — are harvested in the autumn during the plant's second year of growth.

Origin & Distribution

Native to Europe and parts of Asia, and naturalized in North America. Mainly cultivated in Belgium for its oil, with additional production in France, Holland, England, Scandinavia, the western Balkans, Hungary, and China.

Other Names & Related Species

There are over 150 species of valerian found in different parts of the world. The Eastern varieties differ slightly from the Western types: the oil from the Japanese plant called kesso root (V. officinalis) is more woody, while the oil from the Indian valerian (V. wallichii) is more musky. Closely related to Spikenard (Nardostachys jatamansi).

Folk Tradition

Valerian has been highly esteemed since medieval times, when it was called all heal. In the West it has been used for a variety of complaints, especially those involving nervous tension or restlessness, such as insomnia, migraine, dysmenorrhoea, intestinal colic, rheumatism, and as a pain reliever. On the Continent the oil has been used for cholera, epilepsy, and skin complaints. In China it is used for backache, colds, menstrual problems, bruises, and sores. The root is recognised in herbal pharmacopoeia as a specific for conditions presenting nervous excitability.

Magickal Uses

  • Protection and sleep: Powdered root used in protective sachets, hung in the home to guard against lightning, and placed in pillows to aid sleep.
  • Attraction: A sprig pinned to clothing causes would-be suitors to follow.
  • Love: Root added to love sachets; calms quarrels when introduced into the area.
  • Banishing: The dried root is a powerful tool for banishing and dispelling unwanted energy from a space, person, or situation.
  • Dream work: Valerian induces a deeply relaxed state that supports dream work and lucid dreaming. It is not typically brewed as an infusion due to its strong odor — instead the ground root is usually consumed directly, often in capsule form.
  • Exorcism and purification: Hung to dispel evil; powdered root sometimes used as “graveyard dust.”

Magickal Teas

Valerian root can be brewed as a magickal tea to support Crone magics, draw love, strengthen protection, aid purification, and promote rest and sleep. Drink 1 to 2 cups only during the spell event itself.

Meaning When Gifted

When valerian is gifted it carries the meaning of an accommodating disposition.
