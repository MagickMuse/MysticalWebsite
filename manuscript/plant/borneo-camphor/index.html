Borneo Camphor (Dryobalanops aromatica) is a Southeast Asian tree whose trunk yields a crystalline resin called borneol.

Characteristics

A truly majestic evergreen, often growing over 25 metres tall with a thick trunk that can reach 2 metres in diameter. Borneol itself is a natural exudation found beneath the bark in the crevices and fissures of some mature trees (only about 1 percent of trees produce it). Young trees instead produce a clear yellow liquid known as liquid camphor.

Origin & Distribution

Native to the islands of Borneo and Sumatra.

Related Species

Borneo Camphor should not be confused with the Japanese or Formosa Camphor tree (Cinnamomum camphora), which is more commonly known in Europe and is considered relatively toxic. The two trees are unrelated and produce chemically distinct resins.

Folk Tradition

Borneol has long been regarded as a panacea across ancient Persia, India, and China. It was used as a powerful remedy against plague and other infectious diseases, as well as for stomach and bowel complaints. In China it was traditionally used for embalming, and the substance is still highly valued for ceremonial and funeral rites across the East. Marco Polo wrote about it in the thirteenth century, and the sixteenth-century Portuguese poet Camoens called borneol the "balsam of disease."

The wood itself is prized as building timber. Its scent naturally repels insects and ants, making it a treasured material for construction in its native range.
