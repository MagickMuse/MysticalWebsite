History

Witch hazel was introduced to early European settlers by Native Americans, who had long used it as a topical treatment for reducing inflammation. It is indigenous to the United States.

Characteristics

A late-blooming shrub, witch hazel produces bright yellow, threadlike flowers in late autumn — well past the time other plants have gone dormant and shed their leaves. Both the leaves and bark are used medicinally, prepared as a poultice or through steam distillation of the aerial parts.

Source — Specific Uses

Forked divining rods are used for finding water. The leaves, bark, and young twigs can be used as a tonic; also astringent, cleansing, styptic, and a slight sedative.

Magickal Uses

  • Protection: Bark and twigs carried or used to protect against evil influences.
  • Heart-mending: Carried to mend a broken heart and cool passions.
  • Wands and dowsing: Witch hazel is a traditional wood for crafting wands and dowsing rods, making it a key material in tools of divination and magical practice.
  • Divination and inspiration: Its long association with divining rods connects it to uncovering hidden truths and sparking inspired insight.
  • Love: Witch hazel is also associated with love, used in workings to draw affection and open the heart.
  • Aromatherapy preservative: Its distillation is a common ingredient in aromatherapy sprays, where it serves both a practical preservative function and carries its protective energy.

Additional Notes

Witch hazel's seedpods crack open spontaneously when ripe, ejecting seeds with surprising force. This "snapping" quality gives it the folk name "Snapping Hazelnut." Traditionally used in poultices for healing skin conditions and reducing inflammation.
