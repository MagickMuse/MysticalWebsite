Water lily belongs to the greater Nymphaeaceae family, which contains at least sixty different plants. The most common water lily is Nymphaea sp., including the fragrant N. odorata native to North America. The Egyptian lotus is N. lotus, and the sacred lotus is Nelumbo nucifera. The Amazonian giant is Victoria amazonica.

Medicinal Use

The sacred lotus (Nelumbo nucifera) is the most widely consumed water lily in traditional medicine. It is thought to ease anxiety and lower blood pressure, and has even been studied as a treatment for Alzheimer's. In traditional Chinese medicine, lotus seeds are added to a tonic wine to counteract diarrhea and help heal the spleen.

Magickal Meaning

The water lily, or lotus, can represent fertility, enlightenment, beauty, prosperity, power, wisdom, harmony, and knowledge. The color of the bloom can shift its meaning, but in any color the flower serves to connect the practitioner with divine wisdom and all water spirits. Because the water lily grows in mud yet purifies the water around it and offers blooms shaped like stars, it is said to make the world around it a better place.

Water Nymph

The very name of the water lily invokes the Nymphs, water spirits of Greek mythology. The Greeks also tell of a young woman who was madly in love with Heracles. She died of jealousy and was transformed into the water lily. The single bloom of the water lily represents learning, wisdom, beauty, and the divine feminine across many cultures. Though associated with fertility, it is not a symbol of virility.

Osiris and the Anti-Aphrodisiac

In ancient Egypt the lotus was believed to carry anti-aphrodisiacal qualities, owing to its association with Osiris, whose body is often shown adorned with them. Osiris was killed by his brother and chopped into pieces, but Isis collected all his parts, minus one, and revived him just enough to ensure he could pass on to Ra. Those seeking an aphrodisiac instead turned to the blue water lily. Early Egyptian tomb paintings show these lotus flowers cultivated in ponds alongside Papyrus.

In Hindu Imagery

The lotus plays heavily in Hindu mythology. Sarasvati, goddess of knowledge, creativity, art, music, and wisdom, associated with rivers and small bodies of water, is often depicted seated on a white lotus blossom. Lakshmi, goddess of power, beauty, prosperity, and fortune, stands or sits on a pink lotus. Ganesha, the elephant-headed god who removes obstacles and brings fortune, is also seen on a pink lotus.

The Lotus of Enlightenment

In Buddhist teaching, sacred to the Buddha, the lotus represents enlightenment. The plant grows in mud yet purifies the water around it while bringing forth great beauty and nourishment.

Edible Lotus

The rhizomes of the lotus are eaten fresh, cooked, fried, dried, or pickled. The flowers, seeds, and embryos can also be eaten. In China, Korea, and Vietnam a tea is made from the roots, flowers, and seeds. In Korea it is called yeonhwa-cha.

Naia and the Giant Water Lily

The Tupi-Guarani, Indigenous people of Brazil, tell a story of the giant water lily, Victoria amazonica. Naia, daughter of the tribal leader, became so enchanted with Jaci, the moon goddess, that she began dreaming of becoming a star herself. Each night she went out and tried to touch the moon. On the night of a full moon, when the moon reflected perfectly in the water of a river, Naia jumped in. She drowned, but Jaci took pity on her and transformed her into a star on earth, the giant water lily's blossom.

The Queen, the Gorgon, and the Naming Controversy

The giant Amazon water lily was described by botanist Thaddeus Haenke in 1801. The first recorded discovery was made by Eduard Poeppig, who named it Euryale amazonica. Robert Schomburgk later rediscovered it on expedition and sent his notes and detailed drawings to John Lindley, botanist for the Crown. While the species was already well known to the Tupi-Guarani people, it was a marvel when it reached England. A special conservatory was built for it at Kew Gardens, and it was nursed along until it bloomed. Lindley presented Queen Victoria with the bloom and told her this rare flower would be named for her: Victoria regina. Poeppig, however, had already entered it into the botanical record as Euryale amazonica.

A secret debate ensued, as no one wanted to offend the queen. One camp held that it was Victoria regina because the name had been declared first, the queen had already been told, and botanically speaking it could not be a Euryale. The other camp argued that the botanical record had already been made official and could not be changed. Incidentally, Euryale is another genus that falls under the Nymphaeaceae umbrella and includes the curious prickly water lily, Euryale ferox. Euryale was also one of the Gorgons, sister to Medusa.

A compromise was proposed: Victoria amazonica. But no one wanted to tell the queen. Some felt that associating her name with the Amazon was undignified, holding the ethnocentric view of the time that the region was unfit for a queen. Others simply did not want to offend her by taking the name away. So the new name was kept secret. When she died, the botanical record was updated. Until 2022 it was believed to be the largest water lily in the world. That title now belongs to Victoria boliviana.

Victoria amazonica can grow up to eight feet in diameter. Its pads support about sixty pounds on average, and some have been known to hold up to one hundred sixty pounds.

The Water Lily and the Gold Spinners

A fairy tale tells of three maidens who lived deep in the forest with an old woman who was a witch. Every day the three girls spun flax into thread from sunrise to sunset. Every few weeks the woman would disappear for the day, never telling where she had gone. Before leaving she would caution the girls to stay inside and not talk to anyone, especially a man, if one should happen by. No one ever came that deep into the forest, so they laughed and kept spinning.

One day, as the witch left with her usual warning, a prince came along, lost in the woods. The older two kept spinning, but the youngest could not help staying in his company. Three days went by. The prince still sat with the maiden, who had lost all track of time and whose golden thread had lost all of its shine. His cavalry found him, but not before he asked her for her hand in marriage, promising to return.

The witch came back that night, saw the dull thread, and knew what had happened. She forbid the girl from seeing him again, telling her she had brought misfortune on herself and on the prince. But the girl sneaked out of the house and met him. When she was not home in the morning, the witch asked the sisters, who pretended to know nothing. The witch bound nine different enchanting herbs, plus salt, into a cloth, wound it into a ball, and threw it into the air toward the river, cursing the girl that she might be pulled into the current as punishment for her disobedience.

Just down the river the prince and the maiden were about to cross when the ball came zooming by, spooking the horse and sending the girl flying into the current, where she quickly disappeared.

The devastated prince mourned her so deeply that he returned a year later to the spot where she had drowned. As he wept he heard a beautiful song. When he looked around, he saw no one. Then he spotted a yellow water lily floating on the surface. He heard the song again, and with no one near, he became convinced that the water lily was his beloved.

He rode off to find her sisters and told them of the singing lily. They knew the witch had hidden their sister away through magick, but they did not know where. So they made the prince a cake full of magickal herbs and gave it to him to eat so he could understand the birds. They asked him to dream and tell them what the birds said. The birds told him the water lily was indeed his bride, and that to rescue her he needed to go to the edge of the water and cover himself in mud and transform into a crab, then scurry as close to the water lily's roots, among the mud and reeds, as he could get. He must claw at the roots and hold tight as they came loose, riding them to the surface. He was then to follow the current and drift until they came to the base of an ash tree that grew along a bank near a large stone. There he was to stop and transform back, from a crab and a lily to a man and a woman.

And so he did, and it worked. The prince and the maiden returned to the kingdom and were married. A little while later they were sitting in the garden when a bird told the prince to go rescue the sisters. They headed to the cottage, where the sisters were thrilled to be reunited. One of them baked a cake full of poison and left it for the old woman, who ate it and died.
