Hyacinth

Notes/Warnings
Not recommended for internal use. Hyacinth bulbs contain a substance that can cause an allergic skin reaction in some handlers, usually itching and a rash, so wear gloves when planting or repotting. The whole plant has a long traditional medicinal use for wounds, prostate complaints, and hemorrhoids, and modern research points to anthocyanin-driven anticancer, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and immune-supporting potential.

Hyacinth (Hyacinthus orientalis) soothes sorrow, blesses love, guards sleep, and preserves joy.

Magickal Properties

Powers: Love, Protection, Happiness

Magickal Uses

  • Ease childbirth: Sew into sachets to reduce the pains of childbirth.
  • Sleep and warding: A plant grown in the bedroom guards against nightmares.
  • Sorrow relief and de‑fascination: Sniffing fresh blossoms lifts grief and cures fascination.
  • Love: Dried flowers are added to love mixtures.

Magickal Lore

Hyacinth is named for the Greek youth Hyacinthus, whom Apollo loved. When Hyacinthus was accidentally killed, Apollo commemorated him by creating this flower from his blood, forever linking hyacinth to love, beauty, and divine remembrance.

Botanical Source Detail

  • Scientific name: Hyacinthus orientalis
  • Family: Liliaceae
  • Type: Cultivated bulbous flowering plant
  • Description: A much loved cultivated plant bearing fragrant, bell-shaped flowers in many colours, with bright lance-shaped leaves rising from a round bulb.
  • Synonyms: Scilla nutans, bluebell

Distribution

Native to Asia Minor and said to be of Syrian origin. Cultivated mainly in Holland and southern France.

Related Species

Closely related to garlic (Allium sativum), onion (Allium cepa), and the wild bluebell (H. non scriptus). At one time bluebell essential oil was produced at Grasse in southern France, with a fresher and more flowery fragrance than the cultivated hyacinth.

Folk Tradition

The wild bluebell bulbs are poisonous; however, the white juice was once used as a substitute for starch or glue. The dried, powdered roots have been described as balsamic, with styptic properties that have not been fully investigated. The Greeks recorded the fragrance of hyacinth as refreshing and invigorating to a tired mind, and the flower has long been used in self-hypnosis work and creative right-brain practices.

Therapeutic Actions

Antiseptic, balsamic, hypnotic, sedative, and styptic.

A Spring in Amsterdam

There is a memory that comes back every time the scent of hyacinth lifts off a cold sidewalk: a thin February ice on canal water, a bicycle ride through cobblestone streets, and then, all at once, the rich floral smell of hyacinths bursting out of every median and public park. The Netherlands move enormous quantities of bulbs out into the world each year, and in the cities at the turn of the season hyacinths are simply everywhere. That sense memory now lives inside the flower itself, so that holding a single stem is enough to be carried back to the city the bulb traveled out of.

Apollo and the Discus

The name of the flower belongs to a Greek myth that crosses winter into summer. Hyacinthus was the youngest son of a Spartan king, and Apollo, the god of music and poetry and the arts, loved him and wished to keep him close. One day the two of them threw discus together. Apollo's throw rose so high it disappeared into the clouds, and when Hyacinthus stepped up to throw in turn, eager to impress, the discus came down and struck him. He died in Apollo's arms. Apollo refused to let him be lost, and declared that he would live on in a flower that would return every year, fragrant and beautiful and brief, dying as quickly as the youth himself.

The Mournful AI

Wild hyacinths are said to carry markings on their petals that look like the Greek letters AI, the sound of a cry of grief. The flower is, in that sense, a permanent record of Apollo's mourning, written into the species so that every spring repeats the lament. To work with hyacinth is to hold that small, perennial grief in the same hand as the sweetness of the bloom.

The Third Figure: Zephyr

In other tellings of the myth, the death is not an accident. The west wind, Zephyr, also loved Hyacinthus, and in jealous rage turned Apollo's discus aside on the breeze, killing the youth he could not have. Both versions of the story belong to the flower. The first speaks of devotion and the cruelty of chance, the second speaks of envy and the cost of unchecked love. The hyacinth remembers all of it.

The Hyacinthia in Sparta

The Spartans kept the grave of Hyacinthus and held the Hyacinthia in midsummer, a two-day festival honoring the duality at the heart of the flower. The first day was given over to grief: sacrifices at the gravesite, no celebration, mourning for the dying back of vegetation and for the dead. The second day turned into revelry: singing, dancing, games, and the joy of the harvest, with Apollo receiving the offerings of gladness. The sister of Hyacinthus, Polyboea, was also remembered at the same festival, doubling the rite of remembrance for the youth who never reached adulthood and the maiden who never reached womanhood.

Magickal Properties Expanded

Hyacinth carries happiness, everlasting love, and a thread of immortality that comes directly from the myth of its making. The flower bonds two people in friendship or as lovers, and giving a hyacinth is an old way to seal the agreement. A growing bulb in the bedroom is said to guard sleep against nightmares, turning the flower's link to grief and remembrance into a quiet kind of protection for the dreaming mind.

Bulb Safety

The bulbs themselves are not friendly to bare hands. The skin can react with itching and a rash to a substance carried in the papery layers, and gardeners and nursery workers usually handle them with gloves. Anyone planting hyacinth in quantity, or repotting from forced bulbs at the end of the season, should do the same and warn the people they hand bulbs off to.

From Turkey to the Queen's Garden

The hyacinth as cultivated today traveled out of central and southern Turkey, with native range reaching the Mediterranean, tropical Africa, Lebanon, and northwestern Syria. It was introduced to Europe and England in 1573, and by 1725 there were already more than two hundred named cultivars. It never set off the speculative frenzy that the tulip did in the same region, but it has never lost its place in the cultivated bed either, prized for both its fragrance and its color.

Hyacinths in the Garden of Live Flowers

In the long tradition of fairy-tale gardens where flowers speak and hold court, the hyacinth is cast again and again as one of the dancers. In those imagined ballrooms the rose is queen, the cockscomb is chamberlain, the tulip and tiger lily are dowagers watching the floor, and the hyacinths and crocuses are the young ladies who dance with the violets. The flower keeps that role in flower lore generally: lovely, fragrant, slightly tragic, and always partnered.

A Bridal Flower and a Curious Belief

The heady, powerful scent made hyacinth a favorite in bridal bouquets, prized for both its beauty and its perfume, and the flower was said to blossom in the fields where the Gods themselves dwelled. A stranger belief grew out of the youth's early death: eating the bulbs was once thought to hold back the sexual maturity of adolescent boys, and up until the eighteenth century a paste made from the poisonous bulb was applied to the male body to keep hair from growing.

Meaning When Gifted

When hyacinth is gifted it carries the meaning of games and sport, rashness, and a dedication to Apollo.
