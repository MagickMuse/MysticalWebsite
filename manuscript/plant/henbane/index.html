Henbane (Hyoscyamus niger) is a notorious baneful herb of old witch-lore, perilous yet long revered for its role in divination, spirit work, and altered states.

History

Some of the earliest references to henbane appear in the Ebers papyrus, one of the oldest known medical texts, where Egyptian physicians describe it as "useful but dangerous." Indigenous to northern Europe, with certain varieties originating in the Mediterranean region, it has spread westward over the centuries and is frequently found growing uncultivated near ancient ruins. Henbane carries a sacred association with the priestesses of the Oracle at Delphi, lending it a reputation as a visionary and prophetic herb.

Characteristics

Henbane bears a superficial resemblance to the potato plant. Its grayish-green leaves grow densely together and have a distinctive texture that is both sticky and covered in fine hairs. The flowers are yellow with striking violet veins running through them. The scent of henbane's flowers is widely described as deeply unpleasant.

Magickal Properties

Gender: Feminine (source: Cold)

Deities: Hecate, Morrigan, Circe

Powers: Love, Rain-making, Hallucinogenic

Magickal Uses

  • From the source book: Similar to datura and nightshade but less powerful. Used to call up evil entities and increase magickal powers. Mind-altering and hallucinogenic.
  • Love (dangerous lore): In older sources, a man seeking love was told to gather henbane naked at dawn while standing on one foot and to wear it to draw love. This is not advised today due to the plant's toxicity.
  • Rain: Burned outdoors to attract rain in folk practice. Do not inhale fumes; substitute fern for safe rain work.
  • Flying ointment: In ancient witchcraft, henbane was a key ingredient in ointments applied to the skin. This practice was associated with the sensation of flight, the so-called "fly on fire," understood as a hallucinatory state induced by the plant's active compounds.
  • Divination and prophecy: Today henbane is used symbolically in workings of divination and prophetic vision, honoring its ancient connection to oracular traditions.
  • Altar anodyne: Henbane may be used with extreme caution as an anodyne when a dried bundle is placed on the altar — never burned or ingested.
☠️
Poison. Do not ingest, burn, or inhale. All parts of the plant are toxic. External and symbolic use only, handled with great care.

Etymology and Folk Names

Hyoscyamus niger earned its English folk name because of the danger it posed to free ranging poultry. Among its other names are devil's eye, god's bean, henbell, hogbean, Insana, Jupiter's bean, and poison tobacco.

Geographic Distribution

Henbane is a biennial that was originally indigenous from Mediterranean regions through Asia Minor. It's been transplanted to the United States where it now grows wild on wasteland, old, neglected gardens, cemeteries, and ruins.

Chemical Composition and Effects

Henbane's active component is hyoscyamine. It is very dangerous when used excessively or over extended periods of time. The general consensus among the ancients was that excessive use of henbane caused madness and insanity.

Henbane's effect is similar to that of datura; however it was indigenous to regions of Europe where datura was unknown until late in the Middle Ages and so henbane was for centuries Europe's most accessible, if secret, hallucinogen. (Toadstools, Amanita muscaria, were typically gathered from the wild, while henbane is relatively easily cultivated.)

For many generations it was the most prominent, beloved "witch plant" in Europe. It is also used similarly in Africa and India, although there it has more indigenous competition.

Germanic Tradition and Lord Balder

Henbane was once among the most important ritual plants of the German lands, sacred to Lord Balder. According to Germanic tradition, for optimum power the ritual harvesting of henbane must be accomplished by naked women, under the direction of magical spirits. This may indicate that the women are ritually channeling these spirits during the harvest.

Traditional Ritual Uses

Henbane was traditionally used for:

  • Conjuring up spirits
  • Divination

Henbane seeds were burned in European bathhouses, a place traditionally associated with divination and spell-casting, well into the Middle Ages. Henbane was also used as a charm in medieval weather magick. It was also once among the ingredients of a very popular medieval beer, one that apparently intoxicated in more ways than one.

Extreme Toxicity

All parts of the henbane plant are deadly poisonous. Allegedly even inhaling the scent of its fresh leaves may lead to intoxication and stupor.

The dead in Hades were crowned with henbane wreathes. Henbane is believed to have been the poison used to kill Hamlet's father in Shakespeare's play.

Historical Medicinal Uses

Although it is poisonous, henbane has historically had various medicinal uses and was believed beneficial for:

  • Hernia
  • Lung disorders
  • Pain relief

Each of these applications required a herbal physician with enough skill and knowledge to administer it.

Because it has narcotic properties, it has traditionally been used medicinally as an anesthetic and a sedative. It was used for various gynecological treatments. Traditional midwifery utilized henbane as a soporific during childbirth so as to create an early form of "twilight sleep."

Irish Herbalism

The Irish name for Hyoscyamus niger is gafann. Meted out in very carefully measured doses, it was once valued in Irish herbalism for its anodyne and sedative properties.

Inquisition Era

During the era of the Inquisition possession and use of henbane was considered sufficient proof for conviction of witchcraft.

Eleven Species of Henbane

There are at least eleven recognized species of Hyoscyamus growing throughout the world, all of them known as henbanes and all bearing narcotic properties. Folklore sometimes treats them interchangeably, but the species with the longest and richest record is H. niger, black henbane. White henbane, H. albus, also surfaces in older accounts, distinguishable mainly by its paler flowers. Black henbane does not actually bear black blooms; its flowers are a pale brownish color with a deep purple, almost black center.

Tropane Alkaloids and Modern Medicine

Henbane shares its core chemistry with Belladonna and Datura, carrying the tropane alkaloids atropine and scopolamine, both still used in surgical practice to suppress saliva and fluid buildup in the lungs. Henbane is also a source of hyoscyamine, applied to hyperactive bladder, motion sickness, nausea, and irritable bowel syndrome. Among the deadliest plants of European folk practice, it stands alongside Foxglove and Wolf's Bane, thriving uninvited in roadside ditches and at the very edge of cultivated crops.

Greek and Roman Anesthesia

The ancient Greeks worked henbane as an anesthetic and pain reliever, and the Romans combined henbane, opium, and Mandrake to produce an inhaled anesthesia administered before surgery. Achilles is said to have used henbane leaves to staunch the wounds of soldiers at Troy, drawing on its anesthetic, pain-relieving, and anti-inflammatory virtues.

External Applications and Antidotes

Classical European herbalism warned that every use of henbane must remain external. Leaves boiled in wine, or juice crushed from leaves or seed, were applied to inflammation of the eyes, breasts, and intimate areas. Mixed with vinegar and laid against the forehead, it was used to ease headaches. In the case of accidental ingestion, traditional antidotes called for goat's milk, honeyed wine, pine nuts, fennel seed, nettle seed, mustard seed, radish seed, or onions and Garlic boiled in wine. Garlic appears yet again as a remedy against poisoning and otherworldly states.

Witches' Brews and Weather Control

Henbane is sacred to witches and counted among the key ingredients of flying ointments and other notorious brews. To break a drought and seize the weather, a witch could dip the stalk of henbane into a well. The plant's reputation for delirium and unnatural sleep made it indispensable to those who worked at the edges of mortal sense.

The Insane Root

A line in Macbeth, when Banquo asks whether he and his companions have eaten on the insane root that takes the reason prisoner, has long been read as a reference to henbane, though mandrake is an equally strong candidate. Old names for henbane include insana and alterculum, words that mark its capacity to produce insanity, stupor, and rage.

Necromancy and the Bane in the Name

Henbane carries a long reputation for necromancy and the magick of the dead. The word bane derives from the old Germanic bana, meaning that which causes death. The English settled on henbane because poultry sicken and die soon after eating it. By folk reckoning, hogs are said to consume the plant without harm, earning it the alternate name hog's bean.

Plutarch, Pliny, and the Dead

Plutarch described the dead as being crowned with chaplets of henbane, their tombs decorated with the plant. Pliny the Elder named henbane a plant of ill omen, scattered across tombs and served at funeral feasts. Given that an overdose can produce heart palpitations, hallucinations, coma, and death, the association with mourning and the grave is hardly accidental.

Völva and Thor's Henbane Beer

The Völva, the Viking witches and seers, are believed to have used henbane in their work with the spirit world. Archeological digs at early Viking settlements have unearthed henbane seeds. Henbane beer was brewed in honor of Thor, and henbane remained a flavoring additive in pilsner-style beers until the Purity Law of 1516 forbade it. In modest doses it carried an aphrodisiacal reputation; in larger doses it produced erratic, frenzied drunkenness. One ethnobotanical theory holds that the seeds and leaves of henbane, rather than the fly agaric mushroom, fueled the pre-battle berserker trance of Viking soldiers.

Hebenon in Hamlet

The cursed hebenon poured into the ear of Hamlet's father is widely understood to be henbane, though hemlock, belladonna, and mandrake have all been proposed. The argument for henbane draws on Renaissance herbal references that catalog the plant under the names Mania and Insana. The shared logic is plain: any of these narcotic plants, given in excess, could take the reason prisoner.

Sleeping Beauty and the Spindle

In the realm of fairy tales, henbane or one of its narcotic cousins may well account for the hundred-year sleep of Briar Rose, the princess sometimes called Aurora or simply the Sleeping Beauty in the Woods. A vengeful, uninvited Fairies curses the infant to prick her finger on a spinning wheel and die; a younger fairy softens the curse so that the wound brings sleep, not death. A spindle anointed with henbane, hemlock, or another narcotic potion would produce exactly such a stupor. An alternate telling lodges a seed beneath the princess's fingernail, sending her into the same long sleep; in this reading, the errant seed could just as plausibly be henbane as flax, given that henbane grows freely at the edges of fields. The good fairies cast the whole castle into trance alongside her, a touch that reads easily as the work of a magickal potion. The two surviving versions of the tale differ in how the princess wakes, and both raise serious problems of consent.

Piedmont Folk Sayings

In Piedmont, folk tradition holds that if a hare is sprinkled with the juice of henbane, every hare in the neighborhood will flee the land. The same region carries a saying that when a mad dog dies, he has tasted henbane.

Meaning When Gifted

When henbane is gifted it carries the meaning of a man's wish to attract the love of a woman.
