History

Myrrh has been traded across cultures since biblical times, prized both for its rich fragrance and its practical use as an embalming agent. In the realm of folk medicine, it has served as an antiseptic, an astringent, and an emmenagogue — a substance used to encourage and regulate menstruation.

Characteristics

Myrrh is a gummy resin harvested from shallow cuts made in the bark of small trees in the Commiphora genus. The secretion hardens into small, pebble-like droplets of yellow and brown, frequently taking on a tear-like shape. The trees are native to Ethiopia, Somalia, and the Arabian Peninsula.

Botanical Information

Commiphora myrrha (Burseraceae family). Feminine resin tree originating from Africa, East Africa, and West Asia. Also known as Bitter Resin and Commiphora. Member of family that also contains frankincense—two distinct trees often confused as one.

Botanical Source

Commiphora myrrha of the Burseraceae family, also classified as Balsamodendrom myrrha and known as gum myrrh, common myrrh, hirabol myrrh, or simply myrrha. The yielding Commiphora trees are shrubs or small trees up to 10 metres high, with sturdy knotted branches, trifoliate aromatic leaves, and small white flowers. The trunk exudes a natural oleoresin (a pale yellow liquid) that hardens into reddish-brown tears, known as myrrh. Native collectors deepen the yield by making incisions in the bark.

Distribution

Native to north-east Africa and south-west Asia, particularly the Red Sea region of Somalia, Yemen, and Ethiopia.

Other Species

Several Commiphora species yield myrrh oleoresin, including African or Somali myrrh (C. molmol) and Arabian or Yemen myrrh (C. abyssinica). Bisabol myrrh or opopanax (C. erthraea) belongs to the same family.

Folk Medicine

Myrrh has served Eastern and Western medicine since the earliest times, with use documented some 3,700 years ago. The ancient Egyptians employed it in embalming, perfumes, and cosmetics. In Chinese medicine it addresses arthritis, menstrual problems, sores, and haemorrhoids. Western tradition (per Joseph Miller) attributes to it an "opening, heating, drying nature," suited to asthma, coughs, common cold, catarrh, sore throat, weak gums and teeth, ulcers and sores. It has also been used to treat leprosy. Myrrh appears in the British Herbal Pharmacopoeia as a specific for mouth ulcers, gingivitis, and pharyngitis.

The Gift of the Magi and the Myth of Myrrha

Myrrh is a small, shrubby tree of the desert, growing in parts of Africa and some Arab lands, and the resin that hardens as it oozes from the pores of its bark was used by the ancient Egyptians in their embalming. It is best known as one of the three gifts the Three Wise Men brought to the infant Christ, alongside gold and frankincense. Though we tend to think of the gold as the most precious, myrrh was worth more than gold and held five times the value of frankincense. Because its scent was used to mask certain parts of the embalming rite, it became a symbol of purity and cleansing, prized too for its healing virtues as a disinfectant and a dresser of wounds.

A Greek myth accounts for the tree's origin. Myrrha was the mother of Adonis, the god himself the fruit of an incestuous union between Myrrha and her father, Theias. When Theias discovered the deception he was enraged, and as he loosed an arrow at her, Aphrodite transformed Myrrha into the tree; when the arrow struck, Adonis was born from the trunk.
