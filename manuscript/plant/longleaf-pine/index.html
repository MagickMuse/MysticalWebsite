Longleaf Pine (Pinus palustris) is the tall southeastern American conifer of the Pinaceae family whose oleoresin and sawdust supply both pine oil and the largest American source of turpentine.

Characteristics

A tall evergreen tree with long needles and a straight trunk, grown extensively for its timber. Longleaf pine exudes a natural oleoresin from the trunk, which provides the largest source for the production of turpentine in America.

Origin & Distribution

Native to the southeastern United States, where the oil is largely produced.

Related Species

Numerous other species of pine around the world are used to produce pine oil as well as pine needle oil and turpentine oil. The closely-related Pine (Pinus sylvestris) is the principal European pine used for pine and pine-needle oils. See also Dwarf Pine, the perfumery-only mountain species, and Pine Essential Oil, the generic commercial pine product.

Folk Tradition

Pine sawdust has been used for centuries as a highly esteemed household remedy for a variety of ailments. It has been described as a grand, gentle, although powerful external antiseptic remedy, applied as a poultice for localised rheumatism, hard cancerous tumours, tuberculosis in the knee or ankle joints, disease of the bone, and other sluggish morbid deposits. Traditional poultice locations include behind the head for failing sight, down the spine for general debility, and on the loins for lumbago. Recorded actions of the oil include mild analgesic, antirheumatic, antiseptic, bactericidal, expectorant, insecticidal, and stimulant.
