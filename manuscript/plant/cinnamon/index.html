Cinnamon (Cinnamomum verum) is a warming spice derived from the inner bark of tropical trees, prized for its ability to amplify spellwork and attract success.

Botanical Information

Cinnamon belongs to the Lauraceae family. Madagascar cinnamon is considered superior to other types such as Saigon cinnamon (C. loureirii) and Batavia cinnamon (C. burmanii).

Characteristics

A tropical evergreen tree reaching up to 15 metres in height. Strong branches carry thick scabrous bark, with young shoots flecked in greeny-orange. The leaves are shiny, green, and leathery; the small flowers are white; and the oval berries take on a bluish-white cast as they mature. Bruised leaves give off a distinctly spicy aroma.

Distribution

Native to Sri Lanka, Madagascar, the Comoro Islands, South India, Burma, and Indochina. The tree is also cultivated in India, Jamaica, and parts of Africa, with each growing region tending to develop its own particular species.

Folk Tradition

The inner bark of the cinnamon tree's new shoots is harvested every two years and sold in the familiar stick form for culinary spice. Eastern medicine has employed cinnamon for thousands of years for a broad range of complaints, including colds, flu, digestive and menstrual problems, rheumatism, kidney troubles, and as a general stimulant. The British Herbal Pharmacopoeia lists it as a specific for flatulent colic and for dyspepsia with nausea.

Therapeutic Actions

Cinnamon is recognized as anthelmintic, antidiarrhoeal, antidote to poison, antimicrobial, antiseptic, antispasmodic, antiputrescent, aphrodisiac, astringent, carminative, digestive, emmenagogue, haemostatic, orexigenic, parasiticide, refrigerant, spasmolytic, stomachic, vermifuge, and a stimulant of the circulatory, cardiac, and respiratory systems.

A staple in baking, cinnamon brings warmth and depth to cookies, pastries, breads, and seasonal desserts.

Historical Magickal Uses

Egyptian embalming: Cinnamon bark was used in the embalming process in ancient Egypt.

Arabian ritual vessels: In Arabia, cinnamon oil was used to anoint sacred ritual vessels.

Medieval aphrodisiac and love potion: Medieval Europeans used cinnamon as an aphrodisiac, and the distilled water was prepared as a love potion. The aroma is considered sexually stimulating.
