History

One of the oldest recorded herbal remedies, St. John's wort was in use from the time of Dioscorides through the Middle Ages and continues to be cited as beneficial for anxiety relief today. Its medicinal history stretches back to the first century, when it was used by a military physician named Proscurides. In the realm of magick, it served as an apotropaic ward against disease, demons, and witches — all of whom were deeply feared throughout the medieval period.

Characteristics

St. John's wort is a woody shrub bearing bright yellow, star-shaped flowers. It grows up to three feet tall and two feet wide, preferring well-drained soil and full to partial sun. A hardy and adaptable plant, it tolerates a range of soil types but struggles in very alkaline conditions.

Gathered on Midsummer or Friday in European rites; hung by windows or dried over Midsummer fires to guard against thunderbolts, fire, ghosts, necromancers, and other evildoers.

Beauty & Skincare

Saint John's wort functions as a mood elevator, and the connection between emotional wellbeing and outward radiance is central to herbal beauty magick. When used to counteract depression and lift the spirits, it improves the overall vitality and glow of the individual. It is included in herbal beauty practice not for topical effects, but for its influence on mood and inner light.

Powerful Solar Affiliations

Saint John's wort is a plant with powerful solar affiliations. If mugwort is the plant of the moon, then Saint John's wort belongs to the sun.

According to the ancient Greeks, while mugwort was Artemis' sacred plant, Saint John's wort epitomized the power of her brother, the solar spirit Apollo. Saint John's wort is understood metaphysically as mugwort's brother.

Etymology: Feast of John the Baptist

Saint John's wort derived its English folk name from the feast day of John the Baptist, which coincides with Midsummer's Eve and the Summer Solstice. It is when the sun is at its maximum height and it is when Hypericum perforatum is at its peak, too.

(So is mugwort—a folk name for that plant is Saint John's Girdle, connecting both plants to the Midsummer celebrations.)

The Sunny Plant: Light Against Darkness

Saint John's wort is a sunny plant; it brings light and cheer and clarity where previously there was darkness and despair. It is probably the plant in this section most familiar to the average reader because Saint John's wort's magical uses have been found by modern science to be true: Saint John's wort is a modern remedy against depression.

Although the concept of standardized medication is new, Saint John's wort's reputation for providing light in the darkness is ancient.

Medieval France: Against Fairy Interference

In medieval France, Saint John's wort was traditionally used as a remedy against interference from the fairies, especially when that interference is experienced as depression and malaise.

This tradition recognizes depression as potentially having supernatural origins, and positions Saint John's wort as a protective plant against otherworldly influences that manifest as emotional distress.

Greek Tradition: Portraits of the Dead

In Greek tradition, sprigs of Saint John's wort were hung over portraits of the dead so that whatever ills the deceased had suffered, whether physical, emotional or psychic, would be relieved.

Their afflictions, pain, and suffering would be terminated so as not to infect the living with these emanations. This practice protected the living from the lingering spiritual effects of the dead's suffering, using Saint John's wort's solar power to cleanse and neutralize negative energy.

Complementary or Oppositional? The Mugwort-Saint John's Wort Duality

Mugwort and Saint John's wort may be understood as complementary powers or as oppositional forces, depending upon your perspective.

The Witch-Hunters' View

If mugwort epitomized witchcraft, then witch-hunters believed that Saint John's wort would oppose and eradicate it.

If mugwort is maximum yin—an emphatically female plant—then Saint John's wort is maximum yang, the epitome of masculinity.

If mugwort is the evil sister, then Saint John's wort is the heroic brother.

This binary opposition reflects the dualistic thinking of witch-hunting ideology, which sought to pit solar, masculine, "righteous" power against lunar, feminine, "dark" magic.

Cultural and Magical Appropriation

The use of Saint John's wort by witch-hunters may be understood as cultural or magical appropriation.

That Saint John's wort was also popular amongst witches is indicated by its German folk-name, Walpurgis Herb. This name directly connects the plant to Walpurgis Night (April 30th/May 1st), the witches' sabbath celebrated on the eve of May Day—a thoroughly Pagan and witchcraft-associated festival.

However, in French Saint John's wort is called "chasse-diable" or "devil-chaser," reflecting the Christian reinterpretation of the plant as a weapon against evil rather than a tool of witchcraft.

Witch-Hunters and the Devil's Compact

Witch-hunters fed Saint John's wort tea to accused witches in the belief that it negated the devil's compact.

However, negating the compact didn't mean that now everything was all right and the ex-witch could go home free. She was doomed anyway; "negating the compact" merely ensured that it would be safe to execute her.

She would lack the power to execute vengeance on her judges and executioners. This practice reveals the twisted logic of witch trials: even when the alleged source of a witch's power was neutralized, the punishment remained death. Saint John's wort served not to free the accused, but to protect the executioners from supernatural retaliation—ensuring their safety while condemning the victim to death without recourse to magical defense.

The Terrestrial Sun

The species name perforatum points to the tiny oil glands that freckle the leaves and look, held up to the light, like a scatter of little holes, while hypericum is read from the Greek words for above and picture, recalling the old habit of hanging the plant above images and icons to turn away evil. When the petals are squeezed they bleed a red oil, and this blood colour bound the flower ever closer to St. John the Baptist, beheaded at the asking of Salome, daughter of Herod. The bloom even fades in late August, its petals mottling brown red as though marking the saint's martyrdom. For the Celts it stood for the Summer Solstice, blooming at the height of the year in the Sun's own yellow, so that in the Middle Ages it was called sol terrestris, the terrestrial sun. Joined thus to both the Baptist and the Sun, St. John's wort became a potent guard: in Greece it was worn on the body and hung over precious objects to avert evil, and elsewhere it was tied above doorways to keep the home safe. Its oil was prized for healing deep wounds, a virtue the Knights Hospitaller of St. John learned in Jerusalem while guarding pilgrims on the road to the Holy City, and the same plant was long turned to against depression and melancholy.
