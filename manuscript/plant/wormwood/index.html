History

The earliest known reference to wormwood appears in the Ebers Papyrus, an ancient Egyptian medical text believed to date to around 3500 BCE and obtained by the Egyptologist George Ebers in 1873. A member of the Artemisia family, wormwood later became the key flavoring ingredient in absinthe, an alcoholic drink that rose to popularity in late eighteenth-century Paris. Its reputation as a dangerous intoxicant, largely due to the extreme behavior of those who consumed it, led to its ban in many countries by 1915. It is regarded as a mind-altering substance.

Characteristics

Native to Europe, wormwood has a pleasant but faintly musty aroma, a notably bitter taste, and a long, sinister reputation. A slow-growing deciduous shrubby perennial, it is commonly found across the United States. Wormwood has deep roots, silvery hairy leaves, and intensely yellow flowers. It grows to about 18 inches in height, propagates fairly readily, and favors growing near the sea.

Necromancy

Burning wormwood in a cemetery is said to cause the souls of the dead to rise and speak.

Specific Uses

A very magickal herb sacred to the Moon. Use in charms, incense, scrying, divination, prophecy, and astral projections. Works well in incenses containing mugwort and for exorcism blends.

Magickal Properties

Powers: Psychic Powers, Protection, Love, Calling Spirits

Magickal Uses

Psychic development: Wormwood is burned in incense or worn on the body to help develop psychic powers.

Protection: Carried, it guards against bewitchment and harm. Hung from a rear-view mirror, it protects a vehicle traveling on dangerous roads.

Counterpoisons: Folklore claims wormwood counteracts poisoning from hemlock and toadstools, though this should never be relied upon.

Love mixtures: Its absinthe associations brought it into love workings over time. Placed under the bed, it draws a loved one near.

Spirit work: Burned, often blended with sandalwood, it summons spirits. In graveyards it is burned to converse with the dead.

Apotropaic ward: Wormwood is a powerful protective herb used to guard against evil, and legend holds that planting it around a garden keeps pests and snakes away.

Boundary magick: When protective distance from a person or situation is needed, wormwood added to spells and charms keeps unwanted energy and people at bay.

Banishing and protecting: It is especially effective in its dual role of banishing what must be removed while reinforcing a protective barrier at the same time.

Circle casting: Burned at the opening of a ritual during circle casting, it consecrates and protects the working space.

Additional Notes

Wormwood is a poison. Dried herb in tea should never exceed 1/2 g, taken 2 to 3 times a day, and it is not meant for long-term use. Avoid it during pregnancy or nursing, and when actively trying to conceive.

Its forms are not interchangeable. The essential oil is highly toxic to the point of fatality and must never be used, while the flower essence remedy is very safe.

Medicinal Uses

Wormwood oil, when very carefully diluted, has a long history as a digestive bitter. Bitter herbs stimulate the production of bile in the gallbladder and digestive secretions in the stomach, which improves digestion of fats and relieves sluggishness after meals. Wormwood is one of the most potent bitter herbs known and was a standard component of digestive bitters formulas in European medicine for centuries. It directly stimulates gallbladder contraction and bile flow, making it useful for conditions of bile deficiency and sluggish digestion.

Part used: Essential oil (diluted) or dried herb in very small quantities

Application: Internal in strictly controlled doses only

⚠️
Wormwood is a toxin. Never use the essential oil internally. The dried herb may only be used in extremely small quantities (no more than 1/2 g in tea, 2 to 3 times daily) and never for extended periods. Unsafe during pregnancy or nursing. Misuse can cause seizures, organ damage, and death.

Absinthe: The Green Fairy

Absinthe is an alcoholic beverage distilled from wormwood leaves combined with anise, dittany of Crete, fennel, and star anise. Its emerald-green color earned it the nickname "Green Fairy" (Fée Verte). Invented in 1792-1797 by Swiss doctor Dr. Ordinaire, it was later commercialized by Henri-Louis Pernod, who opened distilleries in Switzerland and France (1805).

The drink had exceptionally high alcohol content (120-160 proof) and was traditionally consumed in a ritual: water dripped over sugar on a slotted spoon into the glass, causing the color to shift beautifully. The Parisian demi-monde adopted absinthe, believing it stimulated creativity. Famous devotees included Toulouse-Lautrec, Van Gogh, Gauguin, Oscar Wilde, and Hemingway.

Absinthe became controversial and was blamed for societal degeneration. After the 1905 Lanfray murder case, it was banned in the US (1912) and France (1915). Modern understanding shows the dangers came primarily from its extreme alcohol content rather than the wormwood itself, though bootleg versions contained toxic adulterants.

The drink's roots as an herbal cure-all fit a wider pattern: many European liqueurs began as healing and magickal potions, whether first brewed by monks (Chartreuse) or witches (Strega). With drinking water so often unsafe, an alcohol base was a necessity. Although the liqueur remains banned in many places, the herb itself is perfectly legal and grows easily in a flowerpot at home. In Latin America, wormwood is called absinthe, but the name refers to the herb used in magick and healing, not the drink.

Pernod was developed to fill the void left by the absinthe ban. It shares largely the same formula and green color but lacks the wormwood. A wormwood stem steeped in it creates a magickal potion (see Wormwood Pernod), though this does not reconstruct absinthe, which had a much higher alcohol content among other differences.

Critical Warning: Never substitute Essential Oil of Wormwood for the plant leaves, as the oil is fatally poisonous due to concentrated toxins.

Thujone Chemistry

The active component in wormwood is thujone, a chemical compound also found in garden sage, cedar (Thuja, from which the compound takes its name), and tansy. Thujone acts on the same regions of the brain as THC (from cannabis) and theobromine (from chocolate), which may explain the dreamy, creative states absinthe devotees reported.

Shakespeare's Wormwood

Shakespeare references wormwood in A Midsummer Night's Dream through Oberon's use of "love-in-idleness" (wild pansy) and other botanical enchantments. The fairy king's mastery of plant magick, including bitter herbs that reveal truth and sweet ones that compel love—reflects Renaissance awareness of wormwood's psychoactive properties and its long association with vision-inducing preparations.

Without Sweetness

The Greek name for the herb, apsinthion, means "without sweetness," and from it comes the name of the liqueur absinthe. Drunk habitually and to excess, absinthe can bring on a condition called absinthism, one symptom of which is complete paralysis. So troublesome did the drink become in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries that it was banned, though it is finding favor again today.

The Bitter Star

Because of its bitterness the plant stands for that quality, and also for grief and for anything poisonous to the soul. In the Book of Revelation, Wormwood is the name given to a star tied to a devilish figure said to lay waste to Israel; the star falls from the heavens and poisons a third of the waters of the earth, a story read as a foretelling of the Last Judgment in the poisoning of our waters.

Sacred to Artemis

The Latin name Artemisia absinthium reveals the herb's tie to the goddess Artemis, the huntress and Great Goddess of the Woods. See Mugwort for the shared lore of the artemisia family. Wormwood's unromantic English name advertises its power as a pest repellent, and like mugwort it banishes powerful evil spirits as if they were little worms, guarding against psychic dangers during psychic journeying. The two work well in tandem: mugwort uncovers power, and wormwood provides the safety needed to learn to use it.

The Original Bitter Herb

Wormwood, mugwort's infamous brother, is an ancient and profound magickal herb, the original biblical bitter herb. Its magickal uses are myriad: healing, protection, exorcism, and love spells. It repels the Evil Eye and provides safety for magickal journeys.

Dried wormwood can be bought from herbal suppliers, but it also makes an easy houseplant. Keep it for its protective guardian properties, harvesting a few twigs and leaves as needed.

Meaning When Gifted

When wormwood is gifted it carries the meaning of absence, and of the torment of separation.
