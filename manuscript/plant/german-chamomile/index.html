Botanical Information

German Chamomile (Matricaria chamomilla or M. recutita) belongs to the Asteraceae family. This strongly aromatic annual reaches up to 60 cm tall, with a hairless erect branching stem, delicate feathery leaves, and simple daisy-like white flowers on single stems. Native to Europe and northern and western Asia, it is now naturalized across North America and Australia. Despite its common name, it is no longer cultivated in Germany; the bulk of commercial production is centred in Hungary and the rest of Eastern Europe.

Other Species

There are many varieties of chamomile, such as pineapple weed (Chamaemelium suaveolens) and Roman chamomile (Chamaemelum nobile), both of which are used to produce an essential oil. See Roman Chamomile and Roman Chamomile Essential Oil. Moroccan Chamomile (Ormenis multicaulis) is also marketed under the chamomile name, but it is only distantly related botanically and chemically and olfactorily distinct from both German and Roman chamomile; it is not a substitute for either. See also Moroccan Chamomile Essential Oil. In appearance German chamomile is very similar to corn chamomile (Anthemis arvensis) but can be distinguished from it because the latter is scentless. German chamomile shares many of the same qualities as Roman chamomile, except that its anti-inflammatory properties are greater due to the higher percentage of azulene.

Folk Tradition

A long-standing medicinal tradition, especially in Europe, for all states of tension and the visceral symptoms that can arise therefrom, such as nervous dyspepsia and nervous bowel, tension headaches, and sleeplessness; especially useful for all children's conditions, calming without depressing. An excellent skin care remedy.

Dual Nature and Associations

Chamomile holds two valid magickal associations depending on how you work with the plant. The bright, sunny appearance of its flowers and apple-like scent create solar associations linked to Apollo. However, the plant's watery, cooling nature and healing properties also connect it strongly to lunar energy. Both associations are supported by research and tradition.

Symbolism & Mythology

The chamomile flower draws its name from a Greek word meaning apple, a nod to the distinct apple scent the plant gives off, especially when its foliage is crushed. The Egyptians held it sacred to the Sun God Ra, and the Teutons in turn dedicated it to their own Sun God, Balder. Its protective reputation runs deep as well: a wreath of chamomile hung in the home is said to guard the house against thunder and lightning.

Financial Magick

Chamomile flowers have long been associated with financial prosperity. Gamblers traditionally washed their hands in chamomile infusions to attract money. The plant is added to love oils to draw a mate who is generous with their finances.

Cooling and Empowering Properties

In herbal medicine, chamomile is recognized as a cooling herb. It subdues hot flashes, excitability, and hysteria. Beyond its cooling nature, chamomile is profoundly empowering. It strengthens the emotionally weak and soothes heated tempers, anxiety, and heightened emotional states.

For meditation and sleep: Add to incenses intended to bring on restful states for meditation and sleep. Chamomile tea can induce sleep when taken before bed.

As a strewing herb: A common medieval strewing herb prized for its wonderful apple-like aroma. Can be added to magickal floor washes.

Ancient Egyptian use: Revered by the ancient Egyptians, who used it for many purposes including in massage oil and ritual offerings.

History

Chamomile traces its origins to western Europe, India, and Asia. Its earliest documented use was as a remedy for fevers in ancient Egyptian medicine, and the Egyptians incorporated it into ritual offerings to their gods. It earned a reputation as something of a cure-all across Europe.

Characteristics

A low-growing perennial rarely exceeding ten inches in height. Beyond its well-known calming qualities, it is a bitter herb that supports the digestion of fats and settles the stomach. The chamomile family includes German, Hungarian, Roman, and English varieties.

Beauty & Skincare

Chamomile soothes reactive or sensitive skin and is used in hair rinses to brighten and condition light-colored hair. A chamomile infusion applied as a facial rinse can reduce redness and promote an even complexion.

Infant Protection

Bunches of chamomile hung above a baby's crib are used as a protective charm, shielding the child from harmful influences and malevolent spirits.
