Seaweed is a common term for thousands of species of marine algae that grow in the ocean and other bodies of water. Coastal communities have harvested seaweed for thousands of years as both food and medicine. The species most often found in folk practice include bull kelp (Nereocystis luetkeana), kelp (Porphyra sp.), sea palm (Postelsia palmaeformis), sea lettuce (Ulva lactuca), murlins (Alaria esculenta), dulse (Palmaria palmata), sweet or Irish kelp (Saccharina latissima), and Irish moss or carrageen (Chondrus crispus).

Folk Medicine

Many seaweeds carry anti-inflammatory properties and have long been used to soothe swelling, cuts, and burns. Several species contain compounds being studied for use in cancer treatments. Seaweed is dense in iron and fiber, and some species are also rich in protein. Carrageenan, extracted from Irish moss, is widely used as a thickening agent in cooking and cosmetics.

⚠️ Many Indigenous coastal communities, including peoples of the Pacific Northwest and Northern California, harvest specific seaweeds for tribally-particular foods and medicines. These traditions are closed practices and should not be replicated by outsiders.

Magickal Uses

  • Use seaweed in workings of self-care, self-love, beauty, and nourishment of mind, body, and soul.
  • Place dried seaweed on the altar to connect with the mysteries of the ocean and the secret witchcraft of mermaids and other water spirits.
  • Carry a strand of dried kelp in spells for success when the goal is to stay afloat through a difficult passage of life.
  • Add seaweed to nourishment-oriented kitchen magick, particularly broths and stocks meant to feed the spirit alongside the body.

Mermaid Lore

Where seaweed grows, mermaids are never far behind. The fascination with these aquatic beings reaches back thousands of years. The early Assyrian goddess Atargatis was depicted in mermaid form as far back as 1000 BCE. According to her oldest stories, she killed a man she loved and, in her shame, banished herself first to a lake and then, when even that could not hide her, into the body of a half-fish, half-woman.

⚠️ The Taino people, the Indigenous people of the Caribbean, speak of Aycayia, a mermaid whose voice carries the power to sink ships. Aycayia belongs to a closed practice and should be honored only by those within Taino tradition.

Mermaids carry great skill and healing power, and they can pass those gifts to those they favor. For this reason they were among the entities persecuted during the witch hunts. A Hebridean tale from the small village of Eilean Anabuich tells of a man who captured a mermaid; in exchange for her freedom she granted him three wishes. He became a prophet who could foretell the future of women, gained the ability to sing (though, by local accounts, only barely), and grew into a skilled herbal doctor who could cure the King's Evil (tuberculosis) and other diseases once thought beyond help.

Even the dread pirate Blackbeard is said to have feared mermaids so deeply that he would sail nautical miles out of his way to avoid mermaid-haunted waters.

The Water Horse and the Kelp Bringer

The Scottish water horse, or kelpie, brings seaweed up from the sea to fertilize the inland fields and nourish the crops of coastal farms. But never mistake this generosity for safety. Water horses can seize the unwary and drag them under in a heartbeat.

One Hebridean account tells of a traveler passing a lonely loch northeast of Mull who came upon a horse grazing along the shore. He noticed green water-herbs, the liaranaich, clinging about its feet and wisely refrained from touching it. A stranger he met along the road later confirmed his caution: had the horse been a water horse and found him unfriendly, it would have carried him straight into the deep.

On Skye, the each uisge is said to have a sharp bill or a narrow brown slippery snout, a long flowing tail and mane, and a coat that shifts from grey to black, sometimes with a white spot at the forehead. Some say its color is fixed like any horse, others that it changes color as freely as it changes shape. When it appears in the form of a man, it can be detected by hooves still hidden in the boots and by sand or green water-weeds still tangled in the hair. However much a farmer may at first profit from capturing one with a bridle, a cap, or a cow-shackle, the bargain ends in ruinous loss.

A Fairy-Strangler on Inishark

A man called Shaun Mor was said to have lived on the small Irish island of Inishark, where he kept company with the fairy folk by night, carrying their goods and receiving in return their gifts and secrets. Should they ever try to cross him, he is said to have strangled them in the seaweed.

The Sea King's Garden

Old fairy tales describe the gardens of the Sea King, far below where the water turns deep and blue as cornflowers. There, on sand the color of blue sulphur flame, grow the strangest flowers and plants, their leaves and stems so pliant that the slightest stir of water sets them moving as if alive. Fish glide between the branches like swallows through orchard trees, and over everything lies a peculiar blue radiance, as if the sky itself were spread above and below. Each of the young sea princesses tends her own plot in this garden, and in the deepest one a quiet child plants a rose-colored weeping willow over a marble statue carved from a shipwreck, until the crown of the tree and its roots seem to lean toward each other in a long kiss.
