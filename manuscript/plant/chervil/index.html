Chervil (Anthriscus cerefolium), also known as Sweet Cicely, French Parsley, or British myrrh, is a delicate annual herb with feathery leaves and a mild anise-like fragrance.

Botanical Information

Chervil belongs to the Apiaceae (Umbelliferae) family. It is a cultivated form of the wild chervil or garden-beaked parsley (A. sylvestris), which shares similar properties and uses. It should not be confused with sweet cicely (Myrrhis odorata), a separate garden herb that also goes by the name sweet or smooth chervil.

Characteristics

A delicate annual reaching about 30 cm in height. The stems are slender and heavily branched, the bright green leaves are finely divided and fernlike, and the plant produces umbels of small flat white flowerheads followed by long, smooth seed-fruits. Bruising any part of the plant releases a pleasing aromatic scent.

Distribution

Native to Europe and western Asia and naturalized across America, Australia, and New Zealand. The herb is widely cultivated, particularly in southern Europe and the Americas.

Folk Tradition

The name chervil derives from the Greek word meaning "to rejoice," a nod to the leaves' delightful fragrance. The fresh leaves serve as a domestic spice in salads, soups, omelettes, sauces, and bread doughs. In folk medicine the herb is steeped as a tea said to tone the blood and nerves, support poor memory and mental depression, and sweeten the entire digestive system. The juice pressed from the fresh herb has been applied to skin complaints such as eczema, abscesses, and slow-healing wounds, and it has also been employed for dropsy, arthritis, and gout.

Therapeutic Properties

Chervil is recognized as aperitif, antiseptic, carminative, cicatrisant, depurative, diaphoretic, digestive, diuretic, nervine, restorative, metabolic stimulant, stomachic, and tonic.

Essential Oil Safety

Steam distillation from the seeds yields a pale yellow oil with a sweet-herbaceous, anise-like odour, composed mainly of methyl chavicol along with 1-allyl-2,4-dimethoxybenzene and anethole. Because methyl chavicol and anethole carry toxic and irritant effects (and methyl chavicol may be carcinogenic), the essential oil is best avoided for therapeutic, aromatherapy, and home use. The plant remains in extensive food-flavour use, especially in meat products and in alcoholic and soft drinks.

Magickal Uses

For funerary rites: Chervil was among the sacred funerary herbs of ancient Egypt. Remains were found within Tutankhamun's tomb. Burning dried chervil as incense comforts the bereaved and is believed to open a channel for the living to contact the deceased.
