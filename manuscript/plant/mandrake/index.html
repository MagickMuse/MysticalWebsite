Mandrake (Mandragora officinalis; Atropa mandragora) is the storied sorcerer’s root of protection, fertility, money, love, and health.

Magickal Properties

Planet: Mercury (existing Astrology property also has Jupiter — conflict noted)

Element: Earth (source: Earth; existing content says Fire)

Deities: Hecate, Hathor, Circe (from source)

Powers: Protection, Fertility, Money, Love, Health

Magickal Uses

  • House guardian: A whole root on the mantel protects the house and brings fertility and prosperity.
  • Sleep and ward: Hung on the headboard for protection during sleep; carried to attract love and prevent illness; used in exorcism, demons cannot reside where mandrake is present.
  • Activation rite: To awaken a dried root, set it in a prominent place for three days, then soak overnight in warm water. Use the water to protect doors and windows or to purify people.
  • Image magick and money: Used as a poppet in image magick; money (especially silver) placed beside the root is said to double. Its scent causes sleep.

Note: Extremely scarce; traditional substitutes include bryony, apple, ash roots, American may‑apple, etc.

The Mythology of Mandrake

Few herbs command mythology and mystery quite like mandrake. Pythagoras speculated that the taproot might represent a tiny human form — or perhaps a kind of natural doll, fashioned by the earth itself.

The plant was ascribed a host of dangerous and uncanny powers. The Roman historian Flavius Josephus claimed that mandrake emitted an eerie red glow at night, making it visible in darkness but treacherous to approach. According to folk tradition, the plant could move of its own free will, and before anyone could get close, it had to be immobilized — the prescribed method being a bath of urine mixed with menstrual blood. Harvesting required a dog rather than human hands: the dog was tied to the root and driven to pull, uprooting the mandrake, but dying in the process. As centuries passed, the mystique only deepened. The naturalist Aelian took the legend further still, claiming that by daylight mandrake was altogether invisible, possessing the power to conceal itself among other plants.

The root's human silhouette lent it a sinister theological dimension. Some traditions held that mandrake was one of the creator's early prototypes — a rough draft of the human form conceived before God finally built Adam from clay. Because of this, the plant was believed to grow only in lands adjoining the Garden of Eden. In earthly terms, it favored morbid ground: mandrake was said to sprout most readily beneath gallows trees, nourished by the fluids of the condemned.

Despite — or because of — this fearsome reputation, mandrake commanded extraordinary prices. Owners believed that it made them invincible in battle and irresistible as lovers. It was thought capable of producing the philosopher's stone, the legendary talisman of immortality. But ownership came with strict obligations: the root had to be bathed in wine, wrapped in silk, and regularly fed. If the mandrake failed to deliver on its promises, the explanation was always the same — the root was bored or disinterested in its keeper.

Disposal was nearly impossible. Giving the root away was forbidden, and reselling it for less than the original purchase price was considered equally taboo. Owners who could find no acceptable solution were frequently buried alongside their mandrakes.

The debunking came slowly. In 1551, William Turner was among the first to note plainly that the mandrake root didn't actually resemble a human being. By 1597, John Gerard took the skepticism further, digging one up himself to demonstrate the point — though this hardly dampened the plant's enduring reputation in folk and magical traditions.

The Human-Shaped Root

Mandrake's claim to fame lies in the magickal resemblance of its roots to the human figure. Individual specimens are identifiably female or male (and a few very special ones may be both). There is no plant more identified with magick and witchcraft.

Mandrake's main magickal uses have to do with love, sex, fertility, and wish fulfillment. Because it has narcotic properties, mandrake also has a long history as an early anesthetic.

Names and Aphrodisiac Powers

In Arabic, mandrake is known as Father of Life (referring to its fertility-inducing powers) and djinn's Egg or Devil's Apple (these aren't demonic references but are instead intended to refer to mandrake's power as an aphrodisiac to provide forbidden pleasures. Islam has no demonology comparable to that of witch-hunt era Europe.)

In Hebrew, the word for mandrake is translated as "love-apples," referring to its aphrodisiac properties.

Geographic Distribution

The mandrake plant is indigenous to Mediterranean regions, especially Crete, Sicily, and the Levantine coast, as well as Iraq, North Africa, and Spain. It grows wild and is not uncommon in these areas. Nineteenth-century missionaries traveling to the Middle East wrote that women used this plant in the same manner and for the same purposes as described by the Bible.

Although it grows rampant in warm, dry climates, it may be cultivated with care in areas not naturally conducive to its growth. The master herbalist John Gerard (1545–1612) cultivated it in his greenhouse.

The Whole Plant

Magickal references almost inevitably discuss just the roots; the whole plant is lovely and useful, although potentially poisonous if taken internally. Mandrake has apple-like fruit (all those apple names aren't mere affectation) and lovely flowers possessing a strong aroma.

Mandrake is a member of the Nightshade family, many of whose members contain deadly poisons. Mandrake, too, is poisonous-the berries are particularly toxic. However, for magickal purposes, mandrake is virtually always used as an amulet. It is carried, placed under the mattress, or shaped into a doll. It is safe to handle mandrake root but not to take any part of the plant internally or to otherwise apply it to the body.

The Biblical Success Story: Rachel and Leah

Many tales and legends describe mandrake's magick powers but the most famous occurs in the Old Testament. This is significant because although biblical injunctions are frequently used as the rationale for persecuting witches, in this case, the Bible recounts a magickal success story.

The biblical patriarch Jacob is married to two sisters. He loves the younger one, Rachel, passionately but she's infertile and desperately wants to conceive. He doesn't love the older sister, Leah (who desperately loves him) although that hasn't stopped him from fathering her many children.

One day, Leah's oldest son, Reuben, old enough to understand his mother's situation, finds a mandrake root and brings it to her so that she can use it to magickally gain Jacob's love. Word gets around and Rachel shows up, demanding the mandrake so that she can use it to magickally conceive. The two negotiate and, ultimately, Rachel gets the mandrake, conceiving soon afterwards.

True Mandrake vs. Substitutes

The Bible carried mandrake's reputation around the Eastern Hemisphere; the name "mandrake" developed an aura of allure and power but there was one problem: true mandrake doesn't grow wild in cold climates. People in these colder areas wanted mandrake too but it wasn't available. What were they to do? They began referring to local plants with similar uses and human-shaped roots as "mandrake" too. Thus the name "mandrake" may refer to a variety of different and unrelated plants. True mandrake belongs to the Mandragora species.

Ancient Anesthetic

Along with opium poppies, mandrake was among the very first anesthetics in existence. Mandrake was first administered either as a potion made by boiling the root in wine, or as an inhalant made by soaking cloth in mandrake infusions.

Hippocrates wrote of mandrake in approximately 400 BCE: "a small dose in wine, less than would occasion delirium, will relieve the deepest depression and anxiety."

Mandrake can induce deep sleep, however the incorrect dosage can cause the big sleep from which one never awakens. Fatal doses are very possible, demonstrating that, then as now, anesthesia can be among the most dangerous parts of the surgical process.

Mercy on the Cross

Its most dramatic use was in Roman-occupied Judea. Crucifixion was not a unique punishment; the Romans crucified masses of people. Jewish women brewed draughts from mandrakes and soaked sponges in the liquid to offer men nailed to the crosses, causing an anesthetic effect. Depending upon the dose, this could be fatal, a mercy killing. Sometimes, however, the person only had the appearance of death, often fairly long-term. The body would be returned to their family. Eventually they would recover.

When the Romans discovered what was going on (perhaps witnessing too many dead men walking) policies were changed: it was decreed that before any man was released from the cross, his legs would be broken and/or he was to be brutally pierced with a spear.

The German Alraune Tradition

Nowhere was the magickal use of mandrake root more popular or inventive than in Germany. In sixteenth-century German lands, a synonym for witches was Alraundelberin or "mandrake bearer." "Alraune" already indicated a witch; it now came to mean mandrake, so that mandrake and witch became synonyms.

Although technically alraune just means "mandrake" more is implied. The alraune describes a magickal system, whereby the mandrake root (the alraune) is cared for, fed, and bathed by a person; in return the alraune provides that person with magickal wish fulfillment, protection, and good fortune. This is very similar to various African rituals and to modern African-derived root-working.

Alraune Care Ritual

The alraune became a staple of medieval German magickal practice. Every Friday the root was bathed in wine, wrapped in white silk and laid in a box that was as beautiful or magickally empowered as possible. One would whisper one's desires to the mandrake and hopefully watch them come into fruition.

A slightly different variation existed too: the mandrake root might be surgically enhanced to further its resemblance to a human being, dressed in little clothes, and placed on a throne to serve as oracle or household guardian. Because this practice was extremely illegal (this was at the height of the witch-trials), the seated doll might be kept upright in a small hidden closed cabinet or box.

The Witch's Flying Ointment

Mandrake is among the plants most frequently cited as an ingredient in the legendary witch's flying ointment, a topical preparation documented in sources from the 1500s and 1600s. The ointment was said to grant witches the ability to fly and even to transform into winged creatures such as owls. The full list of plants named in historical descriptions is extensive — including hemlock, belladonna, henbane, monkshood, poppy, thorn apple, and others — but mandrake's narcotic and hallucinogenic properties made it a recurring presence across multiple accounts. Its ability to induce altered states, combined with its deep association with sorcery, ensured it a central place in flying ointment lore.

The Screaming Root Legend

All kinds of superstitions were associated with mandrake, particularly regarding the manner in which it must be gathered. Although it can be carefully dug out the same as any other root, it became traditional to pull the entire mandrake plant from the ground in one fell swoop.

According to legend, mandrake screams when it is pulled; hearing the scream is fatal. Another version suggests that the first one to pull up the root dies. All kinds of complex machinations for harvesting mandrake developed: one had to stuff up one's ears with wax, tie a dog to the plant and then somehow induce him to jump away with such force that the entire root is pulled out of Earth in one piece.

(These are all superstitions, although they are based on tradition. Mandrakes are grown in nurseries today; no dogs are killed during the harvest nor are there any other mandrake-induced fatalities involved in the harvest.)

The Two Mandragoras

A second species, Mandragora autumnalis, grows alongside official mandrake and is used interchangeably with it in folk medicine and magick. M. autumnalis blooms in autumn rather than spring and prefers slightly different growing conditions. In many traditions the darker root of M. officinarum is read as male and the whiter root of M. autumnalis as female. Both are native to southern Europe, and official mandrake also grows widely across the Mediterranean.

Circeium, the Witch's Root

The Greeks called mandrake Circeium after the witch goddess Circe, whose dominion included mandrake, deadly nightshade, henbane, and the rest of the poison plant path. The older botanical classification Atropa Mandragora, still encountered today, names the plant for Atropos, the Greek Fate who decides which mortals live and which die. The same goddess lends her name to Atropa belladonna, mandrake's nightshade cousin.

The genus name Mandragora itself derives from the medieval Latin mandragorās, meaning "little dragon," a warning to anyone who would dare consume the root: this plant breathes fire.

Tropane Alkaloids

Mandrake carries the tropane alkaloids atropine and scopolamine, the same compounds present in deadly nightshade. Modern medicine still uses both. Atropine raises the heart rate, treats certain poisonings, dilates the pupils, and eases muscle spasms. Scopolamine relieves nausea and vomiting after surgery, and both alkaloids reduce saliva during surgery. For centuries mandrake was combined with poppy, henbane, and nightshade to create a stupor used to treat ailments or to serve as anesthesia for surgical work. The plant is a painkiller, sleep-inducer, sedative, and hallucinogen all at once.

The Cautious Harvest

Ancient Roman tradition prescribed an exacting rite for digging mandrake. The harvester was to stand with the wind at their back and, before breaking ground, draw three circles around the plant with the point of a sword. Only then, turning to the west, could the root be taken from the earth. Other accounts insist the plant must never be pulled by human hands at all: one end of a rope is tied to the root and the other to a dog, and the dog uproots it in the harvester's place.

The Mandrake Poppet

Medieval witches carved the harvested root into a poppet. A face was cut at the top, and the natural root threads were left dangling to serve as beard and hair. The figure was then wrapped in a small piece of cotton sheet for endless good luck. A successfully harvested and reverently kept mandrake was believed to grant its keeper intelligence, cure maladies, reveal hidden treasure, undo locks, and ward off evil spirits. The only true loss came from consuming it, at which point the keeper forfeited their wits, and often their life.

The Alraun and Alruna the Valkyrie

In Germanic folklore the Alraun is a tiny domestic imp whose body is made of mandrake root. He lives in a bottle, and if thrown away he returns persistently, like a rubber ball. In the Norse tradition Alruna is the name of a Valkyrie, the warrior woman who, like Atropos, chooses who will live and who will die. The mandrake is sacred to her. In German folklore the word alruna came to mean both mandrake and witch interchangeably.

The Devil's Apple

After the plant's star-shaped flower fades, mandrake produces a luscious ripe berry that earns it the name Devil's apple. All parts of the plant, including the leaves, roots, and that little fruit, carry the same hallucinogenic and hypnotic tropane alkaloids. The whole plant is poison.

Magickal Uses, Expanded

  • Aphrodisiac and lust: Mandrake fuels love potions, lust workings, and spells to invoke desire.
  • Dreamwork and astral travel: The root is carried or kept near the bed to deepen dreamwork and assist astral travel.
  • Protection, fertility, and prosperity: Worked as amulet, poppet, or root charm.
  • Undo locks and reveal hidden treasure: A successfully harvested root grants its keeper these powers.
  • Sacred to: Circe, Hekate, the Norse Valkyrie Alruna, and all witches.

A Symbol of Two Worlds

The Egyptians prized mandrake as an aphrodisiac, and from that reputation it grew into a symbol of love, though as a love philter it always had to be handled with care, since every part of the plant turns poisonous and narcotic when taken in quantity. Shaped like a vegetable yet wearing the likeness of an animal or a person, the root came to stand for the meeting of two seemingly opposite worlds, the green realm of plants and the breathing realm of flesh, part of why it was held in such awe and reverence as the emblem of the sorceress Circe.

The screaming-root legend still echoes in modern storytelling. In the Harry Potter books, students repotting mandrake seedlings must wear protective earmuffs against the plant's deadly cry, a direct descendant of the old belief that the shriek of the uprooted root could strike dead anyone who heard it.
