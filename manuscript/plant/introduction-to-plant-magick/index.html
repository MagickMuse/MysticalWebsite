Plant Grimoire

Introduction to Plant Magick

The art of working with plant spirits for healing, spellcraft, and transformation. This section covers the foundations of plant magick, from understanding plant consciousness to building your practice with herbs, wood, and botanical mixtures.

Flora held only a modest place among the goddesses of the Greek pantheon, yet her standing rose when her name was given to all the abundant plant life of the world. Flowers, plants, and fungi make up one half of this section, and trees the other.

Science can explain how a seed, given only darkness, soil, water, air, and sunlight, becomes something as magnificent as an oak. Even so, the moment green shoots break the surface remains as near to magick as anything a person can witness. For that reason the seed itself stands among our most significant sacred symbols.

Plants carry all the meaning of the Sun, since they draw their properties from the power they gather from it. The spiral spacing of leaves and the shapes of certain flowers can conceal the golden mean, the mystical spiral that expresses the mathematics of growth. In the Vedic tradition, plants with healing properties rank among the greatest gifts ever given to humankind and receive the same reverence as deities. We owe our knowledge of these useful plants to the intuition and experiments of ancestors who risked poisoning and even death to uncover their hidden natures.

The Garden of Life

For many thousands of years of human evolution, trees, plants, flowers, and herbs have surrounded us. The green world offered a lasting and beautiful backdrop to the early struggle for survival, stirring the senses in many ways. Beyond taste, sight, touch, and smell, plants speak through sound as well, since rustling leaves or the snap of a twig could warn of an intruder. Plants endure in the harshest climates and the unlikeliest places. The word "vegetable" descends from the Latin vegetare, meaning "to enliven," from vegetus, meaning "vigorous" or "active."

When humanity began to wonder about its own origins, it made sense to imagine that a God had shaped the first person in a Godlike form, using the same earth that gave rise to plants. The name Adam means "clay," and it is no coincidence that the paradise this first man inhabited was a garden. The plants around him were friends and companions, providing food, medicine, materials for shelter, shade on a hot day, and fuel through cold winters. They also gave the fibers that make fabric, and the first garment was a leaf.

Plants mark the turning of the seasons, dying and then returning each spring like long absent friends. With the rise of agriculture we learned to harness their energy for our own purposes and convenience. The variety in a modern kitchen, drawn from every corner of the planet, would have astonished our ancestors. That abundance rests on thousands of years of testing and experiment: eating plants raw, cooking them, smoking them, and combining them with other substances to see what might happen. Some experiments ended well, others badly. Even now certain mushrooms are listed as "edibility unknown," and many people likely died before poisonous plants were identified.

The Doctrine of Signatures

Out of necessity, our ancestors were a superstitious people. Searching for the secrets held within plants, they applied the Doctrine of Signatures, the belief that a thing relates to whatever it resembles. They had little else to guide them. The idea still carries influence despite advances in botanical science. The pomegranate and tomato became fertility symbols because of their abundant seeds. Folk names also reveal old interpretations. The pansy, also called heartsease, was said to cheer the heart and takes its name from the shape of its leaves. Hemlock earned the name "poison parsley" because it looks like parsley and is poisonous. The rule does not always hold, though. Mistletoe is called "all heal," yet the plant is poisonous.

Anyone with comprehensive knowledge of herbs was naturally accorded respect, for such a person could quite literally kill or cure. The Breton word for herb, louzaouenn, is also the word for medicine. This understanding of the plant kingdom reached even to the herbs and flowers that affect the mind, the plants that hold mind-altering chemicals.

Plants as Tools of Transition

Psychedelic and psychotropic substances act on the mind to offer an alternative view of reality, inducing dreams and visions and opening a gateway to another dimension, the realm of the Gods. These plants and their uses were preciously guarded, secrets held by those considered wise enough to read their messages: the shaman or sorcerer. To take such a substance was a rite of passage, a spiritual exploration wrapped in ceremony so the initiate would arrive safely at the other end of the journey.

Though we now understand these chemical constituents and how they affect us, the plants that contain them still hold sacred status, surrounded by extensive literature and myth. Alongside ayahuasca, opium, cannabis, peyote, and various mushrooms, several more common plant substances act in similar ways. Alcohol, for instance, is the result of plant material fermented with yeast and sugar.

⚠️
Ayahuasca, peyote, and related entheogenic rites are sacred to specific Indigenous traditions and are closed practices meant only for those within the originating cultures.

Flowers, Plants, and Fungi

Flowers are generally symbols of love, a fitting association since they are the sexual parts of plants. Because they fade and die so quickly, they also serve as a reminder of the transitory nature of the material world. In Welsh myth, Blodeuwedd, whose name means "blossoms," was a beautiful girl magickally constructed from thousands of flowers. Like the blooms that formed her, she proved fickle and betrayed her husband.

The symbolism of flowers seems limitless, with a myth for nearly every kind of flower on the planet, the Greeks being especially prolific. Flower and story work together to explain each bloom's "personality." Color also plays an important part in reading flower symbols. The Japanese art of flower arranging, Ikebana, uses flowers to write a kind of poetry tied to the cosmos, the seasons, the passing of time, and the elements, and it carries its own extensive symbolism. The flower also serves as a symbol of the soul. In Tantric and Taoist philosophy, the "Secret of the Golden Flower" is the attainment of spiritual union, an alchemical process that unifies the essence with breath, fire, and water, where the flower itself stands as an allegory for the Elixir of Life.

These flowers and plants are grouped together here, apart from trees, since some herbs are also flowers and some flowers carry value in herbal medicine.
