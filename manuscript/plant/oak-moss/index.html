Botanical Information

Evernia prunastri (Parmeliaceae family). Masculine moss and herb originating from Europe. Also known as Evernia, Tree Moss, and Lungwort. Lichen that attaches to oak and other rough-barked trees.

Herbal Lore

Small gray-green lichen attaches to oak and rough-barked trees. Moss-like fungi soaks up oils well, traditionally used as fixative in potpourri and perfume. Staying power of fixative translates to magickal longevity, folk practitioners stuff poppets with oakmoss to make magick have longer-lasting effect.

Botanical Description

A light green lichen of the Usneaceae family, found growing primarily on oak trees (Quercus robur) but sometimes on other species. Oak itself is indigenous to Europe and North America.

Origin & Distribution

The lichen is collected all over central and southern Europe, especially France, the former Yugoslavia, Hungary, and Greece, and also in Morocco and Algeria. The aromatic materials are prepared mainly in France, but also in the USA, Bulgaria, and the former Yugoslavia.

Folk Tradition (Medicinal)

A closely related lichen, Sticta pulmonaceae (also classified as Lobaria pulmonaria), is a greeny-brown lichen that grows on oak trees and is frequently harvested alongside E. prunastri. Known as oak lungs, lung moss, lungwort, or "lungs of oak," it has been used by Indigenous North American peoples for respiratory complaints and for treating wounds. It is called lobaria in the British Herbal Pharmacopoeia and is used for asthma, bronchitis, and coughs in children. Many types of lichen, especially the Parmelia group, are also used as vegetable dyes.

Other Species

Many varieties of lichen are used for their aromatic qualities. The most common are E. furfuracea and Usnea barbata, frequently gathered from spruce and pine trees and known as fir moss or tree moss in Europe (in the USA also called oakmoss, though they are less refined than the true E. prunastri). Other aromatic species include Sticta pulmonaceae (or Lobaria pulmonaria), Usnea ceratina, and members of the Ramalina, Alectoria, and Parmelia groups.
