History

First documented by the Greek physician Dioscorides in the first century C.E., mullein has a deep history in folk medicine as a remedy for coughs and colds. In the United States, it was listed in the National Formulary — one of the two official compendia comprising the U.S. Pharmacopeia — which was published annually through 1936. Native to Europe, the plant is believed to have been carried to North America by early colonists.

Characteristics

Mullein is a biennial herb with a distinctively woolly texture. In its first year, it grows large, soft leaves arranged in a ground-hugging rosette. In its second year, it sends up a tall central stalk that can exceed four feet in height, crowned with a dense cluster of yellow flowers. Both the leaves and flowers are used medicinally and magickally.

Magickal Uses

  • Peace and Reconciliation: Mullein is called upon to restore harmony and mend broken relationships. To heal a rift or right a wrong, combine a portion of mullein with a piece of rose quartz and a copper coin in a small sachet or charm pouch. Deliver it to those involved in the disagreement, or leave the charm at the location where the conflict occurred.

Physical Description

Mullein is a tall, straight plant with downy leaves. When dried, the down on its leaves and stem are excellent tinder.

Torches for the Dark Moon Goddess

In the days before wax was inexpensive, dried mullein stalks made excellent torches. They were used as such in the rites of the dark moon goddess, Hecate, to whom they are sacred.

Hecate's Sacred Illumination

Hecate, Queen of Witches, is completely nocturnal. She only accepts petitions after dark and she's fairly picky about forms of illumination. Mullein stalks are her favorite choice.

This history is reflected in mullein's many nicknames: The Hag's Taper, or the Witch's Taper, or Corpse Candle.

Graveyard Dust

Another nickname is graveyard dust. Hecate rules the frontier between death and life, often escorting people back and forth over the border.

Powdered mullein, her sacred herb, is considered an acceptable substitute for true cemetery dirt called for in magick spells.

Sacred to Oya

Mullein's associations with death aren't limited to Hecate or Eurasia. Mullein is also sacred to Oya, the spirit of Africa's Niger River, who has become increasingly prominent in African-Diaspora faiths where she is Queen of the Cemetery Gates.

Oya Among the Orisha

Oya is the only one among the Yoruba spirits, the orisha, who has no fear of the realm of the dead. Like Hecate, Oya is a powerful witch and herbalist who protects women and children.

Herbal Uses and Wild Animals

Mullein is used in various herbal preparations, particularly for ear infections. It was believed to ward off wild animals. (Animals in general will not consume mullein because the downy leaves irritate their throats.)

Sinister Reputation

However, because of its associations with dark goddesses and Pagan magick, mullein retained a somewhat sinister reputation and was identified as a witchcraft plant.

Magickal Teas

Mullein can be brewed as a magickal tea to aid between-the-worlds travel, bolster courage, support divination, promote health, and strengthen protection. Drink 1 to 2 cups only during the spell event itself.

Meaning When Gifted

When mullein is gifted it carries the meaning of good nature.

Witch's Taper

"Witch's taper" is one of mullein's nicknames, earned because before inexpensive candles, mullein stalks dipped in wax or tallow served as torches. They were evidently so useful that a popular justification claimed witch's tapers repelled witches, an odd idea considering mullein is among the holiest plants of Hekate. Either way, witch's tapers are said to protect witches and everyone else alike.
