Source — Specific Uses

Poplar and Black Poplar are known in the US (especially the South) as Balm of Gilead. The buds of tacamahac (Populus balsamifera) can substitute for balm of Gilead buds. P. candicans and its buds are also called Balm of Gilead. Black Poplar is the tree of heroes and a funeral tree sacred to Mother Earth. Like willow and aspen, poplar bark is useful as an anti-inflammatory and analgesic.

Gillie Buds Arthritis Salve: Boil black poplar buds with a small covering of olive oil for 10–15 minutes; strain out the buds. Add a small amount of beeswax to slightly thicken. Cap tightly. Rub on joints for arthritis. Gather and dry buds in spring.

The Witch's Flying Ointment

Poplar leaf buds appear in flying ointment formulas from the 1500s and 1600s. The sticky resinous buds of black poplar (P. nigra) and white poplar (P. alba) contain salicin, populin, and various flavonoids with analgesic and mildly sedative properties. The buds also served as a fat-soluble base into which other active compounds were incorporated, so poplar acted as both ingredient and carrier medium.

Underworld Associations

The poplar's connection to the underworld is ancient and cross-cultural. In Greek mythology, the white poplar was sacred to Persephone and the black poplar was associated with Hecate. Heracles wore a crown of white poplar on his descent into Hades. Aspens (a poplar subgenus) were said to tremble because they alone refused to mourn Persephone's abduction; in another tradition, they tremble because Heracles' crown touched their leaves in the underworld and brought the heat of that realm back to the surface.

Botanical Description (genus)

Poplars are tall, fast-growing deciduous trees recognized by their rounded to triangular leaves, which tremble in the slightest breeze due to flattened, flexible petioles. Black poplar (P. nigra) produces sticky, aromatic buds in early spring. White poplar (P. alba) has leaves that are white-felted on the underside. Aspens (P. tremula in Europe, P. tremuloides in North America) are the most recognized members of the genus.

The canonical page is keyed to Populus tremula (European Aspen). For Populus alba specifically, see White Poplar.
