Corn (Zea mays) is a tall annual grass that produces large ears with kernels of various colors.

Magickal Properties

  • Planet: Venus
  • Element: Earth
  • Powers: Protection, Luck, Divination

Folk Names

Indian Corn, Maize, Sacred Mother, Seed of Seeds, Zea

Ritual Uses

⚠️ Corn ceremony is sacred to many Indigenous peoples of the Americas, including the Hopi, Penobscot, Zuni, Iroquois, Cherokee, Maya, and many others. These rites should only be practiced by those within the originating cultures.

The Hopi grind white, blue, red, yellow, black and "sweet" (multicolored) corn for their rituals. They also make prayer sticks of corn. Corn is sacred to many Indigenous American peoples as a life-sustaining gift from the spirits.

Magickal Uses

For protection: A necklace made of dried red corn kernels prevents nosebleeds. Corn is scattered on the ground before ritual circles for protection. Place a corn cob in the cradle to protect the baby from negative influences.

For luck: Corn hung over a mirror brings good luck to the household. Blue corn is especially lucky.

For divination: To discover the location of a lost or stolen object, place a kernel of corn in the center of a white plate. Turn the plate around slowly three times, then sharply once. The kernel will point toward the direction in which the object lies.

Amulet Uses

A corn husk doll carried as an amulet brings a good harvest and luck. Corn kernels carried as an amulet draw money.

Source Book Notes

Planet: Sun · Element: Fire · Basic Power: Healing

Corn is easily digested by the body. Cornbread has more nourishment than wheat bread, making it suitable for those with liver or kidney disorders.

Folk Medicine

Corn carries protein, vitamins A, C, and B, antioxidants, and fiber. Unprocessed corn is a healthy carbohydrate that aids digestion, reduces inflammation, and can lower cholesterol. The bright pigments of colored corn signal antioxidants that strengthen the plant's anti-inflammatory action. Purple corn is also thought to reduce the risks of obesity, diabetes, and certain cancers.

Corn Silk

Corn silk, the yellow thread-like strands from the female flowers of maize, carries iron, potassium, and zinc among other minerals. A decoction made from the silk treats urinary tract infections, overactive bladder, kidney stones, edema, and bedwetting.

Offerings and Floor Sweeps

Corn is a fitting offering to ancestors. Cornmeal, corn flour, or cornstarch laid down as a magickal floor sweep draws prosperity into the home.

Fertility and Abundance

Corn and other large grain crops carry the current of fertility and abundance. Corn husk dollies are often used as magickal poppets during harvest season to represent children or other fertility deities.

Many Varieties

There are many, many varieties of corn beyond the basic yellow or white. In Peru, a specific variety of purple corn called ckolli, grown in the Andes, is brewed into chicha morada, a mildly sweet, somewhat tart, refreshing purple maize drink. Beyond the kernels, corn can be eaten raw or lightly cooked directly off the cob, and it lends itself to soups, stews, mash, bread, tortillas, dumplings, popcorn, cakes, and drinks.

Corn and the Peoples of the Americas

⚠️ The following lore belongs to Indigenous cultures of the Americas and is shared for educational reference only. Ceremonial work with corn within these traditions should be practiced by those within the originating cultures.

Corn was created some nine thousand years ago, hybridized from Zea luxurians, a wild grass. It is the only traditional Indigenous American food plant that needs humans, planting its seeds, in order to survive, binding corn and people together. From the Penobscot Corn Mother of the northeastern United States and Canada, to the Mayan Maize God Hun Hunahpu, to Uti Hiata, the Zuni Corn Maiden, there are as many corn deities as there are Indigenous cultures of the Americas. Maya agronomists transformed the native grass teosinte into the high-yield crop of maize, a feat often called the most significant achievement of Maya agronomy.

The Maya Creation

In the Maya creation stories, seven deities work alongside the Creator and finally succeed in shaping humans from yellow and white corn. The Hero Twins, two brothers, play a ball game at the demand of the gods of the Underworld; when they defeat their opponents, they are transformed into the moon and the sun, and their father becomes the Maize God. Corn sits at the center of the Maya calendar, and offerings of corn atole, a warm corn beverage, are made every two hundred and sixty days to welcome a new time cycle.

Three Sisters and the Ash Method

⚠️ This is a closed Indigenous practice belonging to Iroquois, Cherokee, and other Indigenous nations of the Americas. Documented here for educational reference.

Where corn is a major food crop, cooking it with other plants, especially beans, creates a complete protein. The traditional addition of ash creates potassium hydroxide, which softens the corn, makes it easier to digest, and unlocks the nutritive niacin held within the kernel. Colonists who learned of corn from the Indigenous nations of the Northeast and Southeast, particularly the Iroquois and the Cherokee, failed to recognize this step.

Pellagra and the Vampire Belief

Once corn was introduced to Europe, it flourished as an easy crop, and the foods made from it were filling. Without the ash step that unlocks niacin, however, people grew full but vitamin deficient, and a niacin deficiency disease now known as pellagra spread. Its marks include pale skin, mania, sensitivity to sunlight, confusion, and aggression. Many researchers believe this condition was mistaken for vampirism and helped drive the widespread belief in and persecution of vampires, witches, and their companions.

European Corn Confusion

Like pumpkins, corn is not a plant indigenous to Europe, and yet it appears in many fairy tales and myths. Demeter, the Greek goddess of agriculture, is often called the goddess of corn; her Roman counterpart Ceres and the Norse god Thor are spoken of in the same way. One source of confusion is that the word corn was long used in English to mean any grain crop, including barley and wheat. European tales that name corn as a grain crop may have been retold after maize was introduced, or may refer to a different staple crop entirely. Corn has since become a resilient European crop, especially in sunnier regions, and even in the British Isles it has found some success, which is why corn as we know it has worked its way into British and European folk literature.

The Follet

In English folklore, the follet is a creature not unlike a faun or a satyr, goat-footed or cloven-hooved, more mischievous than evil. The follet is closely associated with Robin Goodfellow, also known as Puck, and is tied to nature and agriculture, helpful, if a little wild. Whether named Puck, Pan, Follet, or Robin, this goblin beastie offers to harvest the corn as hard labor in exchange for a bowl of cream. The bartering between humans and fairy folk runs through the whole folkloric record: a bowl of milk, cakes, and sometimes wine are all that is asked in return for helping hands. Grow lazy, forgetful, or worse, leave out a poor offering, and chaos follows. These creatures can be as destructive as they are helpful.

The Dwarfs of Göttingen

A German tale recalls a time when Dwarfs at June near Göttingen used to slip into the fields and steal sheaves of corn. They did this all the more easily by wearing caps that made them invisible, and they injured one man in particular who had a great deal of corn. At length he set a rope around his field at noon, and when the Dwarfs went to creep under it, the rope knocked the caps from their heads. Now visible, they were caught. They gave him many fair words, promising if he would take the rope away to give him a peck of money, if he came to the same place before sunrise. He agreed, but a friend whom he consulted told him to go not at sunrise but a little before midnight, since the new day truly begins at that hour. He did as directed and found the Dwarfs there, unprepared for him, with the peck of money waiting. A cloak, cap, or other means of becoming invisible runs as a thread through many fairy tales.

Dream Omen

To dream of fields of corn, or of being among unthreshed corn, is a favorable omen and points to success in business. For a lover it foretells marriage, many children, and growing rich and happy. To dream of gathering ripe corn is one of the most fortunate dreams one can have.
