Willow (Salix spp.) is the water-loving tree whose name is said to come from the Celtic sal-lis, meaning "near water." It thrives along riverbanks, springs, and wells throughout the northern hemisphere, where its bark yields salicylic acid (the natural source of aspirin) and a powerful rooting hormone that helps other plants take hold. Long honored as a wand-wood and witching rod, it is sacred to nymphs, water sprites, and other water elementals, especially in Ireland and Wales.

Source — Specific Uses

The bark taken as tea is a pain reliever — the original aspirin. A forked branch is used to dowse for water or underground tanks. Plant a willow in the garden to guard your home. Willow groves give mystic eloquence and poetic skills; the ancients went to these groves to divine by listening to the wind. The Muses are connected with the willow; the Grove of Persephone (goddess of trances) was a willow grove. A branch with fuzzy catkins in the house brings good luck and good health. Bark tea used for intestinal worms, chronic diarrhea, ovarian pain; warm bark compresses help with foot bunions and corns.

The Weeping Willow

The weeping willow grows with a downward arc rather than reaching upward like its more upright cousins, and this single trait carries most of its grave-side lore. It is one of the most common trees carved onto headstones, where it stands for both a life cut short and the promise of immortality. One older tradition holds that the rod used to beat Christ on the cross was cut from a willow, and that the tree bends to the earth ever since in grief and shame.

Water Spirit Sanctuary

Willows grow where water moves, and the spirits of water move with them. In Irish and Welsh lore the tree is sacred to nymphs, water sprites, and other water elementals. In Russian folklore the rusalki, river-spirits of the riverbanks, weave their homes from reeds and bent willow branches. A Czech tale recounts a willow nymph who walked among mortals by day but always returned to her tree by night. She married a man and bore him children, until he cut down her willow, at which moment his wife dropped dead. The felled wood was carved into a cradle that could lull the orphaned child instantly to sleep, and twigs from the stump that had been her body could be cut into a pipe, through which the grown child still spoke with the mother on the other side.

The Sighing Willow

In one old Japanese tale a widow named Ayame loses her only son, Umewaki, on the fifteenth day of the third month, the day sacred to the awakening of spring. After she finds his grave, she discovers a sacred willow planted beside it. Every evening she goes to the graveside, the slender tree stirs in the wind, and the whispered sound that comes through its branches is her son's voice speaking softly to her from his place of rest. The willow becomes the line between the living mother and the dead child.

The willow's voice is not always gentle. Travelers in deep willow swamps describe a low audible humming that can be heard only when the wind dies down, and a sense that the dense, crowding bushes are watching, waiting, and listening from every direction. Where willows mass thickly along a river, the tree's old kinship with water spirits seems to thicken with them.

A Plant That Helps Other Plants Root

Willow bark contains a powerful natural rooting hormone, which is why nearly every commercial rooting compound is, in effect, a powdered form of willow. To make your own, soak branches of willow in water for about two weeks. Use the resulting water on any new cuttings to help them set roots in the garden.

Indigenous Basketry and Medicine

⚠️
CLOSED PRACTICE: The following uses belong to specific Indigenous peoples of Northern California (Pomo, Round Valley Pomo, Yokia, Kashaya Pomo) and should only be practiced by those within those traditions. They are included here for cultural context.

Willow is one of the great basketry plants of the world. The Pomo of Northern California use willow as the primary fiber for the cradle-baskets that carry babies. The Round Valley Pomo use Salix argyrophylla, called Bäm kä-lã, to weave baskets, arrows, and fish-catching traps. The Yokia of Ukiah favor Salix lasiolepis, called Shkä, whose fibrous inner bark is twisted into rope and even worked into fiber clothing; the same plant is used medicinally, in topical decoctions for itches and in teas to reduce fever and ease chills. The coastal Kashaya Pomo use the root of the white willow, Salix hindsiana, twined into baskets, and apply the bark or leaves to treat sore throats.

Mourning, the Afterlife, and the Voice of the Leaves

Of all trees the willow is the one most bound to death and mourning, a kinship written even into the name of the weeping variety. Its branches were laid along the tops of coffins and used to line graves, for they were believed to ease the passage of the soul into the Afterlife, and in ancient Britain burial grounds were planted with willows to shelter the spirits of the dead. It is a tree for Samhuin rites, when the veil thins and the living would speak with the spirit world.

The willow's gift of intuition and clairaudience flows from its watery, Moon-ruled nature. The sighing of wind through its branches makes it the tree of phyllomancy, the art of divination from the sound of leaves, and it was this same whispering that Orpheus is said to have carried with him as a token of inspiration. In China the willow is also a tree of immortality, for a twig that looks long dead will root and sprout into new life even when planted upside down. It stands for the letter S, Saille, in the Ogham tree alphabet.
