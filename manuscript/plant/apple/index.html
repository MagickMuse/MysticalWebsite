Magickal Properties

Gender: Feminine

Planet: Venus

Element: Water

Deities: Venus, Dionysus, Olwen, Apollo, Hera, Athena, Aphrodite, Diana, Zeus, Iduna

Powers: Love, Healing, Garden Magick, Immortality

Ritual Uses

August 13 was Diana's Festival in Greece (Venus' in Rome) and on this day a ritual meal was prepared, part of which consisted of apples still hanging on their boughs.

Wiccan altars are often piled high with apples on Samhain, for the apple is considered to be one of the foods of the dead. For this very reason Samhain is sometimes known as "Feast of Apples."

The apple is a symbol of immortality. A branch of the apple which bore buds, flowers and fully-ripened fruit (sometimes known as the Silver Bough), was a kind of magickal charm which enabled its possessor to enter into the land of the Gods, the Underworld, in Celtic mythology.

Magickal Uses

The apple has long been used in spells of love. The blossoms are added to love sachets, brews and incenses, and they are infused in melted pink wax, then strained out, to make candles suitable to burn for attracting love.

A simple apple love spell consists of cutting an apple in half and sharing it with your loved one. This ensures that you will be happy together. A similar spell directs you to hold an apple in your hands until warm, and then give it to your intended. If he or she eats it your love will be returned.

Cut an apple in two and count the number of seeds. If they are even, marriage will soon occur. If one of the seeds is cut it may be a stormy relationship. If two are cut, widowhood is foretold. However, if an uneven number of seeds are found the woman will remain unmarried in the near future.

For healing, cut an apple into three pieces, rub each on the affected part of the body, and then bury them. Do this during the waning of the Moon to banish illnesses. To ensure that you do not contract a fever, eat an apple.

If you are a gardener, pour cider onto freshly-turned earth to give it life just before planting. Also pour libations on roots just before tree rituals. And if you grow apples, bury thirteen leaves of an apple tree after harvest to ensure a good crop of apples next year.

Use apple cider in place of blood where it is called for in old recipes. Apple wood makes excellent magickal wands, especially suited to emotional magick, as well as love rites.

  • Alphabet divination: Peel an apple while simultaneously reciting the alphabet. When the peel breaks, the letter you had reached is your true love's initial. Continue reciting through the alphabet if the peel holds, until it either breaks or is removed completely.

Safety Notes

⚠️ Seeds, in large amounts: Poison - Apple seeds contain compounds that can be toxic in large quantities.

The Hidden Pentacle

Apples are magickal fruits. Slice them in half horizontally and the star or pentacle secretly hidden within is revealed. This five-pointed star embedded in the apple's core has long been recognized by magickal practitioners as a sign of the apple's inherent sacred geometry.

In ancient days, apples were associated with love, lust, and pleasure, but eventually love, lust, and pleasure fell out of grace and apples became identified with witches and the devil.

Aphrodisiac Properties

The apple is infamous as the fruit that Eve gave to Adam, a symbol of sexual awakening. All seed-bearing fruits are considered aphrodisiac; their numerous seeds, texture, scent, and color make them a naturally sensual foodstuff.

The Biblical Apple Debate

The most famous apple of all may be the one with which Eve tempted Adam; the story is often told as if the apple were a euphemism for sex. Apples were already long associated with love, sex, and forbidden pleasures when Christianity came to prominence, whereupon translations then identified the apple as the fruit of the Tree of Knowledge.

However, apples are native to temperate regions and are not indigenous to the region where Bible stories were first told. Many biblical scholars are absolutely certain that apples were not the forbidden fruit (figs, quinces, and pomegranates are the front-runners, although of course the argument has been made that all trees existed in the Garden of Eden, therefore the forbidden fruit could be anything).

From Love Symbol to Witches' Tool

Apples became synonymous with sex, sin, and feminine wiles. Fairy-tale apples, like the one the wicked witch-queen feeds Snow White, look seductively beautiful and innocently tasty but are secretly poisonous and perversely dangerous.

Despite (or perhaps because of) these associations, apples remained prized love spell ingredients—there are literally hundreds of love spells featuring little more than apples. Perhaps for this reason, apples became classified as more than just a food; they were witches' tools, especially those bright scarlet apples.

Apples in Love and Happiness

Apples were once considered the fruits of life, symbols of love and happiness. The "golden apples of the sun" were associated with glorious goddesses like Freya, Hera, Idunn, and (especially) Aphrodite. Apples had their very own deity, the apple-goddess Pomona.

Post-Christianity, apples were re-envisioned as the forbidden fruit of the Garden of Eden, emblems of sin and desire. Associations with goddesses evolved into associations with witches: poison apples are the fairy-tale witch's favorite tool. No recipes for poison apples are included here: fairy tales to the contrary, witchcraft favors apples for love, seduction, and divination, not for cursing.

Apples are primary ingredients of special Halloween recipes and simple, popular apple spells.

Rune Candle Magick

Apple wood makes an excellent scribe for carving runes into candles when your query or cast concerns love or relationships. The wood's Venus associations amplify romantic and relational intentions.

Amulet Uses

An apple seed carried as an amulet draws love.

Moon Magick

Key themes for lunar workings: Love, healing, longevity, fertility, wisdom, magick.

Hera's Tree and the Three Maidens of the West

The Greek goddess Hera received a sacred apple tree as a gift from Zeus. Its golden apples grant immortality to anyone who eats them, and the tree is guarded by the Hesperides, nymph goddesses of the evening and the west. Juno, Hera's Roman counterpart, was also gifted a golden apple tree. Hers is protected by the Three Maidens of the West, who dance around the tree singing of a new tree springing from the roots of the old, of leaves unfolding fresh blossoms, of boughs hung heavy with apples of gold.

Atalanta and the Three Golden Apples

The Greek heroine Atalanta, raised in the forest first by bears and then by hunters, vowed to marry only the suitor who could outrun her in a footrace. Losing meant death. Hippomenes, sometimes called Melanion, was chosen as the race's judge and fell in love with her on sight. Before the race he prayed to Aphrodite, who had already kindled his love, and the goddess slipped him three golden apples from her own hand with whispered instructions. As Atalanta gained on him, Hippomenes tossed an apple onto the path. She stopped to pick it up. He threw the second, and at the third Aphrodite herself urged Atalanta to claim the apple. Hippomenes reached the finish line first, and the two married. On their wedding day they neglected to thank Aphrodite. The angered goddess turned them both into lions and yoked them to her own golden chariot.

Idun's Apples of Asgard

In Norse tradition, the most delicious golden apples in the world grow in the gardens tended by Idunn, goddess of youth. The other gods come to her for these apples to maintain their immortality. The Heroes of Asgard especially love them, because a few bites of one of Idun's apples leaves them rejuvenated and full of joy. They are the most requested fruit on any journey.

The Talking Apple Tree

In one folktale, a girl falls through a well into another world where bread cries to be taken from the oven and an apple tree calls out to be shaken. She shakes the tree until every ripe apple falls, gathers them carefully in a heap, and is rewarded with riches for her industriousness. Her lazy stepsister, who walks past the very same tree without helping, receives only humiliation in return. Conversing with apple trees is not unusual in folklore: the trees know, and they answer those who tend them.

The Golden Apple Thieves

In several tales, an emperor's golden apple tree blossoms and bears fruit overnight, only for the fruit to vanish before dawn. The thieves turn out to be a flock of enchanted birds, sometimes peahens, cranes, pheasants, or swans, one of whom shapeshifts into a beautiful maiden who enchants the guard. She leaves an apple or two behind so that the watcher appears to have succeeded, and the secret of the theft is finally exposed by a supernatural helper such as a witch or a werewolf.

The Silver Branch and the Otherworld

In Irish lore, golden apples grow on the Silver Branch, the white and silvery bough of the apple tree of the Otherworld. The queen of the Land of the Ever-Living and Ever-Young gives the branch, or sometimes a single apple from it, to the mortals she wishes as her companions. The branch serves as passport, as food, and as drink for those who travel with her. Its music is so soothing that any mortal who hears it forgets all sorrow and ceases to grieve for those whom the fairy women take.

Conn-eda and the Golden Apples of Lough Erne

An Irish story tells of Conn-eda, favored son of a king of Connacht. His scheming stepmother sets a chess match: the loser must journey to the Firbolg realm beneath the twin lakes and return with three golden apples, a black horse, and a supernatural puppy. Conn-eda loses. Aided by a powerful Druid, kind and fearless, he completes the quest. He plants the three golden apples in his garden, and a great tree springs up heavy with golden fruit. Soon all the land is abundant with grain and orchards, and the region is named for him: Connacht.

Wassailing the Orchard

The practice of apple howling, also called apple wassailing, has been recorded since the mid-sixteenth century. A favored apple tree in the orchard is chosen and surrounded with singing and dancing. Cider is poured onto its roots as libation. Something loud is fired off to ward away evil spirits. Bread soaked in cider is placed within its branches as an offering. Revelers move orchard to orchard, carrying the blessing forward and asking each tree for an abundant harvest. See Apple Wassailing.

Bobbing for Apples

A Halloween custom popular in Ireland, Wales, and parts of Britain and Europe. In Welsh it is called twco am 'falau. A dozen or so apples are tossed into a tub of cold water and players try to grab them with their teeth, hands held behind their backs. A more dangerous variant suspends an apple on one end of a stick and a burning candle on the other, hung by string, with the player tasked to bite the apple without singeing their hair. See Bobbing for Apples.

Queen of the Garden

While a mortal queen may walk her garden tracing the lines of a velvet petal, the true sovereign of plant life is the Queen of the Fairies. Named among the Fairy queens are Shakespeare's Titania and Celtic Queen Maeve, true rulers of flower, stem, and leaf, alongside the creatures, natural and supernatural, who guard and nourish every plant.

Avalon and the World Tree

The apple is a tree of the Underworld and of immortality, held sacred to Apollo. The mythical Isle of Avalon, whose name means orchard from the old Welsh afal for apple, is the resting place of Celtic kings and heroes, and one of the places where King Arthur is said to wait until the hour he is needed to rise again and defend his people. For the Celts the apple tree stood for the World Tree itself, the axis of the universe, and the apple was reckoned the most magickal of all fruits, a fruit of immortality and prophecy. At Samhain, the season of the apple harvest, the fruit takes a leading role in the rites and revels, including practices of divination.

The Pentagram and the Golden Spiral

Cut across its equator, with the stalk standing at the top, the apple reveals five pips held within a five-pointed star. That pentagram can in turn be drawn into the golden spiral, while the round body of the fruit speaks of eternity. In the biblical tale, when Eve drew Adam to eat from the Tree of Knowledge she also passed him the hidden star within, an emblem of the spiritual nature of humankind, though scripture never names the fruit as an apple. Eating it awakened him to new possibilities, the flesh joined to the spirit, and at once the pair covered themselves, a sign of sexual awakening. Something of this survives in the wedding custom of cutting the apple across the middle so that bride and groom each eat a half.

The Paradox of Good and Evil

In Latin the word for apple, malum, also means evil, and the fruit carries that double face as a symbol of both good and ill. In the tales of the Arabian Nights the apple of Prince Ahmed cures every illness, while in the story of Snow White the heroine is felled by a gleaming red apple offered by the witch and sinks into a sleep like oblivion.
