Parsley (Petroselinum crispum; P. sativum) is an ancient herb of the dead, sacred to Persephone, used in funerary rites, Samhain workings, lust, protection, and purification.

History

Parsley has been cultivated and used for more than two thousand years. The Cherokee employed it as a tonic for bladder ailments. In ancient Greece it was a funerary herb, with wreaths of parsley laid upon tombs to honor the dead. The taproot, seeds, and leaves were all used medicinally.

Characteristics

A popular biennial culinary herb, parsley bears finely divided leaves and clusters of small yellow flowers. It grows slowly — germination can be quite lengthy — and may reach up to three feet in height by its second year. It is widely recognized as both a garnish and a culinary flavoring.

Magickal Teas

Parsley can be brewed as a magickal tea to support purification and strengthen protection. Drink 1 to 2 cups only during the spell event itself.

  • Death and ancestral offerings: The ancient Greek practice of using parsley as a funerary herb informs its modern magickal use. Placing parsley as a garnish at meals carries the tradition of offering respect to those who came before — honoring the lives given so that others might eat.
  • Samhain altar decoration: Wreaths or fresh bundles of parsley make appropriate adornments for the Samhain altar, honoring the dead in keeping with the herb's ancient associations.
  • Underworld journeys: The legendary slow germination of parsley — said to occur because the seeds must travel to the underworld and back before rooting — links it to threshold work and communication with the dead.

Botanical Description

A biennial or short-lived perennial herb of the Apiaceae family, up to 70 cm high, with crinkly bright green foliage, small greenish-yellow flowers, and small brown seeds.

Origin & Distribution

Native to the Mediterranean region, especially Greece. Cultivated extensively, mainly in California, Germany, France, Belgium, Hungary, and parts of Asia. The principal oil-producing countries are France, Germany, Holland, and Hungary.

Folk Tradition (Medicinal)

A very nutritious plant, high in vitamins A and C, also used to freshen the breath. The herb and seed are used medicinally, principally for kidney and bladder problems, but also for menstrual difficulties, digestive complaints, arthritis, rheumatism, rickets, and sciatica. The plant is also said to stimulate hair growth and help eliminate head lice. The root is current in the British Herbal Pharmacopoeia as a specific for flatulent dyspepsia with intestinal colic.

Other Species

There are over thirty-seven different varieties of parsley, including the curly-leaved type (P. crispum), which is used in herbal medicine and matches the existing scientific name on this entry.

Three Forms From One Plant

Parsley yields multiple commercially distinct aromatic products:

  • Parsley Seed Essential Oil — steam distilled from the seeds, apiol-dominant
  • Parsley Herb Essential Oil — steam distilled from the leaves and stalks, myristicin-dominant
  • Oleoresin — solvent extracted from the seeds, used in flavouring

An essential oil is also occasionally distilled from the roots.

The Devil's Herb and the Slow Seed

For all its place in the kitchen, parsley has carried an uneasy reputation, named an evil plant in some countries even as it was prized for cooking. The ancient Greeks held it the herb that most spoke of death, strewing it over graves, and they served it with meat to calm the spirit of the slaughtered animal. Its famously slow germination earned a darker explanation: the seed was said to journey down to the Devil and back nine times before it would sprout. Folk wisdom held that parsley grows best in the garden of a household where the woman rules over the man, and that the only safe day to sow it is Good Friday, the one day when Satan holds no sway over the soil.
