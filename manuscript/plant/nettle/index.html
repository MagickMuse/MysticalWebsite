History

In ancient Rome, soldiers stationed far from the warmth of the Mediterranean would gather bundles of nettle leaves and strike themselves with them to generate body heat in colder climates — a practice called urtication, named for the plant itself, which persists as a folk remedy to this day. Stinging nettle was also considered sacred to Thor, the Norse god of thunder, and was thrown onto fires as a protective measure against lightning strikes.

Characteristics

Stinging nettle is covered in tiny, hollow hairs across both the leaf and stem. These hairs are filled with venom and release histamine on contact with skin, causing irritation and the characteristic sting. Once dried or cooked, however, the plant completely loses its sting — dried leaves are histamine-free, and stewing them neutralizes their irritating properties, making the plant a valued remedy for inflammatory conditions such as arthritis.

Historical Uses and Distribution

The nettles are a family of plants widely distributed over Earth and were once considered very beneficial and widely used. Cloth was spun from nettles. The plant supplied the thread used by Germans and Scandinavians prior to the introduction of flax. Nettle fiber makes a strong, good thread, and nettle fabric was pressed into service for German army uniforms during the First World War, when other material ran short.

The tops of the leaves may be cooked and are very nutritious. (Stinging nettles really do sting and must be picked with gloves; however once dried or cooked, the sting is gone.) Many beneficial medicinal uses exist.

From Beneficial to Evil: The Transformation

By the Middle Ages, however, in the same places where it had once been prevalent and much used, stinging nettles were so associated with witchcraft that possession was grounds for accusations of being a witch. How did this once beneficial plant develop such an evil reputation?

A Woman's Friend

Although nettles are used to dissolve gallbladder stones, heal wounds, and to relieve the stiffness of arthritis, its primary medicinal associations are largely female-oriented. Stinging nettles are a woman's friend.

Labor and Childbirth

Traditional medicinal uses included soothing and hastening labor, so the nettle became perceived as a demonic plant because Eve had been doomed to suffer in childbirth. Attempts to relieve labor pains were considered Pagan, sinful, and defiant.

Galactogogue Properties

Stinging nettles have other uses: they are classified as a galactogogue, meaning that they stimulate and increase a woman's milk supply. That's a fairly innocuous use.

Ancient Contraception

However, honey mixed with the juice of Roman nettles (Urtica pilulifera) and applied to a strip of linen inserted vaginally prior to intercourse was an early attempt at contraception in ancient Egypt, as well as the bordellos of ancient Rome. The honey worked as a barrier. Nettle juice may have some spermicidal properties.

Wild Women and the Botanical Equivalent of Snakes

Nettles represented wilderness, wild women, and the general quality of being wild. Because they sting and because the juice of nettles provides the antidote for that sting, nettles were identified as the botanical equivalent of snakes, whose venom both heals and harms.

Snakes were understood as the animal companion of Satan. Nettles were perceived as diabolical plants.

Stimulating Lust

Consuming them allegedly stimulated lust, which perhaps doesn't seem so bad today, but was, once upon a time, among those sins for which witches, especially alluring, enchanting ones, were blamed. Nettles came to represent witches; they share the witches' essence and back then that wasn't meant as a compliment.

Lamia and Blind Nettles

The botanical name for what is known in English as blind nettles—Lamium album—derives from Lamia, often understood as a synonym for "witch." Lamia, in mythology, was a tragic queen reduced to stealing, killing, and maybe consuming other women's babies.

Guardian Plants: The Botanical Watchdog

Stinging nettles are traditionally used in witchcraft to remove curses and break spells. They are protective, guardian plants. Their stinging, prickly nature epitomizes their watchdog nature.

What type of dog is most frequently chosen to serve as a guard? A cute, little, fluffy one or a dog that at least looks like it could inflict some damage? The trade in Rottweilers, Dobermans, and pit bull terriers says it all. Stinging nettles are their botanical equivalent. With stinging nettles on your side, who would trespass against you? Or so many thought.

Nettle Shirts: Power Turned Against Witches

The power of stinging nettles was cruelly turned against convicted witches. Witches were frequently dressed in nettle shirts when they were led to the funeral pyres. This was for many reasons:

  • To break their magic and nullify any potential last spells or curses that the witch might cast, because the judges were afraid of their victims
  • To visually identify them as witches, lest bystanders forget why they were being burned
  • To signify the Satanic pact by the use of this diabolical plant
  • To discourage others from wanting any contact with the stinging nettle—only witches would continue to use them
  • Merely to torture them even more with this botanical equivalent of a hair shirt

Growing Among Ruins

It didn't help that stinging nettles, like mugwort, grows most prolifically among stone ruins and in the cemetery.

The Wild Swans: Awareness of Injustice

However, the fairy tale The Wild Swans suggests some awareness of injustice toward the nettles, magical practice, and practitioners of witchcraft.

Thor's Plant and the Lightning Roof

The nettle was the plant of Thor, god of thunder, and shared his bond with thunder and lightning; its old German name, Donnernessel, means thunder nettle. In deference to the god, bundles of nettles were thrown over the roofs of houses to turn the lightning away from the home.

Remote Healing and the Borrowed Pain

Nettles had a place in healing at a distance. The healer would grasp the plant firmly and speak the patient's name aloud, the sting drawing the suffering off the sick person and onto the one performing the rite, who took the pain into their own hands.

Courage, Blood, and Desire

Paired with yarrow, the nettle was said to lend courage to whoever carried the two together. A decoction of its leaves was held to clear the blood, and the same brew served as an aphrodisiac.

  • Courage sachet: Sew stinging nettle into a sachet or carry it in a pouch to amplify courage and personal fortitude in the face of challenge.
  • Virility: Incorporate into workings intended to increase vitality, masculine energy, and virility.
  • Lightning ward: In the Norse tradition, throw a bundle of nettle onto a fire to protect against lightning and invoke Thor's protective power.

From the source book: Deities: Osiris, Isis, Odin. Stuff a poppet with nettles to remove a curse and send it back to the sender — write the name of the sender on the poppet and then bury or burn it. Add to protection sachets and burn during exorcism ceremonies. The stinging quality disappears when dried.

Magickal Teas

Nettle can be brewed as a magickal tea to support healing, draw love, strengthen protection, and ward negativity. Drink 1 to 2 cups only during the spell event itself.
