Evocative Folk Names

Common club moss has an amazing number of evocative folk names. In English it's known as:

  • Devil's claw
  • Wolf's claw
  • Snake moss
  • Witch's dust
  • Witch's dance
  • Earth Sulfur

In German, it's called Hexenkraut, "witch's herb."

These names reveal the plant's deep connections to witchcraft, shamanism, and the wild powers of nature.

Druidic Sacred Herb

Club moss is one of the sacred herbs of the Druids. It could only be collected by a true priest or priestess who had first taken a purification bath in a running stream. Dressed in white, they made offerings of bread and wine before cutting the herb with a silver dagger. It is an herb of great blessings and protection. The spores were collected in July and August, and fresh plants were used in spells.

Sacred Plant and Ritual Harvest

Long considered a sacred plant according to the Roman Pliny, its harvest first required a sacrifice of bread and mead. Wolf's claw was then gathered with the left hand, while adorned in white robes standing barefoot beneath a New Moon.

This elaborate harvesting ritual—requiring offerings, specific lunar timing, ritual clothing, and barefoot contact with the earth—demonstrates the profound respect and spiritual protocols required to work with this sacred plant.

The Left Hand

Gathering with the left hand is significant in magical practice. The left hand is often associated with:

  • Receptive energy
  • Lunar power
  • The path of witchcraft and magic
  • Drawing in spiritual force

New Moon Timing

The New Moon (or Dark Moon) is the most potent time for magic involving:

  • New beginnings
  • Hidden knowledge
  • Shadow work
  • Deep mysteries

The requirement to harvest wolf's claw during this lunar phase connects it to the most powerful and liminal magical workings.

The Yellow Spore Dust: Lightning Powder

Although the plant has various other magical uses, the dust from its spores made wolf's claw an important shamanic tool.

Many Names for the Dust

This yellow spore dust is known as:

  • Witches' flour
  • Druids' flour
  • Elven flour
  • And perhaps most accurately as lightning powder

Each name reveals a different tradition that valued this substance: witches, druids, and the realm of the fae all recognized its power.

Thunder and Lightning Effects

It's oily and if tossed onto flames explodes with a burst similar to thunder and lightning.

This dramatic pyrotechnic property made wolf's claw spore dust invaluable to shamans and magical practitioners. The ability to command fire and create the appearance of lightning gave practitioners powerful theatrical and ritual tools.

From Sacred Tool to Special Effect

Today it's perceived as only a special effect; magical illusionists remain enamored with it, but once upon a time it was considered magical and used to great effect by shamans.

This evolution reflects a broader pattern: what was once understood as genuinely magical power becomes relegated to mere "trick" or "illusion" in the modern era. Yet the power remains—both in its physical properties and in its ritual applications.

Shamanic Applications

Shamans used lightning powder to:

  • Demonstrate their connection to thunder and sky powers
  • Create awe and spiritual authority during rituals
  • Invoke the presence of storm deities
  • Mark liminal moments in ceremony
  • Frighten away malevolent spirits (who fear fire and lightning)

The dramatic burst of flame would have been understood not as illusion but as the shaman's genuine ability to command the forces of nature.

Medicinal Use

(The spore powder also has medicinal use.)

Beyond its magical and shamanic applications, wolf's claw spore powder has been used in traditional medicine, demonstrating the plant's multi-layered utility in folk practice.
