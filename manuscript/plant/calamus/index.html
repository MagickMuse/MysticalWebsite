Calamus (Acorus calamus), also called Sweet Flag, is an aromatic aquatic herb of love, healing, and altered states — one of the oldest psychoactive plants in use and cited among the ingredients of traditional flying ointment formulas.

History

Calamus has a recorded history stretching back to biblical times, appearing as both a fever remedy and a culinary seasoning. It is mentioned in the Hebrew Bible as an ingredient in the holy anointing oil of Exodus. Ancient Egyptians valued it as an aphrodisiac and tonic, and in Ayurvedic tradition it is considered sacred to Brahma, used to enhance intelligence, memory, and speech. Native American peoples throughout the Great Lakes region used it extensively for medicine, ceremony, and as a stimulant chewed for alertness on long journeys.

Characteristics

Calamus thrives in wetland environments — along the edges of ponds, lakes, swamps, and streams — and is found across midlatitude continents. Its sword-shaped leaves closely resemble those of the iris family. The rhizome is intensely aromatic, warm, and spicy, with a scent somewhere between ginger, cinnamon, and turpentine.

Other Names & Related Species

Known by many names across cultures: Sweet Flag, Sweet Sedge, Sweet Rush, Sweet Cane, Myrtle Grass, Cinnamon Sedge, and the older botanical synonym Calamus aromaticus. The variety used commercially for essential oil production is Acorus calamus var. angustatus. The name itself comes from the Greek calamos, meaning "reed."

Despite the resemblance, calamus should not be confused with the Yellow Flag iris (Iris pseudacorus), which it looks similar to from a distance but is botanically unrelated. Several other aromatic sedges, mostly Eastern, exist as well, including Calamus odoratus, used in India as a medicine and perfume.

Folk Medicine & Modern Use

The healing properties of calamus come almost entirely from the aromatic oil held in the rhizome. The dried root has long been esteemed as an aromatic stimulant and tonic for fever (including typhoid), nervous complaints, vertigo, headaches, and dysentery.

The root remains current in the British Herbal Pharmacopoeia for acute and chronic dyspepsia, gastritis, intestinal colic, anorexia, and gastric ulcer. In Turkey and especially in India, where it is treasured in traditional medicine, calamus rhizome is sold as a candied sweet for dyspepsia, bronchitis, and coughs.

The essential oil, however, is a different story. Modern research has identified beta-asarone (especially abundant in the Indian variety, up to 80 percent) as both an oral toxin and a carcinogen. The oil is therefore reserved for perfumery only, never therapy. See the Calamus Essential Oil entry for full safety details.

Magickal Uses

  • Love: Dried root carried or added to sachets to attract love; root pieces strewn around the home to encourage affection.
  • Money & Luck: Root placed in the four corners of a kitchen to draw prosperity; carried in a mojo bag for financial luck.
  • Healing: Used in healing sachets and incense blends; the scent is considered soothing to the nervous system.

As a strewing herb: When used as a strewing herb on floors, calamus subtly perfumes the room as it is walked upon.

As a culinary substitute: The aromatic root can substitute for cinnamon, nutmeg, and ginger in recipes.

  • Protection: Planted near doorways or carried to ward off negative energies.
  • Uncrossing: A key herb for breaking hexes, lifting jinxes, and removing obstacles placed in your path.
  • Divination: Sprinkle dried calamus on your altar and rest your scrying mirror or crystal on top to sharpen scrying sessions and reveal truth over illusion.

Commanding and Spellwork Uses

Calamus was the traditional material for reed pens in ancient Egypt and Mesopotamia. Thoth, the Egyptian deity of writing and magick, is said to write with a calamus reed pen to give his spells their commanding force. Using a calamus reed pen for spellwork and important ritual writing carries this same authoritative energy. For additional commanding potency, add a drop of bergamot essential oil to any ritual ink before writing.

The Witch's Flying Ointment

Sweet flag root appears in historical flying ointment formulas from the 1500s and 1600s. Beta-asarone, found in the rhizome, is a precursor compound related to mescaline. In sufficient quantities it produces mild stimulant and psychedelic effects, making it a plausible ingredient in preparations intended to induce altered states and visions.
