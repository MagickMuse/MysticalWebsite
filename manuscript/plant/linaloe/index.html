A tall, bushy tropical shrub or tree of the Burseraceae family, with smooth bark and fleshy fruit. The wood is only used for distillation purposes when the tree is twenty or thirty years old. The oil is partially a pathological product, since its production is stimulated by lacerating the trunk; according to tradition, the tree must be wounded on the night of the full moon for it to produce any oil.

Origin & Distribution

Native to Central and South America, especially Mexico. Cultivated in the Far East, particularly in India (Mysore). The wood oil is mainly produced in Mexico; the seed and husk oil is produced in India.

Folk Tradition

The seed oil is known in India as Indian lavender oil and is used chiefly as a local perfume ingredient and in soaps by the cosmetics industry of Mysore state. It is not much found outside India. In Mexico the wood oil is used in a similar fashion to rosewood, which contains similar constituents.

Other Species

Several species of Bursera are all known simply as linaloe. West Indian elemi (B. simaruba) is a close relative, as are Myrrh and Frankincense.

Related Species

The genus Bursera sits alongside the genera that produce Frankincense, Myrrh, and Elemi within the Burseraceae family. The Spanish trade name copal limon overlaps with broader "copal" terminology; see also Copal.
