Coltsfoot (Tussilago farfara) is a low-growing perennial herb with woolly texture and a single yellow spring blossom.

History

Coltsfoot was long cited in folk tradition as a demulcent -- a soothing agent for mucous membranes -- and was a common treatment for coughs before research raised concerns about its safety for internal use. Its medicinal reputation predates those findings considerably, and it remains a subject of interest in botanical study.

Characteristics

Coltsfoot grows close to the ground, preferring sandy, moist conditions. Native to Europe, it sends up a flower stem in early spring that ends in a single yellow bloom before the leaves fully emerge. It has since naturalized throughout southern Canada and the northeastern and north-central United States.

Magickal Notes

Gathering times: Flowers were gathered in February; leaves in June and early July.

British Herb Tobacco: Coltsfoot was used in a mixture called British Herb Tobacco for relief of asthma and bronchitis. The mixture was predominantly coltsfoot plus buckbean, eyebright, betony, rosemary, thyme, lavender, and chamomile.

Cough tea: A tea made from the leaves was prepared for stubborn coughs.

Ritual Uses

Used in love charms and visionary incenses in folk practice.

Magickal Uses

  • Love: Add to love sachets and blends to attract affection and tenderness.
  • Visions: Burn a pinch as incense to invite visionary dreams and second sight (avoid inhalation; use as space fumigation only).
  • Messages and offerings: The hoof-shaped leaves of coltsfoot give it a special connection to Rhiannon, the Welsh goddess of the moon and prophetic dreams, who is often depicted with or as a horse and is associated with the faerie folk. A charm of dried coltsfoot leaves can be used to hasten the arrival of a message, or placed as an offering to Rhiannon.

Additional Notes

Also known as Coughwort and Sun‑Before‑the‑Father; observe safety cautions—internal use not recommended, especially if pregnant or nursing.
