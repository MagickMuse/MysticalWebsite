History

Native to the southern Atlantic coastline, pine has long been celebrated for its beauty, strength, and distinctive fragrance, as well as its vital role in the lumber industry. By the late eighteenth century it was among the most sought-after timber species in the world and stood as a major export of colonial New England.

Characteristics

Pine is a conifer whose aromatic needles grow in characteristic spirals along its branches. Throughout the southeastern United States, it flourishes in dry, sandy soil. Reproduction is carried out through the plant's seed-bearing cones, which have long been used in magick as natural talismans.

Species and Native Range

More than one hundred species of pine grow around the world, all but one native to the northern hemisphere. They scrabble on hillsides in Italy and bow gracefully in the snow of Finnish forests. California alone hosts twenty-nine native species, from low coastal trees to towering sugar pines that drop cones over a foot long. In every culture where pine grows, it is revered for its practical gifts. The wood is disease-resistant and excellent for boards. The cones serve as fuel. The nuts are food. Bark, needles, resin, and fruits all yield medicine.

Medicinal Use

Pine treats tooth pain, coughs, colds, and even thyroid disease. Pine and pine resin carry antiseptic qualities and are used for fevers, tuberculosis, and urinary tract infections, and applied externally to wounds, burns, and lacerations.

Magickal Meaning

Pine represents longevity, immortality, and unflappability. Use it when you wish to overcome obstacles and stay the course.

Sacred to Dionysus

The pine was dedicated to Bacchus, the Romans' name for Dionysus, and its branches decorated the Dionysian festivals. Pine resin and pine-infused wine survive into the present, and Greek wine is still often flavored with pine cones.

Pitys, the Dryad of the Pine

Pitys is the dryad who lived in a pine tree. As a beautiful young woman she was pursued relentlessly by more than one god, including Boreas, god of the cold north wind. Some versions say she was changed by Pan, because he loved her or because he was trying to hide her, or both. Others say Boreas chased her off a cliff and Gaia took pity on her lifeless body and transformed it into a pine tree.

The Lovers' Embrace

An old Roman legend tells of two lovers buried side by side. One was changed into a pine tree, the other into a vine, that they might always embrace. The pattern echoes the rose and the briar, where two graves yield a single rose and a thorny briar, and the rose grows around the briar.

Immortality and Funerary Use

Pine and pine cones are symbols of immortality. When seen on a headstone or carved into a decorative funerary urn, they declare that the dead will be forever remembered and were incorruptible in life. Pine is an enduring wood, and its resin lasts as well, so the tree carries the meaning of resiliency and trustworthiness. Boughs of pine and fir were laid over coffins, and pine is one of the primary woods traditionally used to make coffins.

Pinocchio, the Boy Carved from Pine

Pinocchio's name carries the tree within it. Pino translates as pine tree in Italian, and pinnochia or pinonchio is the Tuscan word for pine nut. Although the log in the original Italian tale is described simply as a common block of firewood, the boy's name leaves little doubt that he is made of pine. Maestro Cherry, named for the red tip of his nose, is about to carve the wood into a table leg when it cries out, "Ouch!" and "Don't hit me so hard!" Startled, the woodcarver passes the enchanted log to his friend Geppetto, who shapes it into a marionette. The carved boy is more troublemaker than charmer in this version. He kills the Talking Cricket with a hammer. He falls asleep with his feet on a foot warmer and wakes to find them burned off. He dines with robber-assassins at the Inn of the Red Lobster, and they hang him from a tree where he dangles for three hours with his eyes open and his legs kicking. The Blue Fairy first appears not as a fairy but as a ghost, the Lovely Maiden with the Azure Hair, who tells Pinocchio she is dead and waiting for her coffin. Seeing him swinging from the branch, she sends a falcon to cut him down, which reveals her true nature as a fairy. Three doctors attend him, the Talking Cricket among them, with a crow and an owl as the others. Nursed on sugar water, he lies to her about having gold, and his nose grows. The Blue Fairy dies, then does not. He runs off, joins a circus, is turned into a donkey, becomes the star of the show, and when he falters is thrown into the sea, where the water shifts him back into a marionette. A shark, not a whale, swallows him, and inside he finds Geppetto, who was swallowed up trying to rescue him in a little boat. They swim to shore together. Pinocchio vows to be a kinder, better person, and is rewarded by being made into a real boy. Geppetto is restored as a younger, more talented, wealthier man, and they live happily ever after.

Balder's Midsummer

Pine branches are lit at Nordic Midsummer festivals to light the way for Balder descent into darkness.

Herbal Lore

This laid-back member of the pine family may be found growing out of craggy hilltops, mountain ranges, or cliff faces. This plant knows where other plants fear to tread, and still somehow always winds up there. The oldest living Scots pine is over 210 years old and almost 150 feet (48 meters) tall. This gorgeous specimen lives in Estonia and people will be visiting it for decades to come, as these rough-and-ready dwellers can live up to 300 years old.

⚠️
Pine Oil Expiration Warning

When you purchase pine essential oil, place a date on the bottle 2 years into the future from date of purchase. Pine oil expires after two years due to oxygenation, and though it will not smell any differently, it can cause contact dermatitis or even sun sensitivity and burns. It is safest to throw away the oil and purchase new. Expired oil could theoretically be used in a diffuser for aromatic purposes, or added to homemade candles, but for the very low cost of this oil, it is not worth getting blisters.

⚠️
Warning: Avoid cheap pine oils, as they are commonly cut with turpentine.

Magickal Uses

  • Pinecone display: Collect baskets of fallen pinecones, looking for strong, healthy ones filled with seeds. Place them in bowls and baskets in plain sight throughout the home to radiate their fertility power and transfer it to those who dwell there.
  • Fertility and vigor: Carry cones to increase fertility and ensure vigorous old age; a Midsummer‑gathered cone (with seeds) is a famed charm.
  • Cleansing and reversal: Burn needles in winter to purify the home; burn to exorcise and to reverse and send back spells; scatter on floors to drive away evil; add to cleansing baths.
  • Sickbed wards: Place branches above the bed to keep sickness away or aid recovery.
  • Living protection: Pine trees protect by their presence, and the fragrance of the needles carries that protection across an area. Small living pines kept indoors year-round, not only at Christmas, can be decorated with protective amulets (see Living Pine Protection Trees).
  • Joy and hearth wards: Hang a pine branch over the door (custom noted in Japan) for continual joy; make a cross of needles before the fireplace to block evil.
  • Money work: Use in money spells; pine sawdust is a classic incense base for prosperity blends.
  • Love and longing: The verb "to pine" — to long painfully for someone or something — reflects the tree's deep association with aching desire. Pine is a powerful ingredient in love spells, especially those calling someone back or drawing a distant beloved closer. Pine cones make beautiful and aromatic herbal talismans for this purpose.

Additional Notes

Amber Connection

Amber is fossilized pine sap, linking pine to this ancient and precious material. This connection extends pine's associations to preservation, ancient wisdom, and solar energy.

Gunshot‑immunity lore via daily pine nuts from a single cone is folkloric and not literal.

Amulet Uses

A pine cone carried as an amulet promotes health and longevity.

Tree of the Immortals

As an evergreen the pine is a natural emblem of immortality and longevity, and because it stands untouched by storm and foul weather it signifies incorruptibility and fortitude besides. In Japan the followers of Shinto set two pine trees on either side of the home's gateway at the New Year, for in that animistic faith every living thing holds a spirit, and the pine is said to be home to benevolent spirits, the kami. The Immortals of the Taoist religions ate the cones, needles, and resin of the pine, which made them light enough to fly, and the trees are said to stand at the gates of the City of the Immortals. The familiar egg and dart frieze, built of stylized lotus flowers and pine cones, carries a hidden sexual meaning, the flowers standing for the vagina and the cones for the phallus. The pine appears in the Ogham tree alphabet under the name Ailim, and though it looks unlike the spruce and fir of the Christmas tree, all belong to the one family, the Pinaceae, which takes its name from the pine.
