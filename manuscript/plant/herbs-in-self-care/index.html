Plant Grimoire

Herbs in Self-Care

Witches have long served as healers. Long before pharmaceuticals existed, communities turned to cunning folk, alewives, and wise women for remedies rooted in the natural world. Herbs were the foundation of those preparations, and the oldest known written record of medicinal herb use is an ancient Sumerian clay tablet engraved with herbal cures. The plants that brought balance to body, mind, and spirit in those early traditions are still in use today.

This guide explores the major systems of herbal health and how those traditions connect to modern witchcraft practice.

Herbal Systems of Health

Across cultures and centuries, herbs have been woven into formalized systems of healing. Each system carries its own philosophy about what health means and how balance is achieved. Understanding these frameworks helps contextualize the plants you work with and the traditions they come from.

Allopathy

Allopathy is the dominant healthcare model in Western society. It works by targeting and reducing specific symptoms rather than treating the whole person. Most allopathic treatments rely on pharmaceutical drugs, and a significant portion of those trace their active ingredients back to plant sources. Roughly 25 percent of all prescription medicines contain compounds originally derived from plants.

These plant-derived compounds are called phytochemicals. When isolated and purified for clinical use, they become part of allopathic medicine. When the whole plant is used instead, this is referred to as phytomedicine.

Many plant-based preparations are sold in the United States as dietary supplements. It is worth noting that "natural" does not mean "safe." Herbal preparations carry real potency, and allergic reactions can develop at any stage of life. Anyone adding herbal supplements to their routine should research potential interactions, contraindications, and side effects carefully, particularly if also taking prescription medications.

Naturopathy

Naturopathy takes a fundamentally different approach. Rather than targeting isolated symptoms, naturopathic practitioners work to support the body's own capacity for healing. Treatment is individualized, with remedies and regimens tailored to the specific needs of each person.

Beyond herbs, a naturopath may incorporate dietary changes, exercise, massage, and detoxification practices into a healing plan. In the United States, naturopathic physicians complete a four-year graduate program and must pass licensing examinations before practicing.

This whole-person philosophy aligns closely with the witchcraft tradition of working with plants to support overall balance rather than simply suppressing symptoms.

Homeopathy

Homeopathy operates on the principle that "like cures like": that a substance capable of producing symptoms in a healthy person can, in highly diluted form, stimulate the body to heal those same symptoms. Like naturopathy, homeopathy aims to activate the body's innate healing capacity rather than suppress symptoms directly. Unlike naturopathy, however, homeopathy remains centered on symptoms rather than the whole person.

Remedies are prepared in extremely low concentrations, which makes the practice controversial. Two people presenting the same symptoms may receive entirely different remedies, making efficacy difficult to measure through conventional clinical trials. Some view this as a limitation; others see it as reflecting the same individualized logic that underlies magickal practice, the idea that intention, resonance, and subtle energetics can produce real results beyond what standard measurement captures.

In the United States, homeopathic physicians generally complete a four-year degree, though licensing requirements vary by state. Homeopathic products are regulated by the FDA, but no uniform prescribing standards exist.

Asclepius & Hygeia: Deities of Medicine

Asclepius, born of Apollo and the mortal Coronis, was both hero and god of medicine in Greek mythology. His descendants, the Asclepiades, included Hygeia, goddess of health and a companion of Aphrodite. Both deities are associated with the snake, a creature long regarded as magickal and symbolic of renewal through the shedding of its skin. Together they represent the dual nature of healing: maintaining ongoing health and seeking remedy when balance is lost.

Indigenous and Tribal Medicine

Indigenous healing traditions weave together herbs, minerals, animal medicine, and magickal practice into a unified approach to health. These systems are transmitted orally across generations, and each tradition is distinct, shaped by the specific land, culture, and spiritual worldview of the people who practice it.

Because these traditions are inseparable from their cultural and ceremonial context, they belong to the communities that hold them. They are not interchangeable or freely transferable to outside practitioners. Awareness of and respect for that boundary matters, particularly as certain practices have moved into mainstream wellness spaces.

⚠️
Practices such as smudging with sage and sweetgrass, or sweat lodge ceremonies, originate within specific Indigenous traditions and carry deep cultural and spiritual significance. These practices are sacred to their communities and should only be engaged in by those within those traditions, or with explicit guidance and permission from knowledge holders.

Aromatherapy

Aromatherapy uses medicinal-grade plant extracts, typically in the form of essential oils, diluted into carrier oils for topical application or, in some practices, internal use. It also works through direct inhalation, engaging the olfactory system to produce therapeutic and emotional effects. Aromatherapy bridges the practical and the magickal naturally, as scent is one of the most immediate ways plants communicate their properties.

Flower Essences

Developed by Dr. Edward Bach in the 1930s, flower essences contain trace amounts of plant material steeped in water and preserved with a small amount of alcohol. The philosophy underlying flower essences is that emotional wellbeing is the foundation of physical health. By stabilizing the emotional state, the body is given the conditions it needs to heal.

In the Bach system, specific flowers address specific emotional states: Gentian supports those feeling discouraged or low, Vervain helps those who are tense or overextended, Oak supports those who push themselves past their limits, and Pine addresses persistent guilt. Once emotional equilibrium is restored, healing follows naturally.

For the practicing witch, flower essences are particularly interesting as magickal tools. They function as gentle banishing preparations, clearing emotional blockages, and can be used both internally and topically within a ritual context.

What Is an Herbalist?

An herbalist is someone trained in the therapeutic use of plants for health and wellness. According to the American Herbalists Guild, formal training involves 1,600 hours of study at an herbal medicine school plus 400 clinical hours.

Herbalists generally take a whole-plant approach, working with herbs in their complete form rather than isolating individual compounds. This philosophy holds that the natural balance within a plant contributes to its healing effect. The synergy of a plant's full spectrum of constituents works differently than any single extracted ingredient, a view that aligns closely with how witches understand the inherent intelligence of the natural world.
