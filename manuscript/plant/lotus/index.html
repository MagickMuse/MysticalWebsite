Lotus (Nelumbo nucifera; Nymphaea lotus) confers protection, opens locks, and blesses with divine favor.

Magickal Properties

Gender: Feminine (source: Cold)

Deities: Isis, Khonsu, Hekate, Neith, Selene (from source)

Powers: Fertility, Visions, Magick, Protection, Lock‑Opening

From the Source Book

Revered by the Egyptians; represents the goddess Isis and human fertility. Flowers are used in perfumery. Root is poisonous.

Ritual Uses

Revered across the East as a mystical symbol of life, spirituality, and the center of the universe; sacred in ancient Egypt and offered to the gods.

Magickal Uses

  • Breath of protection: Those who breathe the scent receive its protection.
  • Open locks: Place the root under the tongue and speak “SIGN, ARGGIS” toward a locked door to open it (traditional lore).
  • Break love spells: Seeds and pods are antidotes to love spells.
  • General blessing: Any part carried or worn ensures divine blessings and good luck.

In Hindu Imagery

Sarasvati, goddess of knowledge, creativity, art, music, and wisdom, associated with rivers and small bodies of water, is often depicted seated on a white lotus blossom. Lakshmi, goddess of power, beauty, prosperity, and fortune, stands or sits on a pink lotus. Ganesha, the elephant-headed god who removes obstacles and brings fortune, is also seen on a pink lotus.

The Lotus of Enlightenment

In Buddhist teaching, sacred to the Buddha, the lotus represents enlightenment. The plant grows in mud yet purifies the water around it while bringing forth great beauty and nourishment.

A Sacred Trinity and the Eight Auspicious Signs

Along with the lily and the rose, the lotus is among the most important flower symbols on Earth. In both Chinese and Tibetan Buddhist iconography it is one of the eight auspicious symbols, and it carries that weight in nearly every culture that has met it.

Rising From the Mud

The lotus lifts its perfect head above muddy swamps, untouched by the dirty water beneath it. Egyptian, Indian, and Chinese sages read three lessons in this. First, the flower rises in complete perfection from the murky primal waters of creation. Second, it climbs from darkness into the light, woken by the Sun. Third, it stands for the triumph of spirit over matter and becomes a metaphor for the journey to enlightenment. Because the lotus sinks back beneath the water through the night and rises again at dawn, the Egyptians made it a symbol of death and rebirth.

The Bud, the Vulva, and the Goddess

The tight closed bud of the lotus is a symbol for the whole Universe. The open flower is an archetypal image of the vulva, and through it the lotus belongs to the Goddess.

The Flower of the Elements

In every culture the lotus holds a reminder of the elements: its seed rests in the earth, it grows in water, its blossom opens in the air that carries its scent, and it is woken by the Sun, the fire it also resembles with its bright central disc ringed by rays of petals. Its eight petals stand for the four cardinal and four intercardinal directions, and for the Ashtadikpalas, the rulers of the eight directions of the Universe.

Mandalas and the World Axis

In mandalas the lotus is often half-hidden, its petals forming a border at once symbolic and decorative, with the Buddha seated at the center of the eight-petaled bloom, detached from the material world and its cycle of death and rebirth. In Hindu imagery the lotus is the base of the earth from which the holy mountains such as Kailash and Meru rise, and its stalk is the world axis climbing up through that sacred mountain.

The Lotus and the Chakras

The lotus is the symbol of each of the chakras, the energy centers set along the spine. The number of petals given to each lotus reflects the role and function of its chakra, and the flowers are said to unfurl, petal by petal, through exercise and meditation.

Colors and Kinds

The colors of the lotus carry their own meanings. The sacred lotus, the Indian or Oriental lotus, is pink or white, a great bloom whose petals can span half a meter; every part of it is edible, and its sugared seeds are a festival treat. The red lotus is the ultimate symbol of the Sun and the emblem of India. The lotus shown in stylized Egyptian friezes is the blue lotus, which is in truth a form of Water Lily rather than a true lotus; beyond its ethereal color it holds a psychotropic substance called apomorphine, and it is this blue flower whose seeds the lotus eaters of Greek myth, immortalized in Tennyson's Song of the Lotus Eaters, ate to live in a state of hedonistic bliss.

Edible Lotus

The rhizomes of the lotus are eaten fresh, cooked, fried, dried, or pickled. The flowers, seeds, and embryos can also be eaten. In China, Korea, and Vietnam a tea is made from the roots, flowers, and seeds. In Korea it is called yeonhwa-cha.

Meaning When Gifted

When lotus is gifted it carries the meaning of estranged love and of being forgetful of the past.
