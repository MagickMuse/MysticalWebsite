Bamboo (Bambusa vulgaris) is a fast-growing grass with hollow, segmented stems used in construction, crafts, and magickal practices.

Folk Tradition

In Japan, bamboo is one of three lucky plants, along with the pine and the plum. Its roots are remarkably strong and resilient, and a saying holds that a bamboo grove is the safest place to be during an earthquake, since the roots can withstand the moving ground. For this reason bamboo is associated with strength and stability.

For Buddhists and Taoists, painting bamboo stems is a spiritual, meditative practice. The spaces between the knots along the straight stem represent the concept of "voidness of heart."

⚠️
The Yek'uana people of Brazil hold bamboo as sacred, sharing a name with it (zara, meaning something close to "clarinet") and crafting instruments from it for the sacred music of their wa-wana ceremony, which invokes the God Uanaji. This is a closed cultural and religious tradition belonging to the Yek'uana people.

Magickal Properties

Powers: Protection, Luck, Hex-Breaking, Wishes

Ritual Uses

Bamboo is used in divination in Chinese temples. Bits of the wood are thrown to the worshipper by the priest. According to the way they fall, the omen is interpreted as good or bad.

Magickal Uses

For wishes: Carve your wish on a piece of bamboo and bury it in the ground in a secluded place. Or, carve a symbol of protection, like a five-pointed star (pentagram), on a length of bamboo and plant it in the ground to protect your home.

For protection and luck: Grown near the house, bamboo gives it and its residents good fortune. Bamboo is also placed over the door because, since its wood never changes color, it is considered lucky.

For hex-breaking: Bamboo is used to break hexes, either by carrying it in a sachet, growing a plant near the house, or crushing the wood to a powder (called "bamba wood") and burning it.

For spirit work: The Chinese use bamboo as a charm against evil spirits. To call up good spirits, make a flute out of bamboo. Carve the name of the spirit (if any) and play an improvised melody.

Tabasheer

Tabasheer is not truly a stone but a hardened secretion drawn from a kind of bamboo, whose native name in Malaysia and Indonesia means rough bamboo. The fluid thickens until it leaves behind a white solid that resembles a fragment of sea shell.

As a warrior's talisman, tabasheer was bound so tightly against the skin that the pieces sometimes became embedded in the body, a practice believed to make a soldier proof against wounds from steel. One account tells of Genghis Khan meeting eight such warriors in Japan, all fitted with tabasheer and seemingly impossible to kill, so he ordered his men to set aside their steel blades and beat the eight to death with wooden clubs instead.

In traditional Chinese herbal medicine, tabasheer is ground to a fine powder and used to ease fevers, liver infections, and stomach complaints.
