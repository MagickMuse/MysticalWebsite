Green Tea (Camellia sinensis) is the true tea plant of the camellia family, the source of the cup that cheers, long prized as a stimulant, a status symbol, and a focus for ritual stillness.

Characteristics

A member of the camellia family, the tea plant yields the leaves from which real tea is brewed, as distinct from the many herbal decoctions that merely borrow the name. The leaf is roughly the shape of an eyelid, and it carries caffeine, which rouses the drinker and holds off sleep.

Folk Tradition

One legend tells of the holy man Bodhidharma, who travelled to spread the teachings of the Buddha and swore never to sleep until his work was done. After years of ceaseless teaching he at last nodded off, and waking in fury at his own weakness he cut away his eyelids and cast them to the ground. Where they fell, the Buddha caused the first tea plant to spring up, its leaves eyelid-shaped and its draught a banisher of sleep, so that the tale explains at once the plant's form and its virtue.

Tea reached Europe in the early seventeenth century and swiftly became so prized that a vast trade grew up around it. Its costliness made it the preserve of the wealthy, and so a cup of tea became a mark of status.

Magickal and Ceremonial Uses

  • Courage and strength: Tea is carried and drunk to lend courage and to fortify the will.
  • Prosperity: As a one-time luxury of the rich, tea is used in workings to draw wealth and prosperity.
  • Stillness and focus: The Japanese tea ceremony is a Zen rite carried out by strict form, seeking to dissolve the ego in the simple act of preparing and sharing tea, a model for mindful, ego-quieting ritual. The English custom of afternoon tea, made fashionable by the Duchess of Bedford in the mid nineteenth century, carries no such sacred weight, yet marks tea's place at the heart of daily life.
