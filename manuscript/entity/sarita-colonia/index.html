Sarita Colonia is the patron saint of the marginalized, immigrants, professional drivers, bus drivers, and the LGBTQ+ community.

Classification

Unofficial saint.

Life and History

Sarita Colonia (1914-1940) was the eldest child in a poor Peruvian family. When her mother died, young Sarita inherited all domestic responsibilities. After her father remarried, fell ill, and couldn't work, she took on his responsibilities too.

Around 1930, she moved to Lima seeking work to support her family. She served as a live-in nanny for nearly three years, then moved to the port city of Callao to work at her aunt's fish stand. She held two or three additional jobs simultaneously, sending all her earnings home. Her generosity at the market became legendary: whatever Sarita had, she shared.

When her stepmother died, her father sent most of her siblings to live with her. In December 1940, Sarita fell severely ill and died the next day. Her death certificate lists malaria, though relatives blame an overdose of castor oil. Various more dramatic legends have since emerged.

Emergence as Saint

Since her death, Sarita has emerged as a powerful intercessor with almost unlimited abilities. Stevedores and port workers were her first devotees. She developed a reputation as protector of the marginalized, those who feel excluded by conventional saints.

In the 1940s and 1950s, she attracted devotion from society's outcasts: gay and transgender people, sex workers, thieves. Though sometimes called the patron of criminals (police stake out her shrines for wanted suspects), her devotion actually spans all social groups.

Who She Protects

Sarita is patron of:

  • The abandoned, marginalized, and poverty-stricken
  • Those who face death daily (dangerous neighborhoods or professions)
  • Peru's LGBTQ+ community
  • Prisoners, both literal and those imprisoned by circumstances
  • Illegal immigrants (from anywhere, not just Peru)
  • Professional drivers (truckers, taxi and bus drivers)
  • Market vendors and nannies

Immigration Aid

Her image worn against the skin allegedly confers invisibility or prevents notice. She protects passage across borders, then helps with finding jobs, finances, papers, and reuniting families separated by borders.

Magical Flowers

Flowers that have touched her tomb absorb her power. Water from her flower vases becomes holy water. Flowers from her shrines are used for healing and spiritual cleansing. If taking flowers from a public shrine, bring replacements for others.

Iconography

Based on a photograph: a young woman with long dark hair gazing directly at the viewer.

Sacred Site

Originally buried in a common grave, her bones were retrieved during construction and reburied. In 1983, devotees funded a mausoleum shrine in Callao. A prison in Callao has been nicknamed after her.

Festivals

Two annual fiestas: March (birth) and December (death). Devotees bring gifts (food, clothing, images) to distribute to crowds.

Feast Days

  • 1 March (birth)
  • 12 December (death)
