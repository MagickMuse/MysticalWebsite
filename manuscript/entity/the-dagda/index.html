Father of the Gods

The Dagda stands at the head of the Irish pantheon as Father of all the Gods, a central figure in the mythological cycle of the Tuatha de Danaan, the People of the Goddess Dana. He was not, by any account, among the more beautiful of deities: he appears as an ungainly figure, ugly and pot-bellied, dressed in the rough clothes and heavy sandals of a peasant rather than the finery one might expect of a god.

The Great Club

The Dagda carries a club of extraordinary size, so colossal that it must be dragged along the ground behind him, carving furrows and dykes into the earth as it goes.

The Cauldron of Plenty

His other great implement is a cauldron that never empties, one of the treasures of the Tuatha de Danaan. It appears repeatedly across the mythological tradition as a symbol of abundance and eternal life, and it stands as an ancestor of the many sacred cauldrons and grails that recur throughout later Celtic and Arthurian legend.

The Challenge of Samhain

Like many gods, the Dagda was required to undergo a trial. His was a curious and very earthy one. On November 1, the feast of Samhain and the old Celtic New Year, he was challenged to eat an enormous quantity of porridge that had been prepared in a vast crater dug into the earth. He ate it all.

The Harper

For all his ungainly appearance, the Dagda was a harpist of extraordinary skill. The tunes he played were said to govern the turning of the seasons, calling each one in its proper time.
