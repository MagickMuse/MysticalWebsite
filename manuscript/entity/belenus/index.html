Magickal Properties

  • Brilliant solar-healing god linked with therapeutic springs, fertility of people and livestock, and erotic vitality
  • Patron of prosperity, protection of cities, and the shining life-force of May

Ritual Uses

  • Petition at springs and on Beltane/May Eve for healing, fecundity, and good fortune
  • Bless herds and fields; request strength and renewal from the sun’s brilliance

Offerings

  • First fruits, votive horses, carved infants, clean water from springs

Favored People

Farmers, herders, the ailing, and keepers of sacred springs

Petition For

Healing (sun and water), fertile flocks and fields, protection, and joyful erotic power

Warnings

Keep rites dignified and reciprocal; acknowledge local place-spirits at spring shrines

Additional Notes

  • Latinized name of Bel/Belen; widely venerated across the Celtic world; Romans identified him with Apollo
  • Sacred day: Beltane (May Eve/May 1)

Historical Context

Belenus is considered to be one of the oldest of the Celtic gods thus far recognized, venerated from prehistoric times until Christianization around 400 CE and in some regions much later. He bears many similarities with the Greek deity Apollo as a god of light, sun, and healing, and though he most often appears as a purely Celtic god, he was sometimes worshipped as Apollo Belenus. In Ireland he was known as Bile.

Sacred Sites

His principal sanctuaries lay in northern Italy, especially at Aquileia, and in southwestern Gaul in the region of Aquitaine. Additional shrines are recorded in Austria and northern Italy, and a thermal spring sanctuary was dedicated to Apollo Belenus at St Sabine in the Cote d'Or, where model horses were left as offerings.

Iconography

Horse statuettes, stone carvings, and reliefs. In his guise as Apollo Belenus he became particularly associated with horses, which are well-attested as sun symbols in the Celtic Bronze Age.

Beltine

He is celebrated long into the Christian era in the festival of Beltine, also called Cetshamain, set on May 1 as the start of the "warm season." The rites involved lighting huge bonfires and driving cattle between them as a protection against disease. Beltine marked the season when cattle were liberated after winter to graze the open pastures.
