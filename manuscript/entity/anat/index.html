Source-Text Additions

Origin and Region

Canaanite and Phoenician origin, with cult centers spanning northern Israel, Lebanon, and Syria.

Period of Worship

From prehistoric times (circa 2500 BCE) until 200 CE or later.

Synonyms

Anath, Lady of the Mountain, and Antit (her Egyptian name).

Centers of Cult

Ugarit (Ras Samra) and the grain-growing coastal regions of the eastern Mediterranean. A sanctuary was dedicated to her at Tanis in Lower Egypt.

Art and Literary References

Named in Egyptian hieroglyphic on a stele from Bethsan and described on various other votive inscriptions and clay plaques. Literary sources include the Ugaritic texts from Ras Samra and various offering lists.

Iconography

As sister of Baal, she is primarily a fertility goddess. In art she is usually depicted naked with breasts and vaginal area prominent. She often wears a coiffure similar to that of the Egyptian goddess Hathor, with whom she has at times been closely linked. She is described variously as mother of the gods and mistress of the sky.

War Goddess and Egyptian Adoption

In addition to her fertility role, she is a youthful and aggressive goddess of war. Egypt adopted her in this capacity from the end of the Middle Kingdom (early eighteenth century BCE), and she was particularly prominent in Lower Egypt during the Hyksos Dynasty. Identified as a daughter of the sun god Re, she carries warlike attributes of lance, battleaxe, and shield. She impressed Rameses II, whose daughter was named Bin-Anat (daughter of Anat). Rameses III adopted her as his shield in battle. The Ras Samra stele names her Antit, queen of heaven and mistress of all the gods.

The Virgin Anat

Known as the virgin Anat, she indulged in orgies of violence, wading up to her thighs in blood and gore. She may belong to a triad of goddesses alongside Athirat and Asera.

The Defeat of Mot

In the classic Canaanite confrontation legend, after the primordial battle between good and evil in the guise of Baal and Mot, Anat searched out the body of Baal, buried it, and caught up with his slayer Mot to make appropriate retribution. She cleaved and winnowed him, burned and ground him, a curious variation of a theme associated elsewhere with gods of vegetation such as Osiris.

The Legend of Aqhat

She also features in the Legend of Aqhat, in which she sends an eagle to slay the youth when he refuses to give her his magical bow.
