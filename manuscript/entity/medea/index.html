Magical Properties

High Priestess of Hekate, herbalist supreme, potions-mistress and spell-caster. Medea is among those serving as prototypes of the witch. Her name is related to metis (wisdom) and translates as "the cunning one."

Medea may channel and embody Hekate herself. She is Hekate's priestess and acolyte. Her mother is variously described as an ocean spirit or as Hekate herself—whether literally or spiritually is subject to interpretation.

Medea's identity remains ambiguous. She may be a human being, or she may be a pre-Olympian deity whose murders reflect past human sacrifices. Circe, her aunt, is plainly a spirit whom Homer calls "goddess." From surviving Greek and Roman plays, Medea was usually portrayed as a foreigner witch.

Ritual Uses

Call upon Medea for protection when facing impossible tasks or dangerous opponents. Petition her assistance for powerful spell work involving salves, potions, and enchantments. She specializes in protective magic that shields against fire and iron, sleep spells that work even on sleepless guardians, and transformation magic involving garments and materials.

Medea's magic operates through incantations, herbal concoctions, and enchanted objects. She creates protective salves, bewitches guardians, and crafts magically charged items. Her power is particularly strong in situations requiring cunning strategy combined with magical force.

The Golden Fleece Quest

Medea is the hero who accomplishes the task Jason receives credit for—obtaining the Golden Fleece. When Jason was sent to retrieve the Golden Fleece from Colchis, King Aeëtes set an impossible task: harness fire-breathing bulls, plough a field, and sow it with dragon's teeth between sunrise and sunset. Failure meant death for Jason and the Argonauts.

Hera asked Aphrodite to tell Eros to shoot Medea with an arrow of love. Without being asked, Medea concocts a protective salve rendering Jason safe from fire or iron for 24 hours. She meets him at the Temple of Hekate, confesses her love, and gives him the salve. Jason swears by all the gods to make her his queen and love her forever.

When Aeëtes orders his men to kill the foreigners despite Jason completing the task, Medea warns Jason and leads him at night to the grove where the Golden Fleece hangs, guarded by a sleepless dragon. She bewitches the dragon through incantations so it falls asleep—notably she does not kill it, as dragons are Hekate's sacred creatures. They seize the Fleece and escape.

When the king's faster ship overtakes them, Medea arranges an ambush for her brother on a nearby island, having tricked him into meeting her. Jason kills her brother, forcing her father to stop pursuit for funeral rites.

Later Acts

Upon arriving at Iolchus years later, Jason is warned that King Pelias intends to kill him. Medea saves him again. Disguised as a humble old crone witch peddling magical herbs for rejuvenation, she tricks Pelias' daughters into boiling their father to death.

The throne belongs to Jason, but the people refuse to accept Medea as queen, perceiving her as a foreigner witch. Another king is chosen, and Jason and Medea flee to Corinth.

Jason eventually asks Medea to leave so he can marry the Princess of Corinth and inherit her father's kingdom. Medea sends a magical robe to his bride-to-be—irresistibly beautiful but bursting into flames when worn, incinerating the bride and her entire palace. Medea escapes in a dragon chariot sent by Hekate (or by her grandfather Helios).

Multiple Endings

Different versions exist of what happens to Medea:

The Blessed Isle: In her youth, Medea rejected Zeus' advances, earning Hera's eternal devotion. Although she dies (commits suicide), she is sent to the Isles of the Blessed, the Greek paradise, where she marries Achilles.

Transformation to Angitia: Now or always a goddess, she travels to Italy where she assumes the name Angitia.

Athens: She marries King Aegeus of Athens and tries but fails to poison Theseus.

Asia: She goes to Asia where the Medes are named in her honor.

Offerings

Offerings appropriate to Hekate's priestess: honey cakes, eggs, garlic, herbs used in potion-making, keys symbolizing her power to unlock magical knowledge.

Favored People

Herbalists and potion-makers, those facing impossible tasks, women betrayed by lovers, foreigners facing prejudice, practitioners of protective magic, those who must use cunning to survive.

Petition For

Protection against impossible odds, assistance with herbal magic and potion-making, sleep spells and enchantments, magical transformation of objects, cunning strategy combined with magical force, justice against betrayal.

Warnings

Do not invoke Medea for coercion or violent revenge because her myth warns of destructive consequences. Her magic is powerful but carries terrible costs. Seek ethical counsel and avoid harming innocents in any petition.

Aconite and the Draught from the Foam

Ovid's Metamorphoses places Medea behind the birth of aconite. To dispatch a dangerous heir she prepared a poisonous draught from a drug long held in reserve, drawn from the foam Cerberus shed when Alcides forced him up from the nether skies. The plant born of that fury is called Aconite because it can unlock all bars and force its passage through rock. The episode names Medea as the conduit through which the wolfsbane entered the world, and through which Hekate's poison knowledge passed into the hands of mortals. See Aconite.

Additional Notes

Medea was the daughter of Aeëtes, King of Colchis (now part of modern Georgia), son of Helios the Sun and brother of Circe. She was central figure in at least ten Greek and Roman plays, of which only two survive in more than fragmentary form.

Jason was a student of the centaur Chiron and under protection of Hera. The Argonauts included the shaman Orpheus, the sons of the North Wind, and even Heracles for a time.

The people of Iolchus welcomed Jason home as a hero but didn't trust Medea, refusing to accept her as queen. This rejection of the foreign witch who had saved Jason repeatedly reflects ancient prejudices that continued through history.
