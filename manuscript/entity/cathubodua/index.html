Overview

Cathubodua is a war goddess of the Continental Celtic religion, known chiefly from a Gaulish inscription found in the Alpine region of Haute-Savoie in what is now eastern France. Her name means battle crow or battle raven, from cathu meaning battle and bodua or bodva meaning crow or raven, and she is understood as the Continental Celtic counterpart of the Irish Celtic war goddess Badb Catha, whose name carries the same meaning in the Irish register.

Classification

War goddess of the Continental Celtic religion, known from epigraphic evidence. Counterpart of the Irish Badb Catha.

Domains

Battle and the field of conflict, the raven that circles the battlefield and reads the outcome of the fighting, the fierce feminine presence over the movement of warriors, the continuity between the Continental and Insular Celtic understandings of the war goddess.

Iconography

Her surviving iconography is limited, but the shared iconographic vocabulary of the Celtic war goddesses suggests a figure of severe aspect associated with the raven or crow, sometimes shown holding weapons and standing over the fallen, sometimes shown in the form of the raven itself surveying the field. The Alpine and Gaulish evidence for her cult is chiefly epigraphic, and no securely identified image has been preserved.

Attributes

Raven or crow, sword and shield, the field of battle at her feet, the fallen warriors over whom she stands.

Sacred Sites

The Alpine region of Continental Celtic settlement, particularly in what is now Haute-Savoie and the neighboring Gaulish territories, where inscriptions record her cult. Her worship belongs to the pre-Roman Gaulish religion carried into the Roman period through the interpretatio romana pattern.

Ritual

Detailed ritual practice is not preserved. By analogy with the wider Celtic war-goddess complex, her worship likely involved the invocation before battle, the dedication of weapons or captured spoils in thanksgiving after battle, and the honoring of the fallen at the field.

Lore

Cathubodua's importance lies in her role as the Continental Celtic mirror of the Irish Badb Catha, and her cult is one of the strongest pieces of evidence for the shared religious inheritance of the Continental and Insular Celtic peoples. The battle-raven figure appears across the Celtic world in various names and forms, from the Continental Cathubodua to the Irish Badb, Nemain, and Morrigan, and the shared name element bodua or badb marks these figures as expressions of a single deep Celtic religious concept: the fierce feminine consciousness of the battlefield, embodied as the raven who circles and reads the outcome of the fighting.
