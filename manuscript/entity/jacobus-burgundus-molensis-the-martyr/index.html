Gnostic Saints

Although this last Grand Master of the Knights Templar is classified as a Gnostic saint under the Latin variant of his name, Jacobus Burgundus Molensis, he is more familiarly known by his French name, Jacques de Molay. Born in approximately 1240, he was the twenty-third Grand Master of this chivalric organization.

Life and History

Little is known of his personal life beyond his role as Grand Master. It is assumed, based on his name, that he was from Molay in the kingdom of Burgundy. He became a Templar in 1265 and traveled to Outremer, the Christian Crusader Kingdom of Jerusalem, in approximately 1270. He may have been Master of the Temple in England before becoming Grand Master. After the fall of Outremer, de Molay relocated to Cyprus, where he became Grand Master in 1293 and engaged in attempts to retake the Holy Land.

The Arrest

King Philip IV of France (1268 to 29 November 1314), also known as Philip le Bel, was heavily in debt to the Templars and wanted to merge the Knights Templar with the Knights Hospitaller, making himself Grand Master of both orders. De Molay adamantly disagreed but was invited by the pope to France to discuss the possible merger.

On 14 September 1307, King Philip sent secret messages coordinating a mass arrest of all Templar Knights throughout France at dawn on Friday, 13 October. Some believe that the superstition that Friday the 13th is an unlucky day derives from this arrest. That morning, Jacques de Molay was serving as a pallbearer at the funeral of the king's sister-in-law. Together with sixty other Templars, he was arrested and tortured into confessing to various charges.

Trial and Execution

The Council of Vienne convened to judge the Knights Templar refused to convict them of heresy, but on 22 March 1313, Pope Clement V abolished the order anyway. Fifty-four Templar knights had already been burned at the stake when, on 10 March 1314, three cardinals sentenced de Molay and three other Templar dignitaries to life imprisonment.

Rather than accept this sentence, Jacques de Molay recanted his confession, proclaiming his innocence and that of the Templars. The only guilt he admitted was betraying their order by false confessions in hope of saving their lives. One of the other knights, Geoffroi de Charney, joined him.

King Philip ordered the two men executed immediately as heretics. Jacques de Molay was burned at the stake on 18 March 1314 on the Isle des Juifs in the Seine River, near the palace garden in Paris. He was seventy years old. De Molay asked that his hands be left free when tied to the stake so he could hold them in prayer while facing the Cathedral of Notre Dame.

The Curse

De Molay and de Charney were roasted slowly over a smokeless fire to ensure they did not die quickly from smoke asphyxiation. Fire was first applied directly to their feet and genitals. De Molay called out from the flames that King Philip and Pope Clement would soon join him.

The pope died slightly over a month later, on 20 April 1314. Lightning struck the church where he lay in state, and the resulting fire almost completely destroyed his body. The king lasted until November, dying on 29 November 1314 after a seizure while hunting. Their deaths were widely viewed as the result of de Molay's curse.

The Shroud of Turin Theory

De Molay and de Charney met death with such composure that the crowd perceived them as martyrs. Their ashes and bone fragments were collected as relics. A theory suggests that Jacques de Molay's face is emblazoned on the Shroud of Turin.

According to this theory, during his captivity de Molay may have been tortured in a manner similar to Jesus. Following torture, his bleeding body was wrapped in a shroud. Blood and lactic acid from his wounds may have blended with frankincense used to whiten the fabric, etching his image into the shroud. The first reliably authenticated owner of the shroud was Jeanne de Vergy, widow of French knight Geoffroi de Charney, believed to be the nephew of the knight burned with de Molay.

Iconography

A bearded man wielding a sword and wearing the uniform of a Templar knight, with white clothing emblazoned with a large cross.

Feast Days

18 March (death)

13 October (anniversary of arrest, especially if it falls on Friday the 13th)

Sacred Sites

A plaque in Paris marks the site of his execution.

The Shroud of Turin is housed in a side chapel of the Cathedral of John the Baptist in Turin.

A full-size replica of the Shroud exists in the Chapel of the Holy Relics in Rome's Basilica di Santa Croce in Gerusalemme.
