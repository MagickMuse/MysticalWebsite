The Devil
⚠️
About the "Demon" Label: The Christian Devil is not a single ancient being but a theological composite assembled from Jewish, Pagan, and Zoroastrian sources. Many Pagan horned deities (Pan, Faunus, Cernunnos, Ördög) were falsely identified with this figure during Christianization. The horned god did not become the devil; the devil was given the horned god's face. Understanding this distinction is foundational to working respectfully with horned spirits.

Magickal Properties

The devil is a complex Christian concept representing the evil force that opposes Jesus Christ. His role is to tempt Christians and undermine Christianity. He is also the ruler of Hell, the Christian realm where damned souls are eternally punished. The devil is a trickster who tempts people to perform acts for which he will later punish them.

This idea of the devil is a Christian concept, this devil did not exist prior to Christianity. It took centuries for him to evolve into the form that became familiar during the Middle Ages and remains so today. The Christian devil evolved from Jewish, Pagan, and Zoroastrian sources, but is not identical with any of them. Because this modern conception is thus an amalgamation, he is often a contradictory and elusive figure, perhaps befitting a trickster.

Origins of the Fallen Angels (Lucifer)

According to Jewish tradition, at the very beginning of time, some angels visiting Earth fell in love with the Daughters of Man (human women), causing them to betray their angelic vows. These angels taught women all kinds of secrets and various magickal arts including metalworking. The Creator banished them from the heavenly host or inflicted severe punishment. Many of these rebel angels evolved into dangerous, volatile spirits associated with witchcraft and the occult and are sometimes described as "demons." Many became allied with Lilith. These ex-angels include Azazel, Samael, and someone described as the beautiful "Son of the Morning Star."

This notion of Fallen Angels entered Christian mythology and continued to evolve. In the Jewish story, the angels come to Earth and transgress once they're here, they rebel against rules and yield to temptation.

In the Christian story, the angels are cast out of Heaven as punishment. Various reasons are given including their refusal to pay homage to Man. The most common reason is that the Chief of the Rebel Angels believed himself equal or superior to God and thus challenged him, intending to take over the throne of Heaven.

The rebellion failed and this angel with his celestial army of followers was thrown out of Heaven. The devil is generally understood to be the chief of the fallen angels. Demons are children of fallen angels and human mothers, which relates back to the original story.

Lucifer: The Light Bringer

The name usually given the chief fallen angel is Lucifer. "Lucifer" means "Light bringer" and it is an ancient epithet attached to many Italian divinities including Juno and Fauna, daughter and close ally of the horned spirit Faunus. It initially indicated glory, not evil.

Lucifer is a beautiful devil. He is not a horned spirit. In the earliest Christian depictions of Lucifer, he is indistinguishable from other angels, except that he is consistently portrayed as falling.

Some translate Lucifer as an amalgamation of light (luci) and iron (fer), interesting in light of the devil's associations with blacksmiths.

Satan and the Serpent

The first animal to be associated by Christianity with Satan was the snake. The Book of Revelation, last book of the Christian Bible, first identifies Satan with the serpent in the Garden of Eden. Snakes and dragons (then understood as great snakes) were strongly identified with Pagan traditions, especially those associated with female divinity and power.

Snakes were sacred in many traditions and particularly associated with women's primal power and mysteries. Tales of devout Christian knights who slay dragons are metaphors for the destruction of Pagan spiritual traditions.

The Amalgamation

Devas, Ha-Satan, and Lucifer are pre-Christian concepts that eventually merged within Christianity.

Additional Notes

The word "devil" is related to the Indo-European root word de or "divine." In pre-Zoroastrian Iran, a deva or dev was a divine being. The word still retains this meaning in Buddhism and Hinduism, where Hindu devas are sacred and benevolent.

Christians were not the first to diabolize other people's gods. When Zoroastrianism came to prominence in Iran, the word "devil" came to indicate dangerous, evil spirits. Zoroastrianism is intensely dualist. The devas form the army of the Lord of Darkness, opponent of the Lord of Light. The Christian conception of the devil as an opposing and almost equal force derives from Zoroastrian tradition, as does this use of the word "devil."

Evolution of the Devil's Physical Form

By the fourth century when Christianity achieved political power, a new vision of the devil emerged that physically resembled male horned spirits, especially those identified with goats. This image superceded all others. The New Testament has no specific physical description of Satan.

Pagan worship and devotion to the male horned god was prevalent throughout Europe. He was a major impediment to Christianity. The horned god resembles a wild goat: rambunctious, anarchic, fun-loving, defiant, and uncontrollable.

The horned gods celebrate the physical glories of Earth. They are voraciously hungry spirits who constantly crave sex, food, intoxicating substances, and comfort. They dance, sing, and make merry. They create musical instruments, teach people to play, invent wine, sponsor shamans, and proudly display their ever-erect phalluses.

When the horned god manifests as a man, he appears as an archetypal Wild Man, resistant to rules, civilization, and all authority. The horned spirits are mediating spirits who negotiate balance between people, animals, and plants. They are not dualist. Their gift is fertility and abundance.

The Horned God is the Lord of Wild Nature, the powers of Earth. His domain eventually became the domain of the Christian devil. To undermine widespread devotion to horned spirits, Christianity labeled their worship as evil. Eventually the horned male god became the prototype for the devil's physical manifestation.

The Devil's Physical Description

By Pope Gregory the Great's reign (590-604 CE), the devil assumed the form of a hunchbacked bearded goat-skinned man with cloven hooves, horns, and a stick. Gregory described Satan as a black man possessing goat's horns and hooves, an evil stench, and the power to control weather.

The devil's stick links him to the phallic sticks and staffs carried by horned spirits like Pan, Hermes, Dionysus, and Krampus. The horned spirit's stick is an emblem of phallic power. According to Christian version, however, the devil's stick is used to punish and torture people and also as a crutch because he is lame.

The rationale for his limp is that when cast out of heaven, he fell and permanently injured his foot. This vision of a limping devil links him to various lame gods and holy people including Hephaestus, Dionysus, Oedipus, Achilles, and Hermes with his one sandal and shepherd's crook. It also links him to the traditional shaman's dance.

Sometimes instead of an ordinary stick or staff, the devil carries a pitchfork. One theory suggests the image of the pitchfork-wielding horned devil is based on Shiva, the pre-Aryan deity from India. Shiva dances in fire carrying a trident that resembles a pitchfork and leads a wild retinue of demons and witches.

Horned spirits like Hermes and Cernunnos often carry huge sacks from which they distribute largesse. The Christian devil also carries a large sack but in his case it is in which to carry away damned souls. That sack survives in modern Krampus and Santa Claus imagery.

Sometimes instead of being carried, the sack was envisioned as internalized, part of the Horned God/Wild Man's body. The hunchback's hump was perceived as this internal bag of treasure, hence the powerful association of hunchbacks with luck.

The Devil and Witchcraft

The devil was no longer perceived as merely the head of a formerly angelic host. Instead he led a fifth column of human devotees, identified by the Inquisition as witches. Eventually for the Inquisition, worshipping the devil was what constituted witchcraft.

There are two different issues: People were accused of worshipping the devil, and people worshipped deities that Christians perceived as the devil. People worshipped horned spirits and other Pagan deities and many still do. However in these spiritual traditions, there is no devil. Horned spirits are not the devil.

It is crucial to distinguish between what witches really believe versus what outsiders intrinsically opposed to witchcraft say that witches believe.

Other people did worship the Christian conception of Satan. This worship arose within Christianity as a reaction against Christianity. In its purest form "Satanism" simply reverses or opposes anything Christian. In order to genuinely worship the devil, one must subscribe to the Christian vision of the devil, as it is the only tradition in which he exists.
