Magickal Properties

Adonis is a breathtakingly handsome spirit whose name epitomizes male good looks. Son of a myrrh tree, he was rescued as a baby by Aphrodite and given to Persephone for safekeeping, leading to a custody battle resolved by dividing the year into thirds. He is fun, gorgeous, a divine lover and deity of pleasure who loves loving and hunting.

Ritual Uses

Adonis was the subject of a Mystery religion venerated almost exclusively by women. His festival, the Adonia, mourned his death then celebrated his resurrection. Women gathered on rooftops at night, drinking and singing, planting Gardens of Adonis, quick-germinating seeds in shallow containers tossed into the sea on the eighth day.

Offerings

Sex toys or hunting gear, aphrodisiacs, arak (ancient alcoholic beverage), special Easter cakes and bread, sensuous gifts that honor his dual nature as lover and hunter. Do not feed him pork.

Favored People

Women (his cult was venerated almost exclusively by women), lovers, those seeking sexual healing, people working with death and resurrection mysteries.

Petition For

Necromantic communication between realms, revelation of missing persons' whereabouts, delivering messages between living and dead, determining where to search for missing people, healing from sexual trauma.

Warnings

Do not venerate him alone; include Aphrodite or Persephone and offer sensuous gifts, quick-germinating garden rites, and abstain from pork because Adonis is a chthonic mystery deity whose cult is communal and specific.

Additional Notes

While hunting, he was gored and castrated by a wild boar, bleeding to death. The blood that flowed onto Earth transformed into red windflowers. Every year Adonis is resurrected, like Persephone, he eternally enters Death only to return alive. He passes easily between realms and can deliver messages back and forth. He is omnisexual and represents the beauty that transcends death.

Tammuz: The Mesopotamian Aspect

In Mesopotamian tradition, Adonis is known as Tammuz or Dumuzi, the divine shepherd who was Inanna-Ishtar's lover. He quickly became her favorite, and she honored him above all others. Their love affair is the subject of sacred hymns.

Following her resurrection, Inanna-Ishtar was permitted to leave captivity in the Realm of Death if she sent someone back to replace her. Returning to her palace, she anticipated her reunion with Tammuz, whom she assumed would be weeping and mourning for her. Instead she discovered him seated on her throne, enjoying the luxuries of her palace. She chose him as her replacement.

Tammuz fled but Inanna-Ishtar pursued. He did not wish to die. His devoted sister Gestinanna fought on his behalf, eventually negotiating a deal in which she serves half his time annually while Tammuz is permitted to return to life.

Tammuz is considered the prototype of the dying and resurrecting grain deity. He is compared to Persephone, a force of nature that refuses to stay dead, although disappearing into the depths of Earth annually. After his descent into darkness, Inanna-Ishtar wept bitterly for his loss, beginning the annual sacred ritual of weeping for Tammuz.

This ritual was still honored centuries later and had been incorporated into folk Judaism. Ezekiel 8:14 recounts the prophet's vision of Jewish women mourning for Tammuz at the gate of King Solomon's Temple. Tammuz, sometime chthonic spirit, is among those deities invoked in the magickal spells of the Magickal Papyri of Alexandria.

Sacred Time

Ritual mourning for Tammuz begins annually with the Summer Solstice. The Hebrew lunar month Tammuz is named in his honor, corresponding in time to the zodiac sign Cancer. The corresponding month in the Turkish calendar is Temmouz.

Attributes

Lapis lazuli flute

Period and Cult Centers

Formal worship of Adonis under that name flourished roughly 200 BCE through 400 CE, with principal cult centers at Berytus on the Phoenician coast and at Aphaca in the mountains of Lebanon.

The River Adonis

The river known in antiquity as the Adonis is the modern Nahr Ibrahim in Lebanon. Each year its waters run red with iron-rich silt washed from the mountains, and the ancient cult read this seasonal staining as the blood of Adonis returning to the sea.

Parentage

Greek tradition gives two genealogies. In the more common version he is the son of Cinyras of Cyprus and his daughter Myrrha, conceived through her transgression and his unwitting participation. An older variant names his parents as Phoenix and Alphesiboea.

Iconography

He is most often depicted nude, holding or playing a lyre, with the smooth youthful body and long hair that became the standard image of divine male beauty.

Etruscan and Local Names

In Etruscan reception he appears as Atunis, attested in local inscriptions from circa 350 BC onward. Other regional sources preserve the name Arris.
