Magickal Properties

Demeter is the primordial Corn Mother, the spirit of cultivation and crops. Her name means "Divine Mother" or "Deified Mother" (De = divinity + Meter = Mother), or alternatively "Barley Mother" from the Cretan word deai. She is credited as the founder, inventor, and promulgator of agriculture. A tremendously powerful ancient indigenous Greek goddess incorporated into the Olympian pantheon, though she preferred to live on Earth rather than in Olympus.

She presided over the famous Mysteries of Eleusis near Athens alongside Persephone, as well as Arcadian Mysteries with her daughter Despoena, and the Mysteries at Lerna. She is a great magician with particular talent for transformation, able to take any form including horse, hag, or beautiful woman with golden hair resembling a field of ripe wheat.

Ritual Uses

Invoke Demeter for agricultural magick, fertility work, and transformation rituals. She is particularly powerful during planting and harvest seasons. Work with her in Mystery traditions focused on death and rebirth cycles. Her magick involves growth, abundance, seasonal transitions, and profound personal transformation. She can bestow healing, fertility, protection, and prosperity, but also punish through insatiable hunger if disrespected.

Offerings

Barley and wheat (her sacred grains), other grain offerings, poppies, pomegranates, pennyroyal, honey cakes, fresh bread, seasonal fruits and vegetables from harvest, votive pigs, images of women carrying piglets, focaccia bread shaped like genitals. Ritual cakes dedicated to Demeter were thrown into sacred chasms during the Thesmophoria festival. In Rome (where she is known as Ceres), women offered millet cakes. Do NOT offer wine. She prefers mint-flavored barley water and other non-alcoholic beverages.

Favored People

Farmers and agricultural workers, those seeking fertility (for land, animals, or themselves), practitioners of Mystery traditions, anyone working with growth magick, transformation, seasonal cycles, or the mysteries of death and rebirth.

Petition For

Abundant harvests and agricultural success, fertility for land, animals, and people, healing from illness, protection for crops and livestock, prosperity and abundance, successful transformation and personal growth, wisdom of agricultural cycles, understanding of life-death-rebirth mysteries.

Warnings

If angered, Demeter punishes by causing insatiable hunger. Another myth involving Demeter suggests that she was the very last Greek deity to stop accepting human sacrifice.

She is incredibly powerful and should be approached with proper respect for agricultural taboos and seasonal rites. Do not attempt Eleusinian-style mysteries without proper lineage or context. Her transformative magick is profound, be prepared for deep changes when working with her.

Additional Notes

Demeter's votive imagery shows her holding wheat in one hand, poppies in the other, while snakes writhe around her. Sometimes she brandishes a pomegranate. She famously has golden hair like a field of ripe wheat. She is a shape-shifter who can appear as horse, hag, or beautiful woman with golden hair.

Sacred animals include pigs, horses, and snakes (as well as grasshoppers and cranes). Her sacred number is 8. Associated with oak trees, entheogenic and visionary plants. Her attributes include torch, ear of wheat, scythe (lost while searching for Persephone), and basket.

Demeter Melaina (Black Demeter) represents her aspect as black fertile earth and is the prototype for several Black Madonnas.

Demeter is not an Earth goddess; she is specifically the spirit of cultivation and crops. Instead of residing in Olympus, she prefers to live on Earth. Although she has liaisons (notably with her brother Zeus, Persephone's father), she is an independent, unmarried woman. She raises her daughter herself.

Her traditional offerings included raw grain, raw honeycombs, and unspun wool, simple offerings that indicate that Demeter was a deity of the ordinary people.

Demeter's most famous myth is the saga of the kidnapping of her daughter Persephone. One day, Persephone, usually identified as the Spirit of Spring, spots an unusual and beautiful black narcissus. It's a trap. When she plucks it, Earth breaks open beneath her feet. Hades, her uncle, Lord of the Dead, rides up in his chariot, grabs Persephone and pulls her down to his realm. In a moment, Earth closes up, as if this incident never occurred.

Persephone just has time to scream; Demeter hears her and comes running but is unable to locate her. Persephone has been playing with various maidens but not one has witnessed her kidnapping. Demeter proceeds to behave like any parent who has lost a child; she runs around hysterically searching, with absolutely no success. She beseeches help from her fellow gods. Only Hecate, lunar Spirit of Witchcraft, offers her assistance.

Demeter's search for Persephone is a lengthy epic saga; she has many adventures and encounters many characters. She eventually receives information from two sources: a young swineherd, the sole witness to the crime (several of his pigs fell into the chasm together with Persephone), and Hecate brings Demeter to Helios the Sun, who confirms that Hades has kidnapped Persephone.

Demeter demands that Zeus, King of the Gods, force Hades to return Persephone. This is when she discovers that technically Hades didn't kidnap Persephone, or at least not from his perspective, as Persephone's father Zeus gave her to him. Neither bothered to consult with mother or daughter/bride. Hades refuses to send her back; Zeus isn't interested in attempting to force him.

In response, beautiful, golden Demeter transforms into the Corn Mother's shadow side. She abruptly withdraws her gift of fertility from Earth: nothing grows. She begins to wander in the guise of an old, gray, bitter, gloomy, humorless, and dangerous hag; she still has her goddess powers; she is still grand. It is during this period that she founds the Eleusinian Mystery religion. A reaped ear of corn (wheat) was displayed as the central mystery at Eleusis.

People begin to starve. Customary offerings to the gods are no longer forthcoming and so the gods begin to starve too. Eventually the protests of the other gods, as well as the potentially disastrous weakening of their powers normally fueled by offerings, finally forces Zeus to order Hades to return Persephone to Demeter. Hermes is sent to fetch her.

Meanwhile in Hades, Persephone has been raped and set on the throne as the queen of Hades. In a parallel action to her mourning mother, she has been on a hunger strike. However, she has consumed six seeds from one of Hades' pomegranate trees and so Hades refuses to let her leave with Hermes. By eating the food of the dead, she has joined their ranks.

A compromise is reached: Persephone will spend half the year with her mother on Earth, half with her husband in Hades. The time spent with Hades corresponds to the period following the harvest when crops are dormant; she emerges on Earth with the first breath of spring. When she is in Hades, her mother mourns and nothing grows; when Persephone emerges in springtime, her mother rejoices and crops are abundant.

This story is ages old; countless interpretations exist. The most obvious is that Persephone, daughter of the Corn Mother, is a metaphor for grain. Hidden undercurrents, however, lie beneath the tale of Persephone's kidnapping and Demeter's subsequent grief, desolation, and rage. It is more than the tale of one mother's loss and more than just an allegory of the harvest.

Demeter in her grieving stage is the prototype for the Hag archetype. When Persephone was kidnapped, Demeter shed her beauty like a snake sheds its skin, transforming into a hag in an instant. She aged, becoming gray, wrinkled, and bent over. The light disappeared from her eyes. She was emotionally bereft, burning with grief, rage, and a passionate desire for justice.

As Demeter transformed, she withdrew fertility from Earth: crops failed, people began to starve. In her hag aspect, however, the normally benevolent Demeter doesn't care, her own grief overwhelms her to the exclusion of anyone else's. This is the essence of the Hag: not merely an old woman, but one transformed by intense grief, rage, and loss into something beyond ordinary aging.

Her saga is often interpreted as an allegory for the year's seasons, her hag phase corresponds with winter while her transformation back into a bountiful goddess with Persephone's annual return corresponds to spring. However, this seasonal interpretation tends to gloss over the emotional and spiritual aspects of her saga. Demeter doesn't just age and become an average old woman; she transforms into the epitome of a Hag specifically because of her overwhelming emotional state.

Demeter's saga, which ultimately became the central focus of the Eleusinian Mysteries, also recounts her healing process and recovery. Demeter rages and grieves, starving Earth and herself, until an aged female servant, a crone but not a hag, named Baubo finally draws an involuntary laugh from Demeter.

Baubo accomplishes this, where others failed, through the mysterious act of ana-suromai, the ritual act of exposing the vagina. This act, which also features in Egyptian mythology, is believed to represent the eternal life force, the unbeatable power of the Great Mother. The significance in Demeter's situation is profound: the ritual act is performed by an old woman for whom literal fertility is not possible. This demonstrates that the power transcends biological fertility and speaks to deeper spiritual forces.

From the moment of her reviving laugh, according to the saga, Demeter channels her private grief into spiritual leadership. This transformation from personal devastation to collective spiritual guidance became the foundation of the Eleusinian Mysteries.

If Persephone represents grain then the story may also be understood as a metaphor for the transfer of power over agriculture and its rituals from women to men with different spiritual orientations. Persephone is now a prize to be violently taken without consulting the Corn Mother. Who owns the child? Previously the child belonged to its mother; Zeus asserts father-right, a radical concept at one time. Hades does not actually kidnap Persephone, her father gave her to him. The two men (father and prospective husband) negotiated the deal without input from mother or daughter, a scenario that replays daily in much of today's world.

Demeter the Corn Mother lives humbly on Earth, not in the palaces of Olympus. Her traditional offerings included raw grain, raw honeycombs, and unspun wool, simple offerings that indicate that Demeter was a deity of the ordinary people. If Persephone is a metaphor for grain, then who owns the grain? Among the underlying themes of the saga are class and property issues very relevant to emergent agrarian societies: rank exists in all societies but peasants don't exist among hunter-gatherers.

The word "cereal" derives from Ceres' name. This Etruscan goddess has now become almost completely identified with Demeter but was originally an independent deity closely allied with Tellus, the Earth Mother. Tellus is the Earth herself; Ceres, her closest companion, is the Spirit of Grain and Cultivation. They shared a festival, the Sementivae, from January 24th through 26th, during which they were petitioned to protect seeds and their sowers.

Ceres had a temple on Rome's Aventine Hill not far from that of Diana. She was originally associated with women's mysteries. By the third century BCE, her worship in Roman territory was heavily influenced by Demeter and the Eleusinian Rites, and she was identified as the mother of Proserpina.

Priestesses originally led her rites, but in the Roman republic few women were allowed positions of spiritual authority. Eventually the official Roman state cult of Ceres would be supervised by the male flamen cerealis. Perhaps in protest, the names of paternal relatives were never pronounced in the precincts of Ceres, unusual in intensely patriarchal Rome.

Ceres' attributes include a scepter and a basket overflowing with flowers and fruits. She is adorned with garlands of grain. Her sacred animal is the pig. Pigs were kept in grottoes beneath her Italian temples, and sleeping among these pigs was a sacred method of incubating dreams.

The Greater Mysteries

The rites that grew from Demeter's saga were the most important of all the mystery traditions of antiquity, observed in two parts. The Lesser Mysteries, dedicated to Persephone, were held each year at a town called Agrae, while the Greater Mysteries, dedicated to Demeter, were kept at Eleusis. They endured for an extraordinary span, from roughly 1500 BCE until the fourth century CE.

Their power was guarded jealously. Initiates swore on their own lives never to speak of what they witnessed to the uninitiated, so no certain account survives. We know only that part of the ceremony revealed the contents of a casket and a covered basket; what lay inside has been variously guessed to be an egg, sacred seeds, or a golden serpent.

At Eleusis everyone fasted, in memory of Demeter's own fast while she searched for her daughter. The fast was broken with a herbal drink called kykeon, brewed with barley, a grain prone to the fungus ergot, which carries psychoactive properties. After long fasting these effects may have been especially potent, and could well have helped lift the "veil of unreality" in the hall called the Telestrion, which only initiates could enter and where repeating anything seen or heard was punishable by death. There is a distinctly shamanic character to the whole undertaking; the Greater Mysteries taught the secrets of spiritual regeneration and the freeing of the higher self from ignorance. Their finale was a great feast in the Rharian Field, said to be where the first grain ever grew, crowned by the sacrifice of a bull.

Because their very nature was secret, the Mysteries could not be absorbed into the new Christian faith as so many older customs were; there was simply nothing left in the open to absorb. With nothing to inherit, the incoming order desecrated the old sacred sites at the close of the fourth century CE.

Triptolemus and the Gift of Grain

Ceres became the foster mother to Triptolemus, a figure the Roman tradition casts as an ill-fated king whose role in the mythic cycle of grain and cultivation parallels that of the Mesopotamian Dumuzi in his capacity as the mortal beloved of the grain goddess. Triptolemus is depicted centrally in the classical Greek Eleusinian Mysteries, in which Demeter/Ceres appears as the one who gave the gift of grain cultivation to mortals and who mourns during the barren half of the year when her daughter remains below ground with Pluto. As the embodiment of vegetation and the growing harvest, Ceres withdraws her gift of growth during the winter period of Proserpina's time in the underworld, restoring it when her daughter is returned to her in spring.

Festivals

Ceres was honored at two major Roman festivals. The Thesmophoria, shared with Demeter, was a women's mystery festival involving fasting and sacred rites. The Cerealia, held in April, was the distinctively Roman festival of Ceres herself, celebrated with games in the Circus Maximus, with the peculiar custom of tying burning torches to the tails of foxes released into the arena, and with offerings by women who fasted throughout the preceding night. Her cult maintained sanctuaries throughout the Greco-Roman world and was honored by her own flamen cerealis in the later Roman state religion.

Role in Hellenic Household Worship

At the household level, offerings to Demeter (Demetra) include the poppy and tokens of pigs, with styrax incense and mint reserved for Her chthonic aspect known as Khthonia. Her tokens are the sickle, the torch, and the plough. The colours linked to Her are scarlet drawn from the poppy and the tones of earth. Her sacred animals are the dove, the bee, and the pig. Her sacred grain is wheat.
