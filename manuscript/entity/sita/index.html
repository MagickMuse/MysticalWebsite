Wife of Rama

Sita, wife of King Rama, was kidnapped by Ravana, King of the Rakshasas, and imprisoned in Sri Lanka. Although she never succumbed to his charms, she was forced to prove her virtue through fire ordeals. The Ramayana tells her story of rescue and the not-so-happy aftermath where public doubt led to her banishment or her voluntary return to Earth.

Goddess in Exile

Sita is the goddess in exile, forced to leave the palace, her husband, and her rightful throne. She always maintains her dignity. She is the goddess of women who must bear humiliation and maintain grace despite suspicion, slander, and abuse.

The Early History of Sita

The word sita means "furrow" or "the line made by the plow," and is the name of a goddess associated with plowed fields in Vedic literature. In a hymn addressed to the lord of the fields, Ksetrapati, she is invoked:

Auspicious Sita, come thou near: we venerate and worship thee that thou mayst bless and prosper us and bring us fruits abundantly. May Indra press the furrow down, may Pushan guide its course aright. May she, as rich in milk, be drained for us through each succeeding year.

In the Kausika-sutra she is the wife of Parjanya, a god associated with rain, and is described as the "mother of gods, mortals and creatures" petitioned for growth and prosperity. In the Paraskara-sutra she is the wife of Indra, a god often associated with rain and fertility, and is offered cooked rice and barley in the sacrificial fire. In the Vajasaneyi-samhita she is invoked when four furrows are drawn during a sacrificial ritual, an act intended to ensure the abundance and fertility of the crops. She is also invoked as one of the names of the goddess Arya in the Harivamsa:

O goddess, you are the altar's center in the sacrifice, the priest's fee, Sita to those who hold the plough, and Earth to all living beings.

Sita is not a very significant deity prior to the Ramayana of Valmiki. She is not mentioned very often and is overshadowed by much more popular goddesses associated with fertility, such as Sri-Lakshmi. Nevertheless, Sita does seem to be part of a fundamental intuition concerning the fertility of the plowed earth and the necessity of a male power to awaken, arouse, and inseminate her. Underlying her connection with Indra, Parjanya, and other male deities associated with the inseminating effects of rain seems to be the basic perception that the ongoing fertility of the cosmos is the result of the interaction between the sky and the earth, between male and female, between the latent powers of the field and the inseminating effects of the plow.

Kings and the Fertility of the Earth

The identification of Rama's wife with a goddess of the plowed fields seems to be related to the central role that kings in ancient India were assumed to play in promoting the fertility of the land. The interrelation between fertility and the manly vigor of the king finds its prototype in the Rg-veda, where the mighty god Indra combats the demon Vrtra, who withholds the creative, nourishing waters of creation. Indra having defeated the demon, the waters rush forth to fructify the earth.

The theme of the king's bringing forth the abundance and fertility of the earth is central in the myths concerning Prthu, the first human king. Prior to Prthu's reign the earth was inhospitable and her fertility remained untapped. The Mahabharata describes Prthu as leveling the earth's mountains and hills to make her fit for agriculture and as milking the earth like a cow (7.69). According to the Prthu myths, the earth, although fertile and potent, does not yield the abundance of her interior without being stimulated by a heroic, royal figure. Conversely, the king's reign will not be fruitful unless he can draw forth the richness of the earth.

The myth of the churning of the ocean may also be understood in this vein. Vishnu, as the cosmic ruler, plays the central role in his various forms: as the tortoise that provides the foundation for the churning stick; as the cosmic serpent Vasuki, who provides the churning rope; as the seductress Mohini, who prevents the demons from partaking of the nectar of immortality. The central action is the churning of the ocean of milk to make it yield the nectar of immortality. The result of Vishnu's action is the drawing forth of the abundance of the earth. Central among these is the goddess Sri-Lakshmi — sovereignty personified, bringing wealth and abundance wherever a righteous king reigns.

The Ramayana takes care to portray Rama as the ideal king and his rule as a model of social perfection. His winning Sita at her svayamvara, their subsequent marriage, and Rama's regaining Sita from the clutches of the demon Ravana should be understood as an expression of this ancient pattern in Indian religion. Sita is no mere human being. Her birth is supernatural, and she is called ayonija, "not born of a womb" (1.66; 2.30), and in appearance she is often likened to Sri-Lakshmi (5.12). According to the Ramayana, Sita is literally unearthed when her father, King Janaka, is plowing (1.66). Janaka was almost certainly involved in a royal ritual, part of which involved the king's plowing the earth to bring about fertility — an act likened to a symbolic coupling of the king with the latent powers of the earth. The effectiveness of the ritual plowing is manifest in the birth of Sita, the earth's personified fertility, abundance, and well-being.

The marriage of Rama and Sita represents a further interplay between a vigorous, virtuous, powerful king and a woman who symbolizes the fecund forces of the earth. The ultimate result of this auspicious relationship between kingly virility and earthly fertility is the inauguration of Ramrajya, "the rule of Rama," an idealized reign in which harmony, longevity, order, fruitful crops, and all social and economic virtues dominate society.

The Ideal Wife

Sita is defined in the Ramayana and in the subsequent cult of Rama almost entirely in relation to her husband. She is portrayed as the ideal Hindu wife, whose every thought revolves around Rama. For Sita, Rama is the center of her life — she is always steadfast in her loyalty to him, and his welfare, reputation, and wishes are uppermost in her mind.

Sita is the ideal pativrata, the wife devoted entirely to her husband. In her selfless devotion and sexual fidelity the pativrata nourishes an inner heat (tapas) that both purifies her and provides her with a destructive weapon that can be used against those who might threaten her purity. This inner heat generated as a result of marital fidelity is understood to be similar to the heat generated through asceticism.

Sita's mythological role as the ideal wife and pativrata is illustrated in several incidents in the Ramayana:

The Exile — When Rama is told he must go into exile in the forest for fourteen years, he prepares to leave Sita behind in Ayodhya. She is grief stricken and delivers a long discourse on her desire to go with him, returning always to the point that a husband is a god to his wife and that apart from him a wife might as well commit suicide. She says:

For a woman, it is not her father, her son, nor her mother, friends nor her own self, but the husband, who in this world and the next is ever her sole means of salvation. ... Ever subject to thy will, docile, living like an ascetic, in those honey-scented woodlands I shall be happy in thy proximity, O Rama, O Illustrious Lord. (2.27)

Captivity in Lanka — When Ravana abducts Sita and takes her to Lanka, he keeps her prisoner in a garden surrounded by demonesses. Through a series of metaphors Valmiki captures both Sita's great beauty and her great grief: she is described as "a flame wreathed in smoke" and "a lotus pool stripped of its flowers." Although her beauty is dimmed and she lacks the presence of Rama, she is described as constantly remembering him and shining beautifully as a result. Hanuman observes: "For a woman the greatest decoration is her lord and Sita, though incomparably beautiful, no longer shines in Rama's absence" (5.16). When Ravana proposes that she abandon Rama and take him as her husband, she refuses and tells him that if she wished she could burn him to ashes with the fire she has accumulated from her chastity (5.22) — but that she will not, because she has not been given Rama's permission.

When Hanuman finds Sita and proposes to carry her back to India, she declines — not only because touching another male would violate her devotion to Rama, but also because it would mean Rama would not obtain the glory involved in rescuing her. His welfare and reputation are always uppermost in her mind.

The Fire Ordeal — After Rama defeats Ravana, he scowls at Sita and announces he has only undertaken the defeat of Ravana to uphold his family's honor, not out of love for her. He disclaims her, saying it would be improper to take her back after she had spent time under another man's control. Sita protests her innocence and asks Laksmana to build a funeral pyre, having been publicly renounced by Rama and preferring death to dishonor. As the fire is kindled she addresses Agni, the god of fire:

As my heart has never ceased to be true to Raghava, do thou, O Witness of all Beings, grant me thy protection! As I am pure in conduct, though Rama looks on me as sullied, do thou, O Witness of the Worlds, grant me full protection! (6.118)

Because of her innocence and purity, Agni refuses to harm her and returns her to Rama so unscathed that even her flower garland remains unwithered. Rama, convinced of her purity, accepts her back.

The Second Exile — Back in Ayodhya during the glorious era of Ramrajya, Rama hears that his citizens are gossiping about Sita. To stop this gossip and set a stainless example, he commands his brother Laksmana to abandon her in the forest — even though she is pregnant. When told of Rama's decision, Sita does not blame Rama. She asks Laksmana to send Rama this message:

As for me, I am not distressed on mine own account, O Prince of Raghu, it is for thee to keep thy fair name untarnished! The husband is as a God to the woman, he is her family, and her spiritual preceptor, therefore, even at the price of her life, she must seek to please her lord. (7.48)

She gives birth to twin sons and spends several years in exile in a forest hermitage. When Rama finally summons her back to undergo a public ordeal to absolve him of shame, Sita agrees — but it seems she no longer relishes life. She asks, on the basis of her purity and loyalty to Rama, to be taken back into the bosom of her mother, the goddess Earth. She says: "If, in thought, I have never dwelt on any but Rama, may the Goddess Madhavi receive me!" (7.97). As she finishes this act of truth, a throne rises from the ground supported by serpents. Earth embraces Sita, seats her on the throne, and the throne and Sita sink back into the ground. Rama lives out his life in sorrow, never remarries, and has a golden image of Sita made which he uses in her place at religious rituals requiring the presence of a wife.

Sita as Paradigm — Sita's self-effacing nature, her steadfast loyalty, and her chastity make her both the ideal Hindu wife and the ideal pativrata. In a sense Sita has no independent existence, no independent destiny. In all things she sees herself as inextricably bound up with Rama. Although Rama is considered the ideal king, he is not a very good husband to Sita — yet throughout all this she remains steadfast and usually tries to blame herself instead of Rama for events that cause her suffering. A common Hindu belief is that a man is strengthened, indeed made nearly invulnerable, by his wife's chastity, whereas he is weakened and endangered by her faithlessness.

In inculcating the nature of the ideal woman in India, Sita plays an important role, perhaps the dominant role of all Hindu mythological figures. The Ramayana is well known by almost every Hindu. Sita represents all the qualities of a good woman and ideal wife. She is by far the most popular and beloved paradigm for wifely devotion, forbearance, and chastity:

From earliest childhood, a Hindu has heard Sita's legend recounted on any number of sacral and secular occasions; seen the central episodes enacted in folk plays like the Ram Lila; heard her qualities extolled in devotional songs; and absorbed the ideal feminine identity she incorporates through the many everyday metaphors and similes that are associated with her name. Thus, "She is as pure as Sita" denotes chastity in a woman, and "She is a second Sita," the appreciation of a woman's uncomplaining self-sacrifice. (Kinsley, Hindu Goddesses)

Ideal Devotee and Intermediary

After Valmiki's Ramayana, Rama increasingly ascended to a position of supreme deity for many Hindus. Today in India he is one of the most popular deities and is the recipient of fervent devotion from millions of devotees. The shift in Rama's status from that of a human hero or incarnation of Vishnu in Valmiki's Ramayana to that of the lord of the worlds is most evident in the sixteenth-century Hindi work of Tulsi Das, the Ramcarit-manas, an extremely popular devotional work in North India. Throughout that text it is clear that the central point of the narrative is Rama's descent to earth in order to provide his devotees a chance to worship him.

In the process of Rama's elevation to divine supremacy, Sita's status was similarly elevated. In his poem Kavitavali, Tulsi Das refers to Sita as the world's mother and to Rama as the world's father (1.15). Elsewhere in the poem, Rama and Sita are praised in fervent devotional language by village women who see them walking along the road, and the very sight of them together has redemptive effects (2.14–25; 7.36). In his invocation to the Ramcarit-manas, Tulsi Das includes this verse to Sita: "Hail to Rama's own beloved Sita, victor o'er all suff'ring, / Mistress of birth, life, death, and of all happiness the giver." In the popular folk dramas of North India, the Ram Lilas, actors playing the roles of Rama and Sita are worshiped by the spectators as deities.

Nevertheless, consistent with her role in Valmiki's Ramayana as the ideal wife who subordinates herself to her husband, Sita never achieves the position of a great, powerful, independent deity. Even compared to such goddesses as Lakshmi and Parvati — who in most respects are portrayed as ideal wives in Hindu mythology — Sita lacks an identity, power, and will of her own. She remains in Rama's shadow to such an extent that she is often hardly visible at all. Sita is rarely mentioned in such devotional works as Tulsi Das's Kavitavali and Vinaya-patrika; Hanuman and Lakshmana are mentioned more often than she is. When Rama's consort is specified, Tulsi Das often prefers to identify her as Lakshmi rather than Sita.

In popular Hinduism today Sita is revered as a deity, and in the numerous Ram Lila performances throughout the Hindi-speaking area of North India the actor who plays Sita is worshiped as a deity. But Sita is rarely worshiped in her own right. It would be very unusual to find a temple dedicated to Sita alone. In Rama and Hanuman temples, an image of Sita is installed alongside or between Rama and Lakshmana, where she receives worship along with her husband and brother-in-law — though it is understood that she is not Rama's equal.

Sita does, however, play two important roles in the context of devotion to Rama: the role of intermediary and the role of ideal devotee. Addressing her as world mother, Tulsi Das petitions Sita to act as his advocate before Rama. She is not approached directly for divine blessing but as one who has access to Rama, who alone dispenses divine grace. Consistent with her subordinate position in the Ramayana — always subordinating her will to Rama's — Sita here acts primarily as a messenger between Rama and his devotees. In her loyalty and devotion she has gained the Lord's ear, and because of this she is sometimes approached by his devotees for help in seeking Rama's favor.

Sita also assumes the role of devotee in the later Rama cult, and thus becomes a model for Rama's human devotees. In the Ramcarit-manas she is typically pictured as intoxicated by the appearance of Rama and steadfastly devoted to him. Tulsi Das has sometimes altered the narrative specifically to emphasize Sita's devotion and love: whereas in the Valmiki Ramayana Sita pleads to accompany Rama to the forest by appealing to law and custom (a wife's duty is to be with her husband), in the Ramcarit-manas her plea rests not on social norm but on the unbearable agony that separation from Rama would inflict — her love and devotion give her plea its force and passion rather than her sense of duty.

Sita's role as devotee, like her role as intermediary, casts her in a subsidiary position vis-a-vis Rama. He is the supreme deity, the object of devotion; she is the ideal devotee, the model for the human devotee. Wifely devotion has here become a metaphor for ideal devotion to God.

Additional Notes

Prior to the Ramayana, Sita was a goddess of fertility and abundance, daughter of the Earth goddess Bhudevi. In some versions of her story, she kills a thousand-headed Rakshasa before demanding, for the second time, that the Earth swallow her.
