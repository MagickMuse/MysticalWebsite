Magickal Properties

Chinnamasta is a Tantric wisdom goddess whose unforgettable image shows her standing headless atop a copulating couple, holding her severed head while blood streams nourish herself and two attending dakinis. This represents the eternal cosmic process of creation, preservation, and destruction, the interdependence of sex, love, life, and death.

Ritual Uses

Chinnamasta serves as a vehicle of transformation and fulfills the desires of her devotees. Advanced practitioners meditate upon her image to understand the paradoxes of existence. She is associated with a powerful yantra used for magickal and spiritual work.

Offerings

Red hibiscus flowers (red jaba flowers), offerings that honor self-sacrifice and spiritual transformation, items acknowledging the life-death cycle.

Favored People

Tantric practitioners and advanced spiritual seekers, those ready for profound transformation through understanding of sacrifice, people seeking to comprehend the paradoxes of existence.

Petition For

Fulfillment of deep spiritual desires, guidance through major life transformations, understanding of the cosmic cycles of creation and destruction, tantric wisdom and advanced spiritual insights.

Warnings

Her image expresses profound spiritual concepts that may require lifetime study. She represents the paradox of the loving mother as killer, embodying both creation and destruction. Approach with understanding of her symbolic rather than literal nature.

As a Mahavidya

Chinnamasta is one of the ten Mahavidyas and among the three (with Kali and Tara) who are important individual goddesses beyond their role in the group. The Guhyatiguhya-tantra identifies Chinnamasta with the Vishnu avatara Narasimha: both burst into existence through acts of extreme, violent self-transformation that solve a crisis by shocking force.

Within the Mahavidya origin myth, Chinnamasta appears as one of the ten fearsome forms Sati assumes to surround and terrify Shiva. The Mahavidyas assert the Devi's autonomous power and supremacy over the male gods. Chinnamasta contributes the most extreme iconography of the group: the self-decapitated goddess who bleeds to nourish others, simultaneously destroyer and sustainer, the literal image of the paradox at the heart of Mahavidya theology.

Her iconography is precise: she stands in a cremation ground on the copulating bodies of Kama and Rati (the god of sexual lust and his wife; some texts substitute Radha and Krishna). She holds a sword in one hand and a platter bearing her severed head in the other. Three jets of blood spurt from her neck and stream into the mouths of two female attendants and into the mouth of her own severed head.

The Buddhist Vajrayogini

Chinnamasta is also significant in Tantric and Tibetan Buddhism, where her headless form is known as Vajrayogini. In Buddhist Tantric description she carries her own severed head in her raised left hand while her lowered right hand holds the kartri, a curved chopping knife. She is nude, her right leg stretched and her left bent. Streams of blood issue from her severed body into the mouth of her own head and into the mouths of the two yoginis flanking her. The two attendants likewise hold the kartri, stand with disheveled hair and one leg stretched and one bent, and are set against the backdrop of the cremation ground. This Buddhist form corresponds closely to the Hindu icon, and the two are counterparts across the traditions.

Life, Sex, and Death

Chinnamasta is perhaps the most dramatic image in the Hindu pantheon of the truth that life, sex, and death form a single interdependent system. The startling reversals of her iconography, the self-decapitation, the copulating couple, and the cremation ground arranged in a strangely harmonious pattern, jolt the viewer into recognizing that life feeds on death and that the ultimate purpose of sex is to generate more life, which in turn decays and dies to feed further life. The lotus and the copulating Kama and Rati beneath her supply a powerful life force. Standing on the back of the woman, the goddess draws their vital, life-affirming energy upward into herself. At the top, like an overflowing fountain, her blood spurts from her severed neck and streams into the mouths of her devotees and into her own mouth, nourishing and sustaining them. The cycle is laid bare: life in the lovemaking couple, death in the decapitated goddess, and nourishment in the attendants who drink her blood.

A Reversal of the Blood-Hungry Goddess

Most fierce goddesses, such as Kali and Durga, sever the heads of others and demand blood as nourishment. Offering one's own head to such a goddess is a way of acknowledging that life received from her must be returned, and that she remains ever hungry for blood. Chinnamasta reverses this pattern yet teaches the same truth. She takes her own head rather than another's, she is sustained by the copulating couple rather than by death, and she feeds her devotees with her own blood rather than feeding on theirs. She represents the alternate phase of a single recurring sequence. The cosmic economy is a harmonious alternation of giving and taking, of life and death. She draws vigor from the couple, then gives it away lavishly by cutting off her own head, mirroring a world in which life is sustained only by consuming other life.

Origins and Possible Prototypes

The headless image of Chinnamasta, like the grouping of the ten Mahavidyas, does not appear to be ancient. The form is no earlier than roughly the twelfth century, and the Hindu list of the ten Mahavidyas is more recent still. Several earlier prototypes have been proposed. Ancient nude goddess figures found in India, squatting or with thighs spread to display the sexual organs, arms raised, and deliberately headless or faceless, may be distant ancestors of the image, though they emphasize sexuality and physiology rather than the shocking self-sacrifice that defines Chinnamasta. Two fierce goddesses have also been suggested as forerunners. Kotavi is a naked, disheveled goddess of the battlefield, sometimes counted among the Matrkas and sometimes an opponent of Vishnu, who in one myth protects the demon Banasura, said to be her son; her character is wild and usually malevolent. Korravai is a fierce, bloodthirsty hunting goddess of the ancient Dravidian peoples of South India, invoked for success in cattle raids and propitiated with blood sacrifices, primarily a goddess of war and victory. Neither closely resembles Chinnamasta, who remains unique as the goddess who decapitates herself to nourish her devotees rather than receiving the blood of others.

Worship

Although Chinnamasta is easily recognized and her image is common in goddess temples, she is not widely worshiped. She is almost always tied to heroic Tantric practice. Temples dedicated to her are rare. One stands in the courtyard of a large Durga temple at Ramnagar, across the Ganges from Banaras, established by a Tantric ascetic, where her worshipers are chiefly Tantrics pursuing siddhis, the supernatural powers.

Additional Notes

Chinnamasta is a great yogini whose consort is Headless Shiva. She appears with sword and bowl of blood, associated with the color red and the cobra. Her image is emblematic of self-sacrifice and represents the eternal dance of life and death energies.
