Overview

Chac is the rain god of the Yucatec Maya of the classical Mesoamerican period, worshipped with great local devotion though standing outside the formal hierarchy of the Mayan divine order. His worship belongs to the intimate and immediate register of the agricultural community, the community of the milpa, the small agricultural holding whose produce depends entirely on rain, rather than to the elevated celestial theology of the great Mayan temples. The tradition holds two distinct forms of Chac. The older form is a single god of immense size who taught agriculture to humanity and was understood as the governing power of thunder, lightning, rain, bread, and the milpa and its produce. This older singular Chac is also known as God B in the academic coding of the Mayan codices. The later development recognizes four Chacs, one for each of the four cardinal directions, each distinguished by a color: red for the east, white for the north, black for the west, and yellow for the south.

Classification

Rain god of the Yucatec Maya. Honored locally rather than as a formal member of the Mayan divine hierarchy. The four directional Chacs are the later elaboration of an originally singular deity.

Domains

Rain and the gift of water to the earth, thunder and lightning in their life-giving agricultural role, the milpa or smallholding and its produce, bread and the sustenance of the community, the four directions of the wind that carry rain, music and rhythm in the ritual context, agriculture as taught by the original Chaac to humanity.

Iconography

The four Chacs are known popularly as the Ah Hoyaob, the sprinklers or urinators, because in Mayan cosmological imagery the rain falls from between the legs of these sky-gods, an image that expresses with directness and humor the intimate connection between the divine body and the agricultural earth. Their attributes include a long pendulous nose, a scroll or curl beneath the eye, and a thin ribbon-like object projecting from a corner of the toothless mouth. They may hold burning torches, an attribute that expresses the paradox at the heart of the rain deity: the same god who sends rain can also withhold it and cause drought, and the torch is the sign of that power over drought as well as flood.

Attributes

Long pendulous nose, scroll beneath the eye, ribbon-like mouth ornament, burning torch. The original single Chaac carries the attributes of the thunder-lord.

Sacred Animals

Frogs and tortoises.

Sacred Sites

Chac was not part of the formal temple hierarchy of the Mayan divine order but was honored at local level throughout the Yucatan Peninsula and across the classical Mayan world. The rain-calling ceremonies of the Mayan agricultural communities were addressed to him directly in the milpa and at local shrines rather than at the great temple complexes.

Ritual

The Chacs are regarded as musicians, and music is part of the ritual address to them. The community's rain-calling ceremonies, still practiced in Maya communities of the Yucatan in modified form into the modern period, involve communal prayer, offerings of food and ritual drink, and the invocation of the four directional Chacs by their colors and their directions. These ceremonies were and remain among the most important of the Mayan agricultural year.

Lore

Chac's standing outside the formal divine hierarchy while commanding intense local devotion expresses a theological pattern characteristic of many agricultural religions, in which the high gods of the official cult are matched by an equally powerful but more intimate deity of the rain and the field worshipped directly by the farming community without requiring priestly mediation. In this pattern, the Chacs belong to the people of the milpa in a way that the great celestial gods do not, and the frogs and tortoises sacred to them are the animals of the raining season itself, the creatures that announce and celebrate the rain the community is calling for. For comparison with a rain deity from another Mesoamerican tradition, see also Tlaloc of the Aztec religion.
