Magickal Properties

The Eight Immortals are Taoist spirits who, though each also venerated separately, are venerated together as a group to reinforce each other and provide even greater inspiration, wisdom, healing, and protection. Allegedly historic people who achieved immortality through mastery of Taoist alchemy, some are even documented.

Ritual Uses

They offer instruction to those deemed worthy and ready to receive it. They offer help and protection to the needy and desperate. Symbolic of good fortune, their images are ubiquitous.

Offerings

Traditional offerings appropriate for Taoist spirits and Chinese folk religion practices.

Favored People

Those seeking instruction, protection, healing, and good fortune are traditionally under the patronage of the Eight Immortals.

Petition For

Inspiration, wisdom, instruction in Taoist arts, healing, protection, and auspicious good fortune.

Warnings

Group veneration reinforces the power of each member. Treat the Eight Immortals as folk-Taoist adepts and cultural icons rather than literal historical figures. Honor cultural context and avoid careless syncretism.

Additional Notes

There are at least one thousand Immortals, but the ones classified among the eight are the most popular and widely venerated. The standard group includes Cao Guo-Jiu, Chang Kuo-Lao, Chung-li Ch'uan, Han Hsiang-Tzu, Ho Hsien-Ko, Lan Ts'ai Ho, Li Tieh-Kuai, and Lu Tong-Pin. They are often depicted together, crossing the sea in a boat. In addition to individual shrines, they are venerated together in Taiwan's Temple of the Eight Immortals. They are sometimes represented by their attributes: sword, fan, flower basket, lotus, flute, gourd, castanets, and fish drum. Chinese religious tradition holds many groups of immortals, and the Ba Xian rank among the most widely revered. Devotees commonly carry amulets and charms fashioned in the form of the Immortals' distinctive symbols for personal protection and blessing.

The Eight

Strictly speaking the Pa-Hsien are not Gods but have attained God-like status through their adherence to Taoist principles. Whether they were real people remains uncertain; what is clear is that they function as spiritual role models, and are often depicted alongside the God of Long Life.

  1. Han Chung-li: A very old man of reckless appearance. He instructed Ts'ao Kuo-chiu, one of the other Immortals.
  2. Ts'ao Kuo-chiu: A pupil of Han Chung-li who does not follow his master's dress sense, appearing instead dressed as a mandarin. He was later helped to convert by Lu Tung-pin.
  3. Chang-kuo Lao: Little is known about this old man, though his donkey appears equally worthy of veneration. The animal could travel colossal distances in a single day and, when no longer needed, could be folded up like a piece of paper.
  4. Lan Ts'ai-ho: Recognizable by one bare foot and one shod. A street-singer who wears tattered clothes, he was reportedly carried up to Heaven by a stork.
  5. T'ieh-kuai Li: A pupil of the sixth-century BCE philosopher-poet Lao-Tzu. When T'ieh-kuai Li was due to join his master in Heaven, only his soul departed, leaving his body behind. He instructed his pupil to guard the body for seven days; if his soul had not returned to reanimate it by then, the body was to be burned. On the sixth day the pupil's mother fell ill and, forced to leave, he burned the body a day early. Li's soul returned the following day to find nothing but smoldering ashes. He quickly took up residence in the nearest available corpse, that of a beggar who had died of starvation, and so appears as a beggar leaning on an iron crutch with a large cooking-pot on his back.
  6. Ho-Sien Ku: Ascended to Heaven in full daylight. She appears as a beautiful young woman holding a lotus flower.
  7. Han Hsiang-tzu: Appears as a young man dressed in the clothes and headgear of a noble.
  8. Lu Tung-pin: The Immortal about whom most is known. He wanders the world in the guise of an ordinary person, rewarding the good and punishing the bad. In the story known as "The Dream of the Yellow Sorghum," Lu stopped at an inn while still a student and met one of the Immortals in disguise. After their conversation he fell into a deep sleep and dreamed of his entire life laid out before him: many successes and accolades, but in the end, dying a pauper killed by a bandit. On waking, he renounced worldly life and embraced the Taoist faith.
