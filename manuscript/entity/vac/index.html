Overview

Vac is the Hindu Vedic goddess of speech. Her name is the word for speech itself, and in the Rg-veda she shows herself through the act of speaking. Her character is the character of speech in all its uses, sacred and ordinary alike. She is the numinous power inside the mysteries and miracles of language: the prompt that inspires the seer, the formula that powers the ritual, and the daily word that lets one friend recognize another. By the time of the Brahmanas she has grown into a creator goddess in her own right, paired with Prajapati at the very beginning of the world. The Brahmanas also consistently identify her with Sarasvati, so the two goddesses share a great deal of their later mythic territory.

In Vedic literature

Speech as goddess

Although the idea that sound and speech are the primal stuff of creation belongs mostly to the post-Rg-vedic layers of the tradition, even the Rg-veda already treats sound, and especially ritual speech, as a creative force and as one of the supports of the joined cosmic and ritual order. Vac is the goddess whose entire being is identified with that force. She is not so much a deity who governs speech from outside; she is speech.

Inspirer of the seers and bestower of vision

Vac is the presence that descends on the rsis, the seer-poets, and that confers Brahmanhood upon a person. She is named truth, and she sustains truth by sustaining Soma, the personification of the intoxicating draught through which vision and deathlessness are tasted. She is the mysterious power that allows a person first to hear, then to see, then to grasp, and finally to articulate the real nature of what is. In that sense she is both the prompt for visionary perception and the vehicle through which the vision can be expressed, and so she is closely bound up with the rsis themselves and with the sacrificial rituals that capture and re-deploy their insights. To call her an essential part of the religious-poetic visionary experience, and of the cult that takes those visions and preserves them, is not an overstatement.

Heavenly queen and bounteous cow

Probably because of her role in granting vision, Vac is honored as a heavenly queen and named the queen of the gods. The hymns call her the one who streams with sweetness and the one who confers vital powers. She is described as a courtly, elegant woman, radiant and decked in gold. Like most Vedic goddesses, she is fundamentally a benevolent, generous presence. She does not only hand out the special riches of language to her devotees; she is also praised more broadly for granting light and strength, and one hymn even claims that she alone supplies people with food. That places her beyond a mere personified abstraction. She is a deity who saturates the world with her sustaining power, quickening the growth of living things just as she quickens language and vision, and the hymns repeatedly figure her as a heavenly cow whose milk feeds gods and humans alike.

Mother of names

She is called mother, and the reason given is striking: she has brought things into being by naming them. Her gentleness also lets people see and recognize one another. When two people share intelligible, familiar speech, each carries her mark, and that shared mark is what makes friendship possible. Vac is therefore a bounteous cow whose milk yields three streams: the soaring discerning vision of the rsi, the prescribed ritual chants of the priest, and the everyday speech of ordinary people that lets them gather themselves into a fellowship of friends.

Vac in the Brahmanas: creation and ritual

Her character grows much more elaborate in the Brahmanas, which assemble around her a tight cluster of creation and ritual myths. Because the divine cult depends on spoken and chanted mantras, the Brahmanas emphasize her absolute indispensability to it: there are stories of how the gods, after the world was made, stole her or seduced her away from the demons, and only once they possessed her could they institute the sacrificial rituals that keep creation running and produce bounty, life, and immortality for the gods themselves. Without her, divine ritual would not exist. As a creator she is said to bring forth the three Vedas, and the three Vedas are then equated with the earth (Rg-veda), the air (Yajur-veda), and the sky (Sama-veda). Elsewhere she is said to have entered the sap of plants and trees, threading herself through and enlivening all vegetation. Prajapati, the great deity of the Brahmanas, is described as beginning creation by impregnating himself through the comingling of his mind and his speech. In another telling, Vac, brought into existence by Prajapati's mind, longed to manifest, to multiply, and to extend herself, and the unfolding of creation followed from that longing of hers.

Coeternal with Prajapati

Vac matters in Vedic literature not only by frequency of mention but theologically. The Brahmanas suggest that she is coeternal with Prajapati, even though the texts are not consistent and sometimes say she was created by him. Either way she holds an exalted theological position. There are hints that Prajapati creates through her or in pairing with her, and that distinguishes her from the later Hindu philosophical notion of sakti, in which the male counterpart tends to be passive: here Prajapati labors and desires the creation actively, even as Vac acts alongside him. Still, her role in the Brahmanas anticipates the later sakti idea. It also points forward to the doctrine of sabda-brahman, the absolute in the form of sound, and to the sphota theory of creation, in which the world comes into being through sound.

Fate in later Hinduism

Vac herself fades out of view in the later Hindu tradition as a named goddess. Her primary meaning, the divine power of inspired speech, is carried forward most clearly by Sarasvati, whom the Brahmanas already identify with her. The post-Vedic notion that the world comes into being through sound is also widely understood to draw on the Vedic conception of Vac. In that sense she does not so much vanish as become absorbed: the goddess Sarasvati and the doctrine of creative sound together preserve what was hers.
