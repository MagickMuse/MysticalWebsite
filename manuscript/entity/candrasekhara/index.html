Overview

Candrasekhara is a form of the god Siva in the Hindu Puranic tradition, whose name means the one whose crest is the moon, from candra meaning moon and sekhara meaning crest or crown. He is the composed and sovereign form of Siva in which the crescent moon rests within his piled ascetic hair as a visible sign of the god's mastery over time, the tides, and the cool wisdom that balances the fierce heat of his ordinary tapas. This form of Siva is one of the standard iconographic types of the god in Puranic sculpture, and it appears widely across south Indian temple imagery.

Classification

Form of the god Siva in the Hindu Puranic tradition. Composed sovereign aspect of Siva as the moon-crested god.

Domains

Sovereign composure of Siva, the reconciliation of the god's fierce and calm powers within a single steady posture, mastery over time as measured by the phases of the moon, the wisdom of the ascetic who has drawn the celestial into his own hair.

Iconography

Portrayed standing stiffly upright, in the samabhanga posture without the flexed hip and turning shoulders of the dancing forms of Siva. He wears the ascetic's piled hair on his crown, and set into that hair on the left side of his headdress is the crescent moon, which is the sign from which his name is taken. He wears snake jewelry: a snake as sacred thread across his chest, snakes as bracelets and armlets, and often a snake coiled at his throat. His face is composed and meditative rather than fierce or dancing.

Attributes

Ax and antelope. The ax is the parasu, the standard emblem of Siva as the god who cuts through the roots of ignorance, and the antelope is the mrga, the wilderness creature whose leaping motion Siva has taken into his own hand as a sign of his mastery over the restless mind and the wandering senses.

Colors

The standard white or ash color of Siva, the pale color of the moon at his crest, and the blue-black of his throat where he swallowed the halahala poison.

Sacred Sites

Saiva temples across south India in particular, where the Candrasekhara form is one of the standard sculptural types carried in the temple's iconographic program alongside the dancing Nataraja, the seated Daksinamurti, and the destroying Bhairava forms of Siva.

Ritual

Honored in the ordinary rhythm of Saiva temple worship and in the meditative practice of the devotee who takes the composed form of Siva as the object of contemplation. His iconography teaches through its stillness, and his worship draws its particular character from the composed and upright quality that distinguishes him from the dancing and destroying forms of the same god.

Lore

The crescent moon on Siva's crest belongs to the wider mythology in which the god received the moon into his hair as a refuge for it during the churning of the ocean of milk, when the moon emerged and needed a safe place within the cosmos. Siva took the crescent into his ascetic hair, and from that time the moon has traveled with him as a sign of his sovereignty over time and tides. The Candrasekhara form crystallizes this mythic gesture into a standing iconographic type, so that the devotee who looks upon this image sees at once the ascetic Siva of the mountain and the cosmic Siva who holds the moon in his own hair.
