Overview

Sarasvati is the wife of Brahma, the creator aspect of the divine. In her earliest incarnations, she was a river spirit with dominion over fertility, procreation, and purification. She retains those powers but has evolved into a matron of literature and wisdom, too. Sarasvati sponsors learning and creative sciences. She is the goddess of music and credited with inventing Sanskrit. A generous, bountiful spirit, she places the fetus in the womb and is the giver of all beautiful things. Sarasvati is invoked frequently in the Rig Veda, the oldest surviving Indian writings, sacred hymns composed approximately 1200 BCE. She was born from a golden egg from the sea.

Sarasvati's answers to petitions may come in the form of artistic inspiration.

In Vedic literature

A river that is also a goddess

The Vedic habit of binding a goddess to a natural phenomenon, already visible in Usas and Prthivi, runs through Sarasvati as well, whose attachment is to one particular mighty river. Which actual stream the Vedic poets had in mind is no longer recoverable; the historical Sarasvati River has long since dried out of the landscape. What survives in the Rg-veda are descriptions whose most prominent features are those of a powerful watercourse, and the hymns slip so easily between the river and the deity that one cannot always tell which is being honored. Many verses simply hail the Sarasvati River itself as a mighty goddess.

Mighty and inexhaustible

She is named mighty and powerful. Her waves are said to grind down mountains, and her floodwaters are described as roaring. She is said to outdo every other water in greatness, to be ceaselessly active, and to stand as the greatest among the great. The hymns also call her inexhaustible because her source lies in the celestial ocean. She is plainly not a mere river but a stream sent down from the sky to bless the earth, and she is said to spread across the threefold creation of earth, atmosphere, and the celestial heights.

Bountiful mother

She is praised for the fertility she brings to the soil. Petitioners approach her for wealth, vitality, children, nourishment, and immortality, and on that account she carries the epithet subhaga, the bountiful one. Her abundance makes her a mother, and not just any mother but the best of mothers. As a nurturing maternal presence she is described in terms that overlap closely with Prthivi: she quickens life, is the wellspring of vigor and strength, and brings good fortune and material well-being to those she favors. One hymn has unmarried men longing for sons calling on her directly; they ask to drink from her breast that is heavy with streams and to receive food and offspring from her hand. She is also petitioned for protection, and in that role she takes on images that have nothing to do with water at all: she is named a sheltering tree and an iron fort.

Guardian of the cult

Sarasvati is tightly woven into Vedic ritual life, both as a participant or witness and as a protector of the cult itself. She is invoked beside the sacrificial goddesses Ida and Bharati, and beside Mahi and Hotra, who carry the work of prayer. She is said to destroy those who slander the gods and is named a slayer of Vrtra, the demon of chaos.

Purifier

She is described as a fundamentally purifying presence. Her waters draw poison out of human bodies. Along with rivers and floodwaters more generally, she cleanses her petitioners with holy oil and carries away defilements.

Forerunner of inspiration and speech

Anticipating the later goddess of inspiration, eloquence, and learning who will become central to Hindu devotion, the Rg-vedic hymns already describe Sarasvati as the inciter of every pleasant song, every gracious thought, and every pious thought. In this office she stands close to the Vedic goddess Vac, the personified power of speech, with whom the Brahmanas consistently identify her.

Sarasvati as a River

Sarasvati as the embodiment of the Sarasvati River is significant in both a historical and a theological sense. The religion of the Vedic Aryans was primarily a portable religion centered around a fire cult that did not require permanent temples or places of worship. The domestic hearth itself was a center of worship. By and large Vedic religion was appropriate for a nomadic people or for a people who had only recently ceased to be nomadic. The Aryans of the Vedas migrated into Northwest India sometime during the second millennium BCE and gradually spread throughout the subcontinent over many generations. The reverence given to Sarasvati as the embodiment of a river in Northwest India is important because it indicates that the Aryans had begun to identify their culture with a specific geographical location and were beginning to settle into a non-nomadic way of life.

The transition from a nomadic to an agricultural, village culture is central in the transition from the religion of the Vedic Aryans to classical Hinduism. In classical Hinduism India herself is affirmed to be the center of the world, the navel of the earth, the special and sacred location of the divine — a quality specified in the sacrality of the subcontinent's major rivers. Sarasvati represents a very early example of this tendency in the Hindu tradition toward affirming the land itself as holy, and the river goddess of the Vedas is a prototype of such important later river goddesses as Ganga and Yamuna.

Two associations are especially important in Vedic descriptions of Sarasvati as river. First, she is said to bestow bounty, fertility, and riches. Her waters enrich the land so that it can produce; they represent life itself in a dry environment, which Northwest India may already have been at the time of the Aryan migrations. Second, Sarasvati represents purity, as does water and particularly running water. Though this characteristic is rarely mentioned directly, the Vedas frequently record that rituals were performed on the banks of the Sarasvati, which were held to be especially sacred for ritual purposes, pointing to the river's purifying power.

Sarasvati's purifying power in the Vedic texts is also suggested in her association with medicine and healing. In the Satapatha-brahmana in particular she is called upon to heal sickness and is referred to as a healing medicine. In the Rg-veda she and the Ashvins, twin gods associated with healing, are said to heal the god Indra. As a divine physician, Sarasvati is petitioned to cleanse the petitioner of disease.

A particularly Indian association with rivers is the imagery of crossing from the world of ignorance or bondage to the far shore, which represents enlightenment or freedom. The religious quest in all three native Indian religions — Hinduism, Jainism, and Buddhism — is expressed by the metaphor of fording or crossing a wide stream. The river represents a great purifying power in which the pilgrim drowns the old self and is born anew, free and enlightened. This imagery is not expressly applied to Sarasvati in the Vedas, but it may have been understood implicitly and may help to explain her later association with inspiration, speech, and wisdom.

Although Sarasvati's nature is overwhelmingly associated with a mighty river, this is no ordinary river. Early Vedic references make clear that the Sarasvati River originates in heaven and flows down to the earth — an assertion of sacred nature also affirmed later of the Ganga. The Sarasvati represents an ever-flowing stream of celestial grace that purifies and fertilizes the earth. Her earthly manifestation as a river thus represents only a partial disclosure of her being. Physical contact with that earthly manifestation, however, connects one with the awesome, heavenly, transcendent dimension of the goddess and of reality in general.

Sarasvati in Later Hinduism

Sarasvati's connection with a river steadily decreases in later Hinduism. As early as the Brahmanas she is consistently identified with Vagdevi, the goddess of speech. What intrinsic connection between Sarasvati and Vagdevi led to this association is not entirely clear. Perhaps the centrality of sacred speech in Vedic cult and the importance of Vedic rituals being performed on the banks of the Sarasvati River led to the identification of the two goddesses. In any case, Sarasvati increasingly becomes a goddess associated with speech, learning, culture, and wisdom; most post-Vedic references to her do not even hint that she was once identified with a river.

In later Hinduism Sarasvati is sometimes said to have been born from the god Brahma. Brahma, desiring to create the world, goes into meditation, whereupon his body divides in two, half male and half female. Enraptured by his female half, who is Sarasvati, Brahma desires her, mates with her, and creates the demigod Manu, who subsequently creates the world. A similar version of her origin is found in the Brahma-vaivarta-purana and the Devi-bhagavata-purana. According to these texts, Krishna, identified with absolute reality (brahman), divides himself into male and female, purusa and prakrti, spirit and matter, in order to proceed with creation. His female half takes on five forms or five shaktis, dynamic powers, one of which is Sarasvati. Her specific creative function in relation to the other shaktis is to pervade reality with insight, knowledge, and learning. In relation to prakrti she is said to be purely sattvic, spiritual. These same texts also describe Sarasvati's origin from the tip of Krishna's shakti's tongue: suddenly a lovely girl appears dressed in yellow clothes, adorned with jewels, and carrying a book and a vina (lute). Sarasvati is also often said to have her origin in and to reside in the mouths or on the tongues of the god Brahma, who has four or five heads. That is, when Brahma undertakes the creation of the world through creative speech, the goddess Sarasvati is born in his mouths.

Sarasvati is also said to have had her origin from the god Vishnu. In several places she is said to be his tongue or to be held in his mouth. Her association with Vishnu makes her the co-wife of Lakshmi in many myths. In this relationship Sarasvati for the most part represents spiritual, ascetic, or religious goals and values, whereas Lakshmi represents worldly well-being as manifest in wealth, material power, and fertility. In some texts the two goddesses do not get along well, suggesting a tension between bhukti (sensual enjoyment) or dharma and mukti (spiritual liberation or perfection) in Hinduism.

Although Sarasvati's nature and appearance change dramatically from the Vedic period to later Hinduism, she does maintain some characteristics of her earlier history. She is associated with clouds, thunder, and rain and is said to be the presiding deity of rain. In the Vamana-purana she is described as moving through the clouds and producing rain, and that same text identifies her with all waters. Her association with Soma in some texts and with water in general suggests that Sarasvati is identified with the underlying sap of vitality necessary for all living things. These continued associations with water indicate a certain continuity between the river goddess of the Vedas and the later goddess. Even so, Sarasvati's character is much changed: in the later tradition she is not so much a river goddess as a goddess who manifests herself through the life-giving and purifying nature of all water — rain, rivers, ponds, and so on. Like Soma she pervades creation; she has transcended her association with any specific river.

Goddess of Speech

Far more characteristic of the later Sarasvati is her association with speech. Even in the Rg-veda she is called impeller of true and sweet speech and awakener of happy and noble thoughts (6.61.9). Such epithets as Vagdevi (goddess of speech), Jihvagravasini (dwelling in the front of the tongue), Kavijihvagravasini (she who dwells on the tongues of poets), Shabdavasini (she who dwells in sound), Vagisha (mistress of speech), and Mahavani (possessing great speech) are often used for Sarasvati. Her mythological identification with the tongues of Brahma, Krishna, and Vishnu also underlines her identification with speech and creative sound.

The importance of speech in Hinduism is both ancient and central. The entire creative process is held to be distilled in the syllable om, and the idea of creation proceeding from shabda-brahman (ultimate reality in the form of sound) is often mentioned in Hindu texts. The potency of speech and sound is also seen in the centrality of mantras: a mantra, which may consist of words or of sounds alone, is held to possess great power. The mantra of a given deity is declared to be equivalent to the deity itself — to pronounce a mantra is to make the deity present. There resides in sound a potent quality, and this quality is embodied in the goddess Sarasvati.

Speech is also revered because it permits communication between people and is associated with rationality and refinement. It represents coherent sound that permits the transmission of ideas, wisdom, and culture. As the embodiment of speech, Sarasvati is present wherever speech exists — preeminently associated with the best in human culture: poetry, literature, sacred rituals, and rational communication.

Goddess of Intellect and Learning

Sarasvati is also identified with thought and intellect. Not only is she speech in the form of coherent sound, she is that which underlies or makes speech possible: intelligence and thought. This association is indicated in such epithets as Smrtisakti (the power of memory), Jnanasakti (the power of knowledge), Buddhisaktisvarupini (whose form is the power of intellect), Kalpanasakti (who is the power of forming ideas), and Pratibha (intelligence, or she who is intelligence). As thought and intellect, Sarasvati is identified with the distinctive ability that sets human beings apart: reasoning. She represents the peculiar human ability to think, which has permitted human beings to create and imagine their innumerable cultural products, from cooking pots to philosophic systems.

Sarasvati's association with science, learning, and knowledge further reinforces her nature as the goddess of speech and thought. She is called Vedagarbha (the womb or source of the Vedas), Sarvavidyasvarupini (whose form is all the sciences), Sarvashastravasini (who dwells in all books), and Granthakarini (who causes books to be made), among many other such names. As mind, intellect, and thought, she inspires the arts and sciences and is also the accumulated products of human thought — the sum of the human intellectual tradition as preserved in the sciences. As the goddess who bears culture, she is sometimes associated with the Brahmins, whose special duty is to preserve culture, and she is especially revered in schools and wherever education takes place.

Sarasvati is also said to underlie, inspire, or embody the arts. She is said to provide inspiration to poets and to be present wherever artistic excellence is evident. She is said to be associated with the Gandharvas, a supernatural race that excels at dancing, and she is often associated with music, both instrumental and vocal. Inspiring and embodying both the arts and sciences, she represents the greatness of human civilization in all its richness and diversity.

Beyond her cultural associations, certain cosmic epithets suggest her primordial, absolute nature: Jaganmata (mother of the world), Shaktirupini (whose form is power or shakti), and Vishvarupa (containing all forms within her). As the reality that permits human beings to achieve dominion over all other creatures — that inspires the beauty and grace manifest in the arts, that has enabled human beings to mold and refine the natural world — this goddess of culture comes to be equated with the highest powers of the cosmos.

Iconography

Sarasvati's iconography illustrates her associations with culture, particularly the arts and sciences, and shows her to be a goddess largely set apart from the natural realm of growth, fertility, blood, and other phenomena central in the iconography of other goddesses. She is usually depicted as having four hands, most commonly holding a book, a lute (vina), a rosary, and a water pot. The book associates her with the sciences and learning in general; the lute associates her with the arts, particularly music; the rosary and water pot associate her with the spiritual sciences and religious rites.

The predominant themes in Sarasvati's appearance are purity and transcendence. She is almost always said to be pure white like snow, the moon, or the kunda flower, or to shine brilliantly and whitely like innumerable moons. Her garments are described as fiery in their purity or as white, and she is sometimes said to be smeared with sandalwood paste. This gleaming white appearance expresses her purity and transcendence and is in keeping with her typical association with the sattva guna, the pure, spiritual thread of prakrti. Sarasvati is rarely described as having fearsome aspects and is usually portrayed as calm and peaceful.

Sarasvati's transcendent nature is also suggested in her vehicle, the swan. The swan is a symbol of spiritual transcendence and perfection in Hinduism. Spiritual masters and heroes are sometimes called supreme swans (paramahamsas) in that they have completely transcended the limitations and imperfections of the phenomenal world. Sarasvati astride her swan suggests a dimension of human existence that rises above the physical, natural world — a realm of beauty, perfection, and grace created by artistic inspiration, philosophic insight, and accumulated knowledge. She also is typically shown seated on a lotus. Like the swan, the lotus seat suggests her transcendence of the physical world: she floats above the muddy imperfections of existence, unsullied, pure, beautiful. Although rooted in the mud, the lotus perfects itself in a blossom that has transcended the mud — and Sarasvati inspires people to transcend their physical limitations through the ongoing creation of culture.

Worship and Blessings

The benefits to be derived from the worship of Sarasvati usually relate to her central themes. She gives eloquence, wisdom, poetic inspiration, and artistic skill. She removes speech defects and dumbness and grants charming speech and a musical voice. Although she is sometimes said to grant wealth, long life, worldly enjoyments, and final salvation, she is primarily the goddess of wisdom and learning and specializes in promoting success among philosophers, scholars, and artists, who are her special devotees.

A persistent theme in the Hindu tradition is that human destiny involves the refinement of nature. The arts and sciences are the most obvious and concrete manifestations of this theme: artistic creation and accumulated knowledge epitomize human culture and demonstrate the extraordinary ability of human beings to mold and refine the natural world into something beautiful and specially human. Sarasvati presides over and inspires this dimension of being human. Her pure, milky-white appearance and completely sattvic nature dissociate her from the mud of existence, from the vigorous, fecund sap of fertility. Her sexual encounters are not emphasized, and when her father/husband Brahma desires her, she seeks to flee. Her motherhood is usually only metaphorical: she is said to give birth to artistic creations by providing inspiration, or to have given birth to the Vedas in the sense that she personifies wisdom. In the Devi-bhagavata-purana she is said to be ascetic in nature and to grant boons to those who perform asceticism. Her presence is not usually sought in the home or in the fields. Her presence is sought in libraries and schools, by those who create and bear culture in the ongoing task of transforming the natural world into a refined and civilized habitation for human beings.

Throughout North India today Sarasvati's special puja is celebrated in early spring. On this day images of the goddess are established in schools and universities, and special cultural programs take place. At Benares Hindu University there is a procession of faculty and students on this day, which also corresponds to the anniversary of the university's founding. This is likewise the day when books, pens, musical instruments, and gurus are formally worshiped.

The White Lotus Throne

Sarasvati is depicted seated on a white Lotus blossom.

Manifestation

Sarasvati wears the crescent moon upon her forehead and may be recognized by her dazzling white skin and vivid clothing. Her four arms represent the four aspects of learning: intellect, mind, ego, and alertness.

Iconography

Sarasvati is usually depicted seated upon her lotus blossom throne accompanied by her white swan, the bird that in Hindu mythology is able to distinguish between substance and illusion, truth and falsehood. She may also be venerated in the form of books, inkwells, pens, and musical instruments.

Honey and Perfect Knowledge

Adherents to the Sarasvati tradition make offerings of honey to their Goddess, because honey is held to be synonymous with perfect knowledge.

Additional Notes

Sarasvati is the presiding spirit of the Sarasvati River. The Vasant Panchami is the festival honoring Sarasvati.
