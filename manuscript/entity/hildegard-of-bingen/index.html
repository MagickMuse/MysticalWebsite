Hildegard of Bingen, an unofficial saint also known as the Sibyl of the Rhine, is the patron of clairvoyants, visionaries, musicians, and herbalists. She is renowned for healing migraines and headaches, as well as for her vast compendium of herbal remedies and gemstone therapies.

Life and History

Hildegard of Bingen (1098 to 1179) was the tenth child of noble German parents. Her father was a knight. As the tenth child, she was the tithe owed to God. Her parents promised her to the Church, perhaps also in response to visions she began experiencing at age three. She was an unusual child who unnerved people.

Hildegard became a novice at age eight, sent to live with the holy woman and hermit Jutta of Sponheim. She lived in a small room with one window, receiving two meals a day at 3:00 AM and 3:00 PM. Her time was devoted to prayer, study, embroidery, and fasting. She received comparatively little formal education, incredible given her achievements. She may be understood as the matron of homeschooling, autodidacts, and independent education.

After Jutta's death, Hildegard became prioress in 1136. She also founded two monasteries.

Extraordinary Accomplishments

  • Wrote plays, poems, hymns, mystical works, biographies of saints, and treatises on natural history and medicine
  • Advised popes, kings, emperors, and bishops
  • Corresponded with Emperor Frederick Barbarossa, Saint Bernard of Clairvaux, and many others
  • Preacher when women were expected to stay silent (she said the Holy Spirit compelled her to speak)
  • Healer and expert herbalist specializing in women's medicine
  • Prescribed exercise, rest, gem elixirs, hot mineral baths, proper nutrition, and gemstone therapy
  • Collected and compiled over 2000 remedies
  • Wrote about sacred bonds between humans and nature, a naturalist ahead of her time
  • Brilliant musician and composer, credited with writing the first opera
  • Approximately eighty compositions survive; she wrote chants for female singers

The Visions

Hildegard's visions continued throughout her life, kept secret until she became seriously ill in her late forties. She blamed her illness on suppressing visionary experiences. After consulting the pope, she published the first of her vision collections.

Her visions were experienced while conscious, not in trance. Her confessor advised her to write them down. She painted portraits of herself experiencing them. She literally saw solutions to medical and theological dilemmas, a future where women were honored, biblical figures, angels, demons, and ancient Pagan goddesses. She saw God as possessing male and female aspects.

The Migraines

The descriptions of her visionary sensations correspond to migraine headaches. She suffered debilitating headaches, followed by temporary blindness and paralysis, culminating in euphoria.

Female Warrior

Hildegard considered herself a female warrior. She believed women were more, not less, spiritual than men, revolutionary for her era. When summoned to perform an exorcism (unheard of for a Christian woman), she demanded seven priests serve as witnesses to her success.

Controversy and Cult

Some condemned her as diabolical, but she had friends in high places. Despite visions, revolutionary ideas, and herbal knowledge, she was never accused of witchcraft, though frequently mocked. She was also called a saint in her lifetime. People flocked to request healings and miracles.

Hildegard is often called Saint Hildegard, and many assume she is official. She is not. In 1324, Pope John XXII gave permission for her "public cult." In 1940, the Vatican recognized her sanctity, but canonization never occurred.

Talismanic Letters

People wrote to Hildegard requesting advice. Her letters answering their queries acquired the status of talismans.

In Film

Actress Barbara Sukowa portrays Hildegard in Vision from the Life of Hildegard von Bingen (2009) by director Margarethe von Trotta.

Sacred Site

Originally buried in the convent of Disibodenberg; in 1642 her remains were transferred to Eibergen, Germany.
