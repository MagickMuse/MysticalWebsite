Saint Domenico di Foligno is renowned for healing snake bites, rabies, and toothaches.

Classification

Roman Catholic saint.

Life and History

Saint Domenico (died 1031) was a Benedictine monk who lived briefly in Cocullo, Italy. During his lifetime, he performed miraculous healings, specializing in rabies, snake bites, and toothaches.

When he prepared to leave Cocullo, the distraught townspeople asked who would heal them now. Domenico gave them two relics: one of his own molars and a shoe from Giulia, his mule. The tooth now rests under glass on his altar in Cocullo. Miniature replicas of the mule shoe serve as healing amulets. Ringing the church bell at Cocullo is said to prevent and heal toothaches.

Inheritor of Angitia

Centuries before Domenico, the goddess Angitia ruled this region. Granddaughter of Circe the sorceress, Angitia was a skilled magical practitioner and snake charmer. Snakes, endemic to the area, featured prominently in her rites.

With Christianity's arrival, San Domenico inherited many of Angitia's sacred functions, including healing snake bites and rabies. He also inherited her rituals.

Festa dei Serpari

Angitia presided over an annual spring festival of snake charmers, similar to the one now honoring San Domenico. Snake catchers (serpari) begin capturing local snakes during the vernal equinox. After Mass on the festival day, a votive image of San Domenico is brought outside and draped with live snakes by the serpari.

The serpari carry the serpent-covered statue through Angitia's old territory while Catholic priests provide escort and costumed young women carry snake-shaped cakes. Live snakes are draped around other serpari and devoted participants. After the festival, snakes are released back to the fields.

Additional Powers

Famed for controlling snakes, Domenico by extension combats the greatest serpent: Satan. He exorcises demons and breaks curses and magic spells. He also performs animal-related miracles, once persuading a wolf to return a snatched baby completely unharmed.

Iconography
