Magickal Properties

Title meaning “Lord,” commonly applied to the storm god Hadd; patron of fertile rains, thunder, and protection of cities.

Ritual Uses

Petition for life‑giving rain, agricultural prosperity, and victory against chaos.

Offerings

Bread, beer or wine, first fruits, and vows to maintain just order.

Favored People

Farmers, city guardians, and those facing drought.

Petition For

Timely rain, protection, and successful harvests.

Warnings

Distinguish Ba’al as title vs. specific deity; keep rites dignified.

Additional Notes

Often consort to Anat; venerated across Levantine cities with local epithets.

Domains

Storms, rain, thunder, kingship over chaos, vegetation, and fertility of the land. In older strata Ba'al is a weather deity; in later cult he emerges as a vegetation god concerned with agricultural abundance and the annual return of the rains. His known period of worship extends from at least the nineteenth century BCE through the second century BCE, and he stands as both a national god and a patron of the grain-bearing coastal plain of the eastern Mediterranean.

Iconography

A seated god crowned with bull horns is depicted on a stele from Ras Samra, identified as either Ba'al or El. A model calf discovered at the same site has also been proposed as a representation of him.

Attributes

His voice sounds like thunder. His palace on Mount Sapan contains a hole in the floor through which he waters the earth. His servants take the form of seven pages and eight boars, all of whom carry a fertility function. He sires a bull calf as the guarantee of his power during his absence in the underworld.

Sacred Sites

His principal cult center is Ugarit at Ras Samra and Jebel el Aqra. Ashdod housed his worship during the Philistine period. Additional centers span the eastern Mediterranean coastal plain, including Baal-Hazor, Baal-Sidon, and Baal-Tyre in Lebanon, along with Memphis in Egypt. His mythic residence is the vast and opulent palace on the mountain called Sapan.

Lore

Ba'al is the son of Dagan. He is the father of seven storm gods called the Baalim and seven midwife goddesses called the Sasuratum. He is the brother of the goddess Anat, and the father of the daughters Pidray, daughter of mist, and Tallay, daughter of showers, both of whom carry a fertility function.

Ba'al gained his kingship in primeval times by wresting it, with weapons forged by divine craftsmen, from the powers of chaos embodied in the sea and the river tyrannies and personified in the god Yamm. The theme of a god constantly and energetically engaged with the forces of disorder was first established in ancient Near Eastern religion, and Ba'al became its central figure. The combat causes his temporary ill-fortune, but he emerges triumphant from it each year.

Before descending to the underworld to confront Mot, Ba'al sires a bull calf as the guarantee of his power in his absence. He dies in the confrontation, is restored through the efforts of Anat, and in the seventh year kills Mot, mirroring the agricultural cycle of six years of harvest followed by a fallow seventh year. His victory is celebrated at the autumn New Year festival in the month of Tisri, timed to the arrival of the rains.

The epithet Baal-zebul derives from Baal combined with zbl, meaning prince. From the mid-sixteenth century BCE, Ba'al enjoyed a significant cult following in the Egyptian New Kingdom, though his death-and-restoration mythos was never equated with that of Osiris. In the Greco-Roman period he became assimilated in the Palestine region with Zeus and Jupiter, and continued as a Punic deity at Carthage.
