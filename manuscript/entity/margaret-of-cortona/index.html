Life and History

Margaret of Cortona (c. 1247 to 22 February 1297), known as The Magdalen of the Seraphic Order, had a childhood that could have inspired Snow White. Her mother died when she was seven. Her father, a tenant farmer from the Tuscany-Umbria border, soon remarried a stepmother who treated Margaret cruelly.

The Nobleman's Mistress

During her teens, possibly as young as thirteen, Margaret fell in love with a young nobleman. He told her that differences in social status prevented marriage, but asked her to live with him as his mistress. Margaret lived openly with him for nine years, flaunting her relationship and living luxuriously. She bore his son. When scandalized neighbors criticized her, she told them she would die a saint and pilgrims would visit her shrine.

Discovery of His Murder

One day, her lover's favorite dog came home alone and led Margaret to his murdered body. The killer's identity and motive remain unknown. His parents immediately expelled Margaret and her son from the family castle.

Rejection and Homelessness

Margaret briefly returned to her father and stepmother. Her stepmother rejected them, and Margaret's theatrical public penance embarrassed her father. She would put a noose around her neck on Sundays and kneel outside the church door for all neighbors to witness. Her father soon expelled her as well.

Conversion

Homeless and desperate, Margaret contemplated prostitution but instead sought help from Cortona's Franciscans. She publicly renounced her old life, though her initial sincerity remains questionable. She earned her keep through midwifery and nursing. Three years later, she joined the Third Order of Saint Francis, taking vows of celibacy and poverty.

Mystical Visions

In 1277, at age thirty, Margaret began experiencing ecstatic visions and intense personal communications with Jesus. She described herself as his oracle, delivering messages as directed. The Franciscans recorded her prophecies and messages.

Struggle with Beauty

Margaret struggled with her beauty and sexuality throughout her life. She told the Franciscans there would be a struggle between her and her body until death. She consumed virtually nothing, slept on cold floors, wore a hair shirt, engaged in self-flagellation, and never bathed. To her despair, she remained beautiful. She wanted to slice off her upper lip and nose, but her confessor refused permission.

The Celebrity Holy Woman

Margaret became a gifted fundraiser and founded a charity hospital in Cortona that still exists. Recognized as a holy woman in her lifetime, she performed healing miracles and became something of a celebrity. She was always notorious, and many doubted her sincerity.

Dante's Consultation

Dante Alighieri, author of the Divine Comedy, reputedly consulted Margaret. Some scholars believe she may have inspired Lucia, the psychopomp who leads Dante through Hell.

Death

Margaret foretold the date of her death, expressing her wish to die of starvation. She died from self-starvation combined with infections from extreme mortification. On her deathbed, an apparition of the devil terrorized her, claiming God would never forgive her because her fasting constituted suicide. However, Jesus, in an opposing vision, told her to ignore those urging her to eat.

Powers and Domains

Margaret is acclaimed for miracle healings, especially alleviating mental illness. She particularly favors those who have fallen and seek redemption.

Iconography

Depicted as a beautiful penitent woman, often with long hair and simple robes.

Sacred Site

The church in Cortona where she lived was rebuilt and dedicated to her. Her body is exposed in a glass-sided reliquary. She was canonized in 1728.

Feast Days

22 February, 16 May
