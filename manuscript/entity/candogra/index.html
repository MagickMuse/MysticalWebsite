Also known as

Chandogra, Candogra (fierce and terrible)

Overview

Candogra is a goddess of the Hindu tradition, a distinct form of Durga and one of the nine navadurgas, the nine forms of Durga worshipped through the nine nights of Navaratri. Her name means fierce and terrible, from canda meaning violent or burning and ugra meaning terrible or fearsome, so that her name doubles the wrathful note characteristic of the canda-root family of goddesses. She belongs alongside Canda, Candanayika, Candarupa, Candavati, and the compound Camunda in that family.

Classification

Goddess of the Hindu Sakta tradition. Distinct form of Durga, one of the navadurgas.

Domains

The doubled ferocity of the goddess, the point at which fierce power crosses over into terrible sovereignty, the ninefold turning of Durga through the nights of Navaratri, the cutting edge that closes down demonic obstacles.

Iconography

Depicted in the manner of the fierce forms of Durga, multi-armed and richly armed, mounted upon the lion, adorned with the ornaments of the wrathful goddesses. As with the other navadurgas, her image belongs to the cycle as a whole rather than to a separate independent iconography, though the doubled fierce name marks her image as one of the most severe among the nine.

Attributes

Sword, trident, bow, arrow, discus, mace, noose, and severed head carried across her multiple arms.

Sacred Sites

Durga temples across India that observe the navadurga cycle during the nine nights of Navaratri.

Ritual

Invoked as one of the nine navadurgas during Navaratri, taking her turn on the nightly worship. She receives offerings appropriate to the fiercest forms of Durga: red flowers, red cloth, and the recitation of the Devi Mahatmya and related Sakta hymns.

Lore

Candogra names herself twice over as fierce and terrible, and this doubling marks her particular office within the navadurga cycle as the form of Durga in which the wrathful edge crosses the threshold into terrifying sovereignty. Sakta theology holds that the terrible face of the goddess is not other than her compassionate face but its cutting expression against the demonic, and Candogra's worship rests on that reading of the fearsome as the mother's own protective ferocity.
