Mermaids are aquatic spirits in the shape of women from the waist up and fish from the waist down. They appear across nearly every culture that meets a body of water, from the lochs of Scotland and the reefs of the Caribbean to the lakes of Slavic forests and the temple ponds of the ancient Near East.

Powers and Skills

Mermaids carry great skill and healing power, and they can pass those gifts to those they favor. They are keepers of beauty, prophecy, love magick, and the secret witchcraft of the sea. They are also gatherers and gardeners of the ocean's strange flora, tending the underwater meadows where coral grows and seaweed sways.

Origins

One of the earliest recorded mermaids is the Assyrian goddess Atargatis, depicted in mermaid form as far back as 1000 BCE. According to her oldest stories, she killed a man she loved and, in her shame, banished herself first to a lake and then into the body of a half-fish, half-woman when even the lake could not hide her.

⚠️ The Taino people of the Caribbean speak of Aycayia, a mermaid whose voice has the power to sink ships. Aycayia belongs to a closed Indigenous practice and should not be approached by outsiders.

Persecution

Because their healing power could be granted to mortals, mermaids were among the entities persecuted during the witch hunts. One Hebridean tale from the small village of Eilean Anabuich tells of a man who captured a mermaid and, in exchange for her freedom, was granted three wishes: he became a prophet who could foretell the future of women, he gained the ability to sing (although, by local accounts, the song was barely worth the name), and he became a herbal doctor skilled enough to cure the King's Evil (tuberculosis) and other diseases once considered incurable.

Avoidance and Fear

Even pirates feared them. Blackbeard is said to have sailed nautical miles out of his way to avoid mermaid-haunted waters. The lesson runs through every culture that knows them: do not approach mermaid waters lightly, do not swim alone at night where the songs rise, and do not steal a merfolk's garment, for that act invites disaster.

The Sea King's Garden

Old fairy tales describe the gardens that mermaids tend at the bottom of the sea: water as blue as cornflower, sand the color of burning sulphur, walls of coral, and amber windows that open like swallow-houses to let the fish swim through. Each young sea princess is given her own plot of garden to plant as she chooses. The most thoughtful of them plants a rose-colored weeping willow over a marble statue salvaged from a shipwreck, and the tree grows so quickly and so fully that its crown and its root seem forever leaning toward each other in a long kiss.

The Crown and the Manatee

Belief in mermaids was once so literal that British law, still on the statute books into the nineteenth century, declared any mermaid found in British waters to be the property of the Crown. Christopher Columbus recorded sighting mermaids on his voyage to the Americas, though what he most likely saw was the manatee, a marine creature that cradles its young in its arms much as a human mother does. The mermaid appears so consistently across the cultures of the world that her very ubiquity has long persuaded people she must truly exist.

Mermen and Merpeople

The mermaid is not alone. Mermen and whole populations of merpeople are said to dwell in the depths of the oceans and in freshwater lakes alike.

Storm Warning and Granted Wishes

Unlike the siren, whose designs on humankind tend toward malice, the mermaid bears people a gentler regard. To a sailor, the sight of one is a warning of stormy weather to come. Mermaids are also said to grant wishes, and in Japan it is held that anyone who manages to eat the flesh of a mermaid will gain immortality, becoming as deathless as the mermaid herself.

Goddess of Desire

The mermaid is at once a symbol of female sexuality and an object of unreachable longing. Her fish's tail denies her the anatomy of a mortal woman, so she remains virginal and unobtainable, the focus of frustrated dreams and a figure set apart from physical love: a Goddess in her own right. She is often shown gazing into a hand-mirror as she combs her long hair, an image that also made her an emblem of vanity.

The Sign at Ephesus

A more pejorative meaning attached to the mermaid at Ephesus, where three symbols carved into a paving slab are popularly called the world's first advertisement: a pointing finger, a coin, and a mermaid. Together they directed passersby to the local brothel, the mermaid standing for the prostitute.

Love and the Winning of a Soul

In story the mermaid often loves a mortal man yet is thwarted, since he cannot breathe or live in her element. A famous fairy tale tells of one who trades her beautiful tail for human legs, though every step she takes is agony, and surrenders her tongue so that she can neither sing nor explain to the prince what she has done. In the end it is love rather than desire that drives her to exchange a mermaid's immortality for a human soul, and in doing so she leaves behind the animal part of herself to become a higher creature.
