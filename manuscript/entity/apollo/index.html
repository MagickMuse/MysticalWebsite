Source Notes

Origin and Period

Apollo is a god of hunting and healing of the Greek tradition, possibly with roots in cultures of Asia Minor. His known period of worship spans circa 1300 BC and earlier until Christianization circa 400 AD and probably later.

Synonyms

Apellon (pre-Homeric); Atepomarus (Celtic).

Centers of Cult

Delos, Pylo-Delphi, and many other sanctuaries throughout the Greek world.

Art References

The Parthenon frieze; the Belvedere Apollo triumphing over the Python; Apollo and Daphne; a famed but lost statue from Delos; Apollo holding the three Graces in his right hand; other contemporary sculpture and painting.

Literary Sources

The Iliad and Odyssey (Homer); the Theogony and Hymn to Apollo (Hesiod); and various other temple hymns.

Character and Lineage

One of the major Greek deities, Apollo was always perceived as a god who epitomizes youthful masculinity, possibly with early links to Lycia in Asia Minor (Hittite) and to Minoan Crete. He is generally a distant rather than an intimate and approachable god. His mother is Leto, who wandered the world in great suffering until she chanced on the island of Delos where she found refuge. Apollo is often portrayed as part of a triad with Leto and Artemis. He epitomizes the transition between adolescence and manhood in Greek male society.

Sanctuaries

At Delphi his sanctuary is central to the complex. At Delos it appears secondary to that of Artemis.

Healing and Pestilence

The paean dance of healing, particularly known from the Hyakinthia festival at Amyklai (Sparta), is closely identified with the Apollo cult. He is a god of healing but also of pestilence. He is the father of Asklepios, the god of healing, and he is continually associated with purification rites and oracles.

Hunter and Lyre Player

Generally Apollo is drawn as a god of hunters carrying a bow and arrow and associated with a stag or roe. He is also pictured with lions. He became, improbably, the patron god of poets and leader of the Muses, daughters of Zeus. Literature often presents Apollo in a dual aspect of fearsome hunter and gracious player of the lyre.

Acts of Mercilessness and Banishment

In his fearsome aspect he was at times merciless, killing the many children of Niobe, who had boasted of them to the chagrin of Leto. He fought and slew the Delphic python and the Olympic Cyclopes, but in both cases himself became subject to general laws of morality and suffered temporary banishment.

Mystical Number

Apollo is strongly associated with the mystical number seven, almost certainly a Mesopotamian concept.

Ugaritic and Celtic Identifications

In Ugaritic inscriptions he is referred to as Resep of the Arrow (see Resep). Apollo was widely revered under various local synonyms by the Celts.

Rampion as Funereal Offering

The ancient Greeks treated rampion (Campanula rapunculus) as a funereal vegetable and laid it on golden plates as an offering to Apollo. Its humble field-and-garden character did not keep it from a place at his altar.
