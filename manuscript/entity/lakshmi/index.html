Overview

Lakshmi, also known as Sri, is one of the most widely venerated goddesses in the Hindu pantheon. Her history in the tradition reaches back to at least pre-Buddhist times, making her one of the older major goddesses in continuous worship. She is associated above all with prosperity, well-being, royal power, and illustriousness, and she is understood to embody rather than merely govern these qualities: when they become visible in the world, she herself is said to be present. She is worshiped across all castes throughout India and carries a substantial body of mythology that has grown considerably since her earliest appearances in the textual record.

Since the late epic period, roughly the fourth century CE, she has been especially associated with Vishnu as his wife and consort. In this role the tradition presents her as a model of wifely devotion, serving her husband as lord.

The term Sri before the goddess

The name Sri does not refer to a goddess in the earliest layers of Vedic literature. It functions there as a term pointing to a cluster of desirable qualities. In the hymns it suggests capability, power, and advantageous skills, and as an outward quality it carries the sense of beauty, luster, glory, and high rank. Later Vedic literature shifts the emphasis toward royal contexts, using sri to describe the ruling power, dominion, and majesty of kings. In these passages sri reads less like a personal quality of the king and more like a mobile, disembodied power attached to the royal office itself. One passage goes so far as to identify sri with the cushion on which the king sits, treating the seat as the temporary carrier of ruling power. Beyond the royal sphere, sri also refers broadly to riches, prosperity, and abundance, something that any person may acquire or possess. Together the usages point to a cluster of auspicious qualities covering physical health, material prosperity, bodily beauty, and ruling majesty.

Earliest myth

In what is likely the earliest narrative that treats Sri as a goddess, she is the embodiment of auspicious qualities, particularly those tied to royal power. She is born through the austerities of Prajapati. When the other gods see her they covet her qualities and proceed to take them from her. Ten qualities or objects are identified: food, royal power, universal sovereignty, noble rank, power, holy luster, kingdom, fortune, bounteousness, and beauty. The myth places her origin squarely in the personification of auspicious qualities, especially those carrying associations of royal power and riches.

The Sri-sukta

The most developed picture of Sri-Laksmi in Vedic literature comes from the Sri-sukta, a hymn in her praise that forms part of an appendix to the Rg-veda and is considered pre-Buddhist in date. It is among the earliest hymns to the goddess and already gathers around her a set of symbols and attributes that she carries through the rest of her history in the Hindu tradition.

Gifts and invocations

Sri is invoked in the Sri-sukta to bring fame and prosperity. She is named bountiful and said to give abundance freely. Among the specific gifts attributed to her are gold, cattle, horses, and food. She is also asked to drive away her sister Alaksmi, whose name means misfortune. Alaksmi takes the forms of need, poverty, hunger, and thirst, and her banishment is as central a petition as any positive request.

Royal imagery

The hymn casts Sri in distinctly royal terms. She is described seated at the center of a chariot, in possession of the finest horses, and gladdened by the sound of elephants. These images connect her to the authority and ceremony of kingship, reflecting the long association between the term sri and the power of rulers.

Appearance

Sri is presented in the Sri-sukta as radiant and richly adorned. She shines like gold and is described as illustrious as the moon. She wears a necklace of gold and silver. She is said to shine like the sun and to glow with the luster of fire.

Fertility and the earth

An aspect of Sri that the Sri-sukta develops more fully than earlier material is her connection to fertility and the earth. She is called moist and is said to be perceptible through scent. The hymn links her to abundant harvests and describes her as dwelling in cow dung, a substance understood in the Hindu tradition as a carrier of fertile and purifying power. Her son is named Kardama, a word that means mud, mire, or slime. The overall picture is of a goddess whose presence is felt in the generative, moist potency of the soil.

Though this agricultural and earth-connected aspect does not remain prominent in her later literary history, it persists at the village level. Women in particular have been recorded worshiping Sri in the form of cow dung on certain occasions, a practice also prescribed in the Nilamata-purana.

The Pink Lotus Throne

Sri-Laksmi stands or sits on a lotus and carries the lotus as her most persistent symbol. In the Sri-sukta she is seated on a lotus, described as lotus-colored, appearing like a lotus, covered with lotuses, and wearing a garland of them. She is frequently called Padma and Kamala throughout the later tradition, both words for lotus.

The lotus carries two overlapping meanings in relation to her.

The first is fertility and the abundance of life. The lotus is rooted in and draws its strength from the primordial waters beneath it, and it stands for vegetative growth that has gathered the life-giving force of those waters into visible, embodied form. Sri-Laksmi, by association, represents the full flowering of organic life. At the largest scale the lotus can be read as a symbol of the created world as a whole: the lotus that grows from Vishnu's navel marks the site of a new cosmic creation, and the lotus appears in Tantric mandalas as a figure for the entire universe. She is, in this sense, the essential quality of creation itself, the rasa or inner nectar that gives the world its richness and beauty.

The second meaning is purity and spiritual authority. The lotus rises out of the mud without carrying any trace of it, and this quality has made it a symbol of spiritual perfection across both Hindu and Buddhist iconography. Deities, buddhas, and bodhisattvas who sit or stand on a lotus are understood to have transcended the constraints of finite existence. Sri-Laksmi as the goddess of the lotus therefore carries not only the royal authority that runs through her early history but also a priestly and spiritual authority, and in her the two are joined.

Gaja-Laksmi: the elephant flanking

Among the most enduring images of Sri-Laksmi is the Gaja-Laksmi composition, in which she stands or sits flanked by two elephants who pour water over her from their trunks or from vessels they carry. The elephants reinforce her two core associations: fertility and royal power.

Elephants as fertilizing rain

An ancient Hindu tradition holds that the first elephants had wings and moved through the sky as clouds, releasing rain wherever they traveled. A sage cursed them when they landed on the tree beneath which he sat and broke his meditation, stripping them of their wings and binding them to the earth. Earthbound elephants are still understood in this tradition as cousins of rain clouds, and their presence is thought to draw those clouds closer. The showering elephants in the Gaja-Laksmi images therefore reinforce her deep connection to the fertility of crops and the generative moisture of the living world.

Elephants as royal authority

Elephants were also an unmistakable sign of royal power in ancient India. Kings maintained elephant stables as part of their military strength, traveled on elephants in ceremonial processions, and were understood to carry personal responsibility for the fertility of their lands and the regularity of the rains. The Gaja-Laksmi images bring both meanings together: rain-bringer and royal emblem in a single composition.

The consecration reading

The act of pouring water over the goddess in these images has been read as a representation of the royal consecration ceremony known as the abhisekha, in which the king was anointed with auspicious waters to invest him with authority and vigor. If the elephants perform the abhisekha on Laksmi, they bestow on her the very qualities of fertility and royal power that she already possesses to the highest degree, and she in turn infuses them with the same. The elephants in many of these images also stand on lotuses, tying together the whole symbolic field: the lotus of purity and abundance, the elephant of rain and kingship, and the goddess who is the source and fullness of all three.

Sri-Laksmi in later Hinduism

Association with Soma

Some texts connect Sri-Laksmi to the god Soma. She is said to attend him after he performs a great royal sacrifice, a pairing that speaks to two of her defining characteristics at once. Her attendance on a king who has just assumed formal royal authority reflects her core function of gravitating toward sovereignty and confirming it by her presence. Soma is also the lord of plants and the embodiment of the fertile sap that flows through all vegetative life, the same vital quality that Sri-Laksmi herself carries. The two deities reinforce each other as expressions of the same underlying vitality in creation.

Association with Dharma

A handful of texts describe Sri-Laksmi as the wife of Dharma, given to him by her father Daksa alongside a group of goddesses who each personify auspicious qualities. The pairing works as theological shorthand: Dharma personifies virtuous conduct and right action, while Sri personifies prosperity and well-being. The association teaches that prosperity follows naturally as the consequence of righteous living.

Association with Indra

The most fully developed series of associations in the later literature links Sri-Laksmi to the storm god Indra. As king of the gods and the foremost deity of the Vedic pantheon, Indra's royal nature makes him a natural partner for a goddess whose central function is the bestowal of royal power. In these myths Sri-Laksmi is the embodiment of royal authority itself: her presence confers it and her absence strips it away.

Several myths show her moving between rulers. She is said to have dwelled at different times with the demon kings Prahlada and Bali. While she resided with each, they governed their realms justly, their societies ran smoothly, their lands were fertile, and the demon kings themselves radiated noble inner and outer qualities. When Sri-Laksmi left Prahlada at Indra's request, he lost his luster along with the qualities she had sustained in him: good conduct, virtuous behavior, truth, activity, and strength. The myth of Bali follows a similar arc. Bali had defeated Indra in battle, and Sri-Laksmi was drawn to his bravery and his winning qualities, joining him along with her attendant virtues. Under her company Bali ruled the three worlds with virtue and they prospered. Only after Vishnu tricked Bali into surrendering the three worlds did Sri-Laksmi depart, leaving him drained of power and luster.

Her presence with Indra also brings abundance in a more concrete sense. In one myth, the moment she sat beside him, rain fell, crops grew abundantly, cows gave plenty of milk, and the whole earth flourished.

The Sri-Laksmi and Indra pairing maps onto a pattern found across many religious traditions: a female deity of the fertile earth paired with a male deity of sky and rain whose combined action generates the growth that all life requires. The Vedic Dyaus and Prthivi are an earlier Indian instance of the same cosmic couple, and the Sri-Laksmi and Indra pairing may be understood as a later expression of the same relationship. Indra wields the thunderbolt and brings rain; he also carried phallic associations through his identification with the plow. Sri-Laksmi embodies the fertile earth that receives and transforms his gift.

Association with Kubera

Some traditions also associate Sri-Laksmi with Kubera, the lord of the Yaksas and the divine possessor and distributor of wealth. Kubera is the guardian of the earth's treasures in the form of gems, and his domain overlaps naturally with Sri-Laksmi's sphere of prosperity. Where either is found, wealth and abundance are expected to follow, and the two become paired as a couple on that basis.

The connection to Kubera also draws Sri-Laksmi into relationship with the Yaksas, a race of supernatural beings who frequent forests and wild places. In Indian art the Yaksas are strongly associated with vegetative fertility. They are conventionally depicted embracing or leaning against trees, or pouring forth vegetation from their mouths or navels. Their iconographic identity is bound up with the inexhaustible fecundity of plant life, which makes them natural symbolic companions for Sri-Laksmi, whose own presence is expressed in the sap and growth of the living world.

Reputation for fickleness

Sri-Laksmi's movement between so many different male deities, combined with the well-known tendency of good fortune to come and go, gave her a reputation in later literature for fickleness and inconstancy. One text reports that she is so restless that even in a painted image she appears to move, and that her attachment to Vishnu owes entirely to her fascination with his many avatara forms. Good fortune is by nature unstable, and Sri-Laksmi was understood to reflect that quality.

Permanent association with Vishnu

By the late epic period, around the fourth century CE, the association between Sri-Laksmi and Vishnu became settled and fixed. Her earlier instability gave way to steadfastness, and she became his devoted wife and permanent consort.

The mythological event that established the connection is the churning of the milk ocean by the gods and demons in pursuit of the nectar of immortality. The myth draws on an ancient cosmological idea: creation proceeds from a limitless body of primordial water, and the potency of life, the rasa or essential sap, lies latent within that formless expanse. Ordered creation becomes possible only when those waters are agitated and refined, drawing their hidden essence upward. Churning milk to yield butter serves as the analogy: the same refining process, applied to the primordial ocean, produces the nectar of immortality. In most later versions of the myth Sri-Laksmi herself emerges from the churning among the treasures it yields.

Her emergence from the ocean makes sense given what she is. She embodies the vitality and sap that pervade all organic life and represents the transformation of formless primordial water into living, ordered creation. Later versions of the myth illustrate her necessity by imagining her absence: when Sri-Laksmi withdraws from the three worlds after being insulted by Indra, all sacrifices cease, austerities lapse, generosity disappears, the sun and moon lose their brilliance, the gods lose their strength, and fire loses its heat. The worlds grow dull and begin to decay. When she returns, vitality is restored and the order of both human society and the divine realm recovers its purpose.

Within the churning myth itself, Sri-Laksmi's attraction to Vishnu is entirely consistent with her nature. Vishnu is the dominant figure in the event: both the churning rope (the cosmic serpent Vasuki) and the churning platform (the cosmic tortoise) are his own forms. As the clear master of the whole operation he is the natural recipient of its most precious outcome. Her movement toward him echoes her long-established tendency to seek out and settle with the strongest and most powerful.

Vishnu as ideal consort

Vishnu's role in medieval Hinduism further grounds the pairing. As the supreme divine king he dwells in his celestial court Vaikuntha, depicted in royal splendor. His central function is the maintenance of dharmic order, carried out through his avataras who intervene in the world when that order is threatened, and also through righteous human kings who serve as his earthly agents. Sri-Laksmi's traditional function of empowering kings and conferring royal authority fits seamlessly into this arrangement: Vishnu designates righteous rulers as his agents, and Sri-Laksmi empowers them to fulfill that role.

As Vishnu's wife, Sri-Laksmi's character becomes more settled. Her restless movement from ruler to ruler gives way to loyal accompaniment. She follows him through each of his avataras, taking a form appropriate to the incarnation: when Vishnu descended as Rama, she appeared as Sita; when he became Krishna, she became Rukmini. In every form he takes she is his consort.

In iconographic representations of the pair, Sri-Laksmi is consistently shown smaller than Vishnu and with two arms rather than the four she carries when depicted alone. In the Laksmi-Narayana type she sits on Vishnu's left thigh, her right arm around his neck while his left arm encircles her waist. Some images show the two holding hands or gazing into each other's eyes. In certain compositions they are merged into a single bisexual figure, Vishnu forming the right half and Laksmi the left. One image from Badami shows Vishnu on a raised stool with Laksmi seated on the ground beside him, leaning against him with her hand resting on his knee.

Several texts locate her presence in the behaviors of orderly social and domestic life. She is said to dwell among those who speak the truth and practice generosity, those who maintain clean bodies and dress with care, those who eat in moderation and conduct their household relations properly. In the Mahabharata she declares that she dwells in truth, generosity, vow, austerity, strength, and virtue. The Vishnu-purana articulates the mutual dependence of the divine couple in a series of paired correspondences: Vishnu is speech and she is its meaning; he is understanding and she is intellect; he is the creator and she is the creation; she is the earth and he is its support; she is the vine and he is the tree to which she clings; he is one with all that is male in the world and she with all that is female; he is love and she is pleasure.

Worship and festivals

General worship

Sri-Laksmi is one of the most widely worshiped deities in the Hindu pantheon. Her auspicious character and her reputation for granting fertility, luck, wealth, and well-being draw devotees in temples and village shrines across India. Her likenesses appear on walls, pillars, lintels, and niches of sanctuaries dedicated to many different deities, not only those with a direct Vaishnava orientation.

Beyond festival worship, she is a constant object of vratas, religious vows in which a devotee undertakes some act of devotion or piety in exchange for a requested blessing. The blessings petitioned through vratas vary with the individual and with the festival context, but the most commonly sought are marital fidelity, the long life of one's spouse, the fertility of crops, and general material well-being.

Dipavali (Diwali)

The most important festival of Sri-Laksmi worship outside of Bengal is Dipavali, also called Diwali, held in the late autumn. Three themes run through the festival in close relation to one another: her association with wealth and prosperity, her association with fertile crops, and her role as the guarantor of good fortune in the year ahead.

Her connection to prosperity is most visible in her particular popularity with merchants during this period. It is customary for businesspeople to worship their account books at Dipavali, on the understanding that wealth does not come without Laksmi's blessing.

Agricultural themes are prominent wherever the festival retains its older character. Cultivators are expected to worship the harvested crops and to offer animal sacrifices. The cow dung heaped up for fertilizing the next season's fields is visited, honored, and petitioned for abundant future harvests. In the Deccan and in Orissa this dung heap is worshiped by every householder on the festival day, a practice that directly continues the earth-fertility aspect of Sri that is already present in the Sri-sukta.

The festival also has a strong end-of-year character. Ghosts of the dead are understood to return at this time; the demon Bali is said to emerge from the underworld to rule for three days; goblins and malicious spirits are active. Gambling, extravagant spending, and boisterous behavior are expected rather than discouraged. Laksmi is invoked throughout to ward off the dangers brought by the returning dead and by Bali's hosts, and to bless gamblers with the success that will signal their good fortune through the whole coming year.

The banishment of Alaksmi is also central to Dipavali. In many places it is held that Alaksmi is driven off for the year by the lighting of lamps, one of the festival's defining visual features, and by noise made with household pots, pans, or instruments. In Bengal, an image of Alaksmi is ritually disfigured by cutting off its nose and ears, after which an image of Laksmi is set in its place, marking the formal installation of good luck for the year to come.

During Dipavali it is also customary in North India to replace the small clay images of Laksmi and Ganesha kept in homes and shops. Laksmi's pairing with Ganesha, the elephant-headed son of Shiva and Parvati, is especially prominent in North India, where it is more common to see the two together than to see Laksmi beside Vishnu. The pairing carries the same symbolic charge as the Gaja-Laksmi iconography: elephants are an ancient emblem of hers, and Ganesha is himself a Yaksa-type figure associated with riches and good fortune. His presence beside her reinforces both her wealth associations and her connection to the fertile, abundant powers of the earth, in the same way that Kubera does.

Kaumudi-purnima

In Orissa, Laksmi is also associated with crops and food at the Kaumudi-purnima festival. Women invoke her over a mound of freshly harvested grain and recount a story in which her disappearance brings the disappearance of all crops and food, while her return restores abundance. The myth mirrors the churning-of-the-ocean account of what happens to the three worlds in her absence.

Durga Puja

Laksmi is also worshiped as part of Durga Puja. Though the festival as practiced today is not primarily a harvest observance, traces of a harvest dimension remain, and Laksmi's place within it reflects her persistent association with agricultural fertility and the renewal of the earth's vigor.

Summer festival with Vishnu

A summer festival centers on the moment when Vishnu is understood to fall asleep for several months. Prayers are offered at this time to prevent the loss of one's spouse. Laksmi and Vishnu together in this festival embody marital harmony and contentment, with Laksmi in her role as faithful and devoted wife.

A separate festival honoring the same divine pair presents her in a contrasting aspect. During this occasion Vishnu is described as going off with another consort. Laksmi, in response to his infidelity, breaks his vehicle and locks him out of their shared home, understood as the temple itself. Here she is the jealous wife and fierce protector of the household, a dimension of her character that sits alongside, and complicates, her more commonly emphasized image as the patient model of wifely virtue.

In the Pancaratra school

Sri-Laksmi's association with Vishnu eventually drew her into the center of two major Hindu theological traditions: the Pancaratra school and Sri Vaisnavism.

In the Pancaratra school she comes to play the pivotal role in the creation and ongoing evolution of the universe, functioning as Vishnu's sakti, his active cosmic power. In the Pancaratra account of creation, Vishnu remains almost entirely passive. After awakening Laksmi at the close of the night of cosmic dissolution, he steps back. His role is that of an architect who provides the plan but takes no further part in the building. Laksmi alone carries out the work, and the entire cosmogony unfolds through her action. She is said to act in accordance with his will, yet the impression throughout is of a goddess operating with full independence.

The practical result of Vishnu's passivity is that Laksmi comes to dominate the Pancaratra vision of the divine entirely. She occupies the position of the supreme underlying reality: the ground upon which all existence rests, the force that pervades all creation with vitality, will, and awareness. The Laksmi-tantra, a major Pancaratra text, states that the whole immensity of the universe's creation is accomplished with only one-billionth of her being. She is so far beyond what the mind can contain that only a fraction of her is ever manifest in the created world. In the same text she describes herself in terms that make her cosmic scope plain: she is present in all that exists whether manifest or unmanifest, the inciting potential within everything; she sends creation forth and withdraws it at dissolution; she sustains it in the interval; she pardons the sins of living beings as the earth absorbs everything placed upon it; she is the thinking process itself and is contained within all things.

In functional terms, Laksmi in the Pancaratra vision has absorbed the roles of the three great male gods of the Hindu pantheon. She creates, as Brahma does; she sustains, as Vishnu does; and she periodically dissolves, as Shiva does. She is simultaneously the object of devotion, the dispenser of grace, and the final bestower of liberation. It is she, not Vishnu, whose form is described in detail and held up as the supreme focus of meditation, and it is her mantra that is understood to carry salvific power.

Despite this elevation to the summit of the divine hierarchy, Laksmi does not lose the character she carried from the beginning of her history. Even in the Pancaratra system she remains the goddess who imbues creatures with well-being and illustriousness and who pervades the creation as its living sap. In one passage of the Laksmi-tantra she describes herself as the oil that keeps a lamp alight, lubricating the senses of living beings with the sap of her own consciousness. Elsewhere the text identifies her with prakrti, the principle of nature that spontaneously generates all material reality. Prakrti is the dynamic, outward-moving aspect of creation, tending toward multiplication, differentiation, and specificity. It is an active and fertile principle closely aligned with the sap-of-existence quality that Laksmi carried from her earliest history. Her identification with Vishnu's sakti in the Pancaratra system carries the same meaning in more refined terms: sakti, though more abstract than rasa or soma, points unmistakably to vigorous, dynamic power associated with life and growth. Her essential character as a positive, fertile force remains intact through all her theological promotions.

In Sri Vaisnavism

Sri Vaisnavism begins from the presupposition that Vishnu, as the supreme deity, is always accompanied by and associated with his consort Sri. Here, however, Vishnu rather than Laksmi holds the central position on the theological stage. He is identified with the highest philosophical principles, and the primary devotional aim is to approach him, love him, and draw as close to him as possible.

Laksmi's distinctive role in Sri Vaisnavism is that of mediator between Vishnu and his devotees. Vishnu as the mighty king of the celestial court Vaikuntha can appear remote and even severe, his majesty making direct approach feel difficult. Sri-Laksmi softens that distance. She is the approachable, compassionate face of the divine, the gracious mother whose warmth offsets her husband's occasional sternness.

The theologian Vedanta Desika (1268-1368 CE) understood Laksmi as all but indispensable to the devotional encounter with Vishnu. In his writings she is a mother who willingly takes the devotee's part and intervenes with her husband on their behalf, pleading their case and winning his attention. Another writer of the school, Periyavaccan Pillai (b. 1228), captures the same dynamic in a dialogue: Vishnu declares that a particular human being has been defying his laws since the beginning of time and that to pardon such a person without punishment would be to disregard scripture; Laksmi replies that if he punishes the human rather than saving them, his own quality of grace will not survive the act. Her argument turns on Vishnu's self-conception, pressing him to be merciful precisely because mercy is essential to who he is. In other passages she is described as using more personal means, distracting Vishnu from his intention to punish by drawing his attention with her beauty. The theologian Manavala Mamunikal (1370-1443) describes her in this mode: she uses her beauty to captivate the Lord, catches his eye, lets her garments fall slightly out of place.

Throughout Sri Vaisnavism Sri-Laksmi embodies divine compassion. Her presence in the theology makes the divine couple feel intimate and warm rather than purely majestic and remote, and it gives the devotee a point of entry that does not require the full weight of approaching Vishnu directly. As mediator she bestows what the Sri Vaisnava devotee values most: the grace of Vishnu himself. And across both schools she remains, as she has always been, sovereignty itself.
