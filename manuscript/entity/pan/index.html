O Goat-Foot God of Arcady

"O goat-foot god of Arcady ... This modern world hath need of Thee!" So wrote poet Oscar Wilde. Beginning in the nineteenth century, Pan became a symbol of wild nature, the untamed libido, primal human nature, and also Paganism. It is no coincidence that James M. Barrie's wild little hero is named Peter Pan.

The Wildman and Greenman Image

Pan embodies the Wildman archetype: the spirit of wild animals and Nature within all creatures. He represents fertility, fecundity, abandon, and ecstasy in their rawest forms. His image is the Greenman come alive with untamed animal energy.

Wild, Irrepressible Life Essence

Pan is the spirit of wild, irrepressible life essence. He is sexually voracious, always chasing after Nymphs, always ready for a romantic interlude with men, women, and probably goats, too. People tend to laugh at Pan because they perceive him as bestial and ugly. Many paintings portray Nymphs running from him, but they tend to be the exception. Pan's erotic talents are legendary. He even seduced Aphrodite. Pan swore to have sex with every Maenad, and he kept his word.

Great God of Tremendous Power

Pan is no trivial woodland sprite. He is a great god of tremendous power. His name derives from Paon, a "pasturer." Pan is lord of fertility, wild nature, ecstatic music, wild goats, shepherds, flocks, and hunters. He bestows musical skill. Officially he is the son of Hermes and a Nymph, but other myths suggest that he is older than the Olympic pantheon:

  • Pan allegedly taught Apollo the art of prophecy
  • He gave Artemis her first hunting hounds

There are many man-goat spirits, including Faunus, Krampus, and Ördög but references to the goat god usually mean Pan.

Spirit of Thresholds and Panic

Pan is the spirit of corners, thresholds, borders, and edges. He is most likely seen at twilight at the edge of the woods from the corner of your eye. The word panic derives from Pan. It's the reaction caused by his shout. Pan has no need for conventional weapons as his voice is sufficient to instill panic. The Greeks credited Pan with causing the Persians to flee in absolute terror at the Battle of Marathon.

Ancient Greek hunters considered Pan their patron. They offered him trophies of their success, usually heads or skins, but if results were disappointing, they scourged his image, demanding that he provide better luck next time.

Inspiration for Art and Literature

Pan may be a musician but he inspires authors:

  • Arthur Machen's 1890 novella, The Great God Pan
  • Algernon Blackwood's 1912 book, Pan's Garden, and 1917 story "The Touch of Pan"
  • Lord Dunsany's 1927 novel, The Blessing of Pan
  • Dion Fortune's 1936 occult novel, The Goat Food God

Pan inspires artists, too, including Rosaleen Norton, Arnold Böcklin, Franz Von Stuck, and Mikhail Vrubel. Pan was among the first deities embraced by Neo-Paganism. He is invoked for vitality, health, fertility, and successful, exciting adventures, romantic or otherwise.

Spirit Allies

  • Pan is among the dancing spirits in Dionysus' entourage
  • Pan is venerated alongside Nymphs, Hermes, Dionysus, and Ariadne

Pitys and the Pine

Pitys was a dryad pursued by more than one god, including Boreas, the cold north wind. Some versions of the tale say Pan changed her into a pine tree, because he loved her, or because he was trying to hide her, or both. Others say Boreas chased her off a cliff and Gaia took pity on her lifeless body and transformed it into Pine.

The Death of Pan:

The Greek historian Plutarch (c.45–c.120 CE), a priest of Apollo at Delphi, wrote that during the reign of Roman Emperor Tiberius (14–37 CE), an Egyptian sailor named Thamus heard a spectral voice demanding: "Thamus, are you there? When you reach Palodes, take care to proclaim that the great god Pan is dead."

Pan, however, was not dead: reports from a century after Plutarch's death indicate Pan was actively worshipped in shrines found in mountain caves and grottoes.

This story was very popular among early Christians who suggested it coincided with the day Christ was crucified, claiming all Pagan oracles ceased from that day forth. The story was interpreted as a parable of the death of Paganism in response to the resurrection of Christ.

Robert Graves, author of The Greek Myths, suggested that Thamus misunderstood "Thamus Pan-megas Tethnece" or "the all-great Tammuz is dead," a reference to Tammuz, Ishtar's consort, a dying god who died annually only to be reborn each year.

Later Demonization and Renaissance:

During the later Hellenic period, Pan developed a disreputable aura. He was identified with rustic, country religion. Classical Greeks, with their emphasis on human beauty and perfection, considered gods who combined human and animal anatomy to be vulgar. Pan became more identified as a woodland creature than as a god.

However, by the Victorian era, Pan returned to the forefront: he was believed to epitomize the vibrant, Earthy, authentic flavor of Paganism, hence Oscar Wilde's poem. Pan became extremely popular during the early Pagan renaissance and is the inspiration for Dion Fortune's 1936 novel, The Goat Foot God. He remains an extremely significant and beloved deity among Neo-Pagans.

Role in Hellenic Household Worship

Within the household pattern of offerings, Pan receives pine, images of goats, fern, wild flowers, and incense of herbs and various perfumes. His tokens are the phallus, horns, and the pan-flute. His colour is green. The goat and the turtle stand as His sacred animals, and the pine, the beech-tree, and reeds among His sacred plants.
