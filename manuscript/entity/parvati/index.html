Daughter of the Mountain

Parvati is most famous as Shiva's bride and Shakti, but she is a goddess in her own right. Because so many Hindu goddesses, including Durga and Kali, are now officially identified as avatars or paths of Parvati, they can be very difficult to disentangle.

The Central Myth

Her central myth describes how Parvati as a little girl fell in love with Shiva and determined to marry him. It was what she was born to do: Parvati is the reincarnation of Sati, Shiva's first love, although he does not recognize her and still grieves for Sati. Parvati may or may not be the same deity as Uma.

She undergoes tremendous austerities to attract Shiva's attention and win his love. They do not always live happily ever after. Shiva and Parvati have a passionate but sometimes contentious relationship. He is a difficult husband, often more committed to his spiritual path than to his relationships or domestic responsibilities. ("What domestic responsibilities?" he would ask.) Parvati is no wilting violet but a proud, powerful, defiant goddess of love and fertility. She is truly Shiva's match and not afraid to stand up to him. Parvati works very hard to maintain Shiva's erotic interest. She ornaments herself with henna, which allegedly creates irresistible sexual allure.

Karttikeya is their son, but Parvati conceived Ganesha all by herself.

Early textual history

Sati-Parvati does not appear in Vedic literature. Several Vedic texts do mention Shiva's sister or consort, but the names given to these figures connect only tenuously to the later, fully developed Sati-Parvati. A figure called Ambika, a name that later becomes a common epithet for several goddesses and especially for Durga, is named Shiva's sister in one Vedic passage and his consort in another. Elsewhere Shiva's wife is called Rudrani. None of these references are detailed enough to confirm a connection to the Sati-Parvati of later mythology.

The Kena-upanishad contains a figure named Uma Haimavati, one of the most common names for the later Sati-Parvati. In this text she carries no connection to Shiva and is associated with mountains only through her name, Haimavati meaning she who belongs to Himavat, the Himalaya Mountains personified. Her function in the Kena-upanishad is to appear suddenly among the assembled gods and reveal the knowledge of brahman to them, after which she vanishes. The identification of this figure with Sati-Parvati is tenuous, though later writers naturally made it, retelling the episode so that it is unmistakably Shiva's consort who reveals to the gods that Shiva is the absolute reality underlying all things.

Textual and archaeological evidence for Sati-Parvati as Shiva's wife becomes clear in the epic period, roughly 400 BCE to 400 CE. Both the Ramayana and Mahabharata present her as Shiva's wife. The Mahabharata references the destruction of Daksha's sacrifice, the birth of Kartikeya and the defeat of the demon Taraka, and describes Shiva and Parvati dwelling together in the Himalayas, playing dice and sporting. The central themes of the later mythology are clearly known to the epic writers, though not yet told in full.

Theological role

Parvati's defining purpose in classical Hindu mythology is to draw Shiva out of his ascetic isolation and into worldly engagement. Shiva as the great solitary ascetic embodies world-renunciation, and the potency he accumulates through withdrawal is understood to be meant for release into the created world. Parvati is the force that accomplishes that release. In her successive roles as maiden, wife, and mother she progressively extends Shiva's involvement in life, transforming his stored-up power into generative energy. She is the complementary pole to his world-denial.

As his sakti, his embodied power, she becomes identified with the creative force of the cosmos and the underlying potency of all things. In some schools of Shaivism she also assumes the role of ideal devotee. In Shaiva Siddhanta, a major southern school, she takes on the role of Shiva's embodied grace, paralleling Sri-Laksmi's mediating role in Sri Vaisnavism.

Name and epithets

Parvati's name means she who dwells in the mountains or she who is of the mountain. Her epithets repeat and extend this identification: Sailasuta (daughter of the mountain peaks), Giriputri (daughter of the mountains), Girirajaputri (daughter of the king of the mountains), and Girisa (mistress of the mountains). Her name and titles together suggest an origin in a mountain goddess associated with non-Aryan tribal peoples, a type of deity occasionally mentioned in Sanskrit literature as a distinct class. Such an origin would make her a natural companion for Shiva, himself a deity of mountain regions and the fringes of settled society. The Mahabharata describes both Parvati and Shiva as foresters.

Despite this possible origin, Parvati as she appears in the literary tradition has shed almost all association with wildness or tribal culture. She is not normally grouped with the Matrkas, a class of goddesses described as bloodthirsty, disease-bringing, foreign-tongued, and fond of inaccessible places. Whatever connection she may once have had with such traditions, the mythology presents her consistently as a figure of the household and the orderly domestic world.

Ancient mountain goddess

In her earliest form Parvati may have been a mountain goddess who presided over a court of nature spirits and was associated with women's mysteries. She reputedly invented rice cultivation. She is now rarely venerated without Shiva. Men are still not permitted into the inner sanctums of her shrines.

The full mythology

Why Parvati was born

Most versions of Parvati's mythology begin by explaining the necessity of her birth. The demon Taraka has received the boon of invincibility against all beings except a child of Shiva's. Since Shiva is a committed ascetic with no interest in marriage or progeny, the gods face a problem: they must find a goddess capable of winning his attention and drawing him into a relationship that will produce the needed child. Parvati is the answer, generally understood as Sati's reincarnation, sometimes described as consenting to be reborn in response to the gods' direct petition.

In some versions Parvati and Sati are understood as manifestations of the supreme reality, the Mahadevi or great goddess, who descends into embodied form for the same reason Vishnu does in his avataras: to restore the balance between order and disorder. In other versions Parvati offers a more personal reason for her birth: she wants to reward Mena, wife of Himavat, for the devotion Mena showed her during her previous life as Sati.

Childhood and the sage's prediction

Parvati is born to Himavat and Mena. She is usually described as dark, and some versions give her the name Kali, meaning the dark one, on account of her complexion. She is also described as beautiful. From childhood she is drawn to Shiva, repeating his name and delighting in hearing about his deeds and appearance. A sage visits her home while she is still a child, examines the marks on her body, and predicts that she will marry a naked yogi. Her parents receive the news as an honor. Parvati is delighted, and some versions say she retains a memory of Shiva from her previous life as Sati.

Kama's destruction

At some point in Parvati's efforts to attract Shiva, the gods send Kama, the god of desire, to rouse Shiva's lust. Kama approaches with the sounds and scents of spring and tries to strike Shiva with his intoxicating weapons. Shiva opens his third eye and burns Kama to ashes on the spot. The gods and Parvati mourn. Later in the story it becomes clear that Kama's power was not truly destroyed when his body was: Shiva does eventually fall in love with Parvati, and in time he recreates Kama in bodily form at the request of Kama's wife Rati.

Parvati's tapas

Parvati sets out to win Shiva through tapas, ascetic austerities. In Hindu tradition a practitioner who performs tapas with sufficient heroism and persistence generates so much inner heat that the gods are eventually compelled to grant a wish in order to stop the process. Parvati's method is also strategically fitting: by entering the world of the renouncer she demonstrates to Shiva that she can meet him on his own ground, that she possesses the inner discipline and control he most values. She abandons the householder's world entirely and outstrips the greatest sages in her austerities. She sits surrounded by four fires in the summer heat, remains exposed to rain and cold without shelter, subsists on leaves and then on air alone, and stands on one leg for years.

The heat she accumulates eventually makes the gods themselves uncomfortable, and they go to Shiva urging him to grant Parvati's wish. In some versions of the myth, before agreeing, Shiva tests her by sending an agent, or coming himself in disguise, and telling her that he is terrible, uncivilized, and inauspicious. She refuses to be dissuaded. Her steadfastness is what ultimately wins him over.

The marriage

The wedding is elaborate. Shiva's marriage procession contains much of the Hindu pantheon. A recurring motif is Mena's reaction when she sees Shiva for the first time. She is appalled that her beautiful daughter is to marry such an outlandish figure. In some versions she threatens to kill herself and faints when told the peculiar-looking figure in the procession is her future son-in-law.

The birth of Kartikeya

After the marriage Shiva and Parvati go to Mount Kailasa and engage in lovemaking so prolonged and intense that it shakes the cosmos. The gods grow alarmed, some at the disruption itself and some at what an extraordinarily powerful child from two such potent deities might become. They interrupt the union. As a result Shiva's seed spills outside Parvati. It is intensely hot and passes from vessel to vessel until it reaches the Ganges River, where it is incubated and born as Kartikeya. The child makes his way back to his parents, defeats the demon Taraka, and saves the world. When Parvati first sees Kartikeya, her breasts are said to flow with milk spontaneously in maternal affection.

The birth of Ganesha

The family is completed when Parvati creates Ganesha entirely on her own. Wanting a guardian for her privacy, she shapes a son from the dirt and sweat of her own body and stations him at the door with orders to admit no one. When Shiva himself arrives and tries to enter, Ganesha bars the way. Shiva, enraged, decapitates the boy. Parvati is furious and demands her son be restored. Shiva replaces the boy's head with an elephant's, the first available head his attendants can find, and places Ganesha in command of all his troops and heavenly attendants.

Family life and quarrels

For the most part Shiva and Parvati's domestic life is presented as happy and intimate. In iconography they are typically shown in close embrace, and the family of four is often depicted in playful, harmonious association.

There are quarrels. Bengali versions of the mythology portray Shiva as an irresponsible husband who smokes hemp and cannot manage himself, while Parvati is the steady wife who complains to her mother but remains loyal. Games of dice sometimes end badly: Shiva once loses his loincloth to Parvati and she laughs, sparking a quarrel. He once covers her eyes playfully from behind while the cosmos is being sustained, and the darkness that follows threatens existence itself. On another occasion Shiva nicknames her Kali, meaning the dark one, which she takes as an insult about her complexion. She performs austerities to shed her dark skin and acquires a luminous golden tone, after which she becomes Gauri, the bright or golden one. In some versions her discarded dark sheath itself becomes a fierce warrior goddess.

The dark alter ego

The theme of a violent aspect separating from Parvati appears in several myths involving demonic threats to the cosmos. When Parvati is called on to fight and grows angry, her wrath manifests as a distinct goddess who fights on her behalf rather than Parvati engaging in battle directly. This fierce figure is often identified as Kali. Parvati's essential character in the mythology remains mild and domestic, and her violent capacity is repeatedly displaced onto this other being generated from her anger.

Benevolent but Temperamental

Parvati is a generous, benevolent goddess invoked for fertility, prosperity, and domestic happiness. She is petitioned to find and win true love and to maintain sexual interest. Parvati is kind but temperamental. She is known to hold a grudge. If angered, she can allegedly only be propitiated by women. Parvati may be invoked alongside Ganesha, who adores her. Ganesha may be requested to mediate with his mother.

Tension and Resolution

The central theme of the Parvati cycle of myths is the perennial tension in Hinduism between the ascetic ideal and the householder ideal. Parvati represents the householder. Her mission in almost all renditions of the myth is to lure Shiva into the world of marriage, sex, and children, to tempt him away from asceticism, yoga, and otherworldly preoccupations. In this role she upholds the order of dharma, enhances life in the world, and represents the beauty and attraction of worldly, sexual life. She cherishes the house and society rather than the forest, the mountains, or the ascetic life. In this role she repeats the feat of Sati in luring Shiva into an erotic relationship, which this time eventually brings him within the sacrificial, priestly order permanently. Parvati civilizes Shiva with her presence — she domesticates him. Of her role in relation to Shiva in the hymns of Manikkavacakar, a ninth-century poet-saint from South India, it has been said: "Shiva, the great unpredictable 'madman,' is rendered momentarily sane when in the company of the goddess. Contact with his properly cultured spouse seems to connect him with ordinary social reality and temporarily domesticates him."

Shiva is always said to have no family, no lineage, and no interest in progeny, which is one of the central concerns of the order of dharma and the householder's religion. Parvati, on the contrary, is born into an established family and longs for marriage, children, and a home. Shiva is associated with fire, which dries up or burns the juices of life. His inner fire is so intense that in one myth he is shown to contain ashes in his veins instead of blood. In one of the most famous of his myths he burns the god of lust to ashes with the fire from his third eye. Parvati is associated with Soma, the deity or substance associated with the life essence of all plants. In many myths it is she who asks Shiva to revive Kama, as she realizes that the god of sexual desire is at the root of the householder's life. In relation to Kama, then, Shiva is a destructive fire, whereas Parvati is a refreshing, liquid glance: "While the fiery glance is Shiva, the Soma glance is Parvati, who revives Kama when Shiva has burnt him." Shiva is often said to wear on his body the ashes of the burnt Kama. Kama is resuscitated when Shiva embraces Parvati and the sweat from her body mingles with the ashes of the burned god. Elsewhere it is said that when Kama was burned by Shiva he entered the limbs of Parvati.

In the most general terms, Shiva as Agni is the fire that destroys the world at the end of each cosmic age. Parvati as Soma, in contrast, is the cosmic waters from which the world is inevitably reborn. As the great cosmic dancer Shiva periodically performs the tandava, an especially violent dance. Wielding a broken battle-axe, he dances so wildly that the cosmos is destroyed completely. His whirling arms and flying locks are said to crash into the heavenly bodies, knocking them off course or destroying them utterly. The mountains shake and the oceans heave as the world is destroyed by his violent dancing. Parvati, in contrast, is portrayed as a patient builder who follows Shiva about, trying to soften the violent effects of her husband. She is a great force for preservation and reconstruction in the world and as such offsets his violence. A seventeenth-century Tamil work pictures Parvati as a patient child who creates the worlds in the form of little houses. Shiva is pictured as constantly frustrating her purpose by destroying what she has so carefully built:

The crazy old madman stands in front,
dancing, destroying the beautiful little house that you have built in play.
You don't become angry, but every time he destroys it you build it again.

When Shiva does his violent tandava dance Parvati is described as calming him with soft glances, or she is said to complement his violence with a slow, creative step of her own.

Parvati's goal in her relationship with Shiva is nothing less than the domestication of the lone, ascetic god whose behavior borders on madness. Shiva is indifferent to social propriety, does not care about offspring, declares women to be a hindrance to the spiritual life, and is disdainful of the trappings of the householder's life. Parvati tries to involve him in the worldly life of the householder by arguing that he should observe conventions if he loves her and wants her. She persuades him to marry her according to the proper rituals, to observe custom, instead of simply running off with her. She is less successful in getting him to change his attire and ascetic habits. She often complains of his nakedness and finds his ornaments disgraceful. Parvati sometimes complains that she does not have a proper house to live in. Shiva, as is well known, does not have a house but prefers to live in caves, on mountains, or in forests, or to wander the world as a homeless beggar. Many myths delight in Shiva's response to Parvati's domestic pleas for a house. When she complains that the rains will soon come and that she has no house to protect her, Shiva simply takes her to the high mountain peaks above the clouds where it does not rain. Elsewhere he describes his "house" as the universe and argues that an ascetic understands the whole world to be his dwelling place. These philosophic arguments never satisfy Parvati, but she rarely, if ever, wins this argument and gains a house. Despite her success in involving Shiva in marital and family affairs, despite her initiating sexual desire in him, Shiva always remains in part antisocial and ascetic, a god who lives on the fringes of society.

The theme of Parvati's domesticating Shiva is also seen in her tempering, or taming, his tapas and his sexual vigor, both of which are dangerous in excess. Shiva, too, does excessive tapas and thus threatens the world, and it is Parvati's role to distract him from his asceticism or seduce him into erotic or domestic entanglements. In many myths Parvati is not so much Shiva's complement as his rival, tricking, seducing, or luring him away from ascetic practices. Shiva's sexual vigor is also threatening to the world in quantity and intensity, and again it is Parvati who subdues, tames, or otherwise controls Shiva's immense sexual vitality.

The sages cursed Shiva's linga to fall to the earth, and it burnt everything before it like a fire. Never still, it went to the underworld and to heaven and everywhere on earth. All creatures were troubled, and the sages went in desperation to Brahma, who said to them, "As long as the linga is not still, there will be nothing auspicious in the universe. You must propitiate Devi so that she will take the form of the yoni, and then the linga will become still." They honoured Shiva, and he appeared and said, "If my linga is held in the yoni, then all will be well. Only Parvati can hold the linga, and then it will become calm." They propitiated him, and thus linga-worship was established.

Shiva is a god of excesses, both ascetic and sexual, and Parvati plays the role of modifier. As a representative of the householder ideal she represents the ideal of controlled sex — married sex — which is opposed to both asceticism and eroticism.

Shiva-Shakti, Ardhanarishvara, and the Linga-Yoni

The conflict between the way of the ascetic and the way of the householder yields to a vision of reconciliation, interdependence, and symbiotic harmony in three images that are central to the mythology, iconography, and philosophy of Parvati: the theme of Shiva-Shakti, the image of Shiva as Ardhanarishvara (the Lord who is half woman), and the image of the linga and yoni.

The idea that the great male gods all possess an inherent power by which they undertake creative activity is assumed in medieval Hindu mythology. When this power, or shakti, is personified it is always in the form of a goddess. Parvati, quite naturally, assumes the identity of Shiva's shakti in many myths and in some philosophic systems. In this role she is often identified with the force underlying and impelling creation. While Shiva remains more or less aloof in the creation of the world, Parvati as shakti is active, pervading the creation as its underlying strength and power. In this active, creative role she is sometimes identified with prakrti (nature), whereas Shiva is identified with purusa (pure spirit). As prakrti, Parvati represents the inherent tendency of nature to express itself in concrete forms and individual beings. Her role as his shakti is almost always interpreted as positive. Through Parvati, Shiva (the Absolute) is able to express himself in the creation. Without her he would remain inert, aloof, inactive. Parvati as shakti not only complements Shiva, she completes him.

A variety of images and metaphors are used to express the harmonious interdependence and close identity of Parvati as shakti and Shiva as the saktiman, the possessor of shakti. Shiva is said to be the male principle throughout creation, Parvati the female principle; Shiva is the sky, Parvati the earth; Shiva is subject, Parvati object; Shiva is the ocean, Parvati the seashore; Shiva is the sun, Parvati its light; Parvati is all tastes and smells, Shiva the enjoyer of all tastes and smells; Parvati is the embodiment of all individual souls, Shiva the soul itself; Shiva is day, Parvati night; Parvati is creation, Shiva the creator; Parvati is speech, Shiva meaning. In short, the two are actually one — different aspects of ultimate reality — and as such are complementary, not antagonistic.

The Ardhanarishvara image shows a half-male, half-female figure. The right side is Shiva and is adorned with his ornaments; the left side is Parvati and adorned with hers. In the Shiva-purana the god Brahma is unable to continue his task of creation because the creatures that he has produced do not multiply. He propitiates Shiva and requests his aid. Shiva then appears in his half-male, half-female form. The hermaphrodite form splits into Shiva and Parvati, and Parvati, at Brahma's request, pervades the creation with her female nature, which duly awakens the male aspect of creation into fertile activity. Without its female half the godhead as Shiva is incomplete and unable to proceed with creation. In this form the godhead transcends sexual particularity — or, more accurately, includes both dimensions of it. God is both male and female, both father and mother, both aloof and active, both fearsome and gentle, both destructive and constructive.

The image of the linga in the yoni, which is the most common image of the deity in Shiva temples, similarly teaches that the tension between Shiva and Parvati is ultimately resolved in interdependence. Sati and Parvati as sexual partners succeed in tempering both Shiva's excessive detachment from the world and his excessive sexual vigor. In the form of the yoni in particular, Sati-Parvati fulfills and completes, while at the same time tempering, Shiva's creative tendencies. As the great yogi who accumulates immense sexual potency he is symbolized by the linga. This great potency is creatively released in sexual or marital contact with Sati-Parvati. The ubiquitous image of the linga in the yoni symbolizes the creative release — in the ultimate erotic act — of power stored through asceticism. The erotic act is thus enhanced, made more potent, fecund, and creative, by the stored-up power of Shiva's asceticism. The linga and the yoni symbolize a creative interaction between the world of the ascetic, in which sexual abstinence is mandatory, and the life of the householder, in which sex is necessary.

In relationship with Parvati, Shiva does not give up asceticism entirely, nor does Parvati give up asceticism entirely after having used it as a means of marrying Shiva. Nonetheless, their mutual bliss also seems to teach that asceticism enhances the intensity of sexuality and makes the orderliness of the householder's world even more attractive. Held together, or in creative tension, yoga and bhoga (worldly or bodily pleasure), dharma and moksha, complement and complete each other in the divine pair.

Devotion and Grace

Another important facet of Parvati is her role as a model of devotion to Shiva, as the devotee of the Lord par excellence. At several points in her mythology Parvati is described as devoted to Shiva, unswerving in her attachment to him, and incapable of being dissuaded from doting on him despite the most outrageous reports concerning his uncivilized, bizarre habits. Her remarkably steadfast, indeed stubborn, devotion to him is most obvious during her long period of asceticism, when she undertakes heroic bodily mortifications in order to be granted the boon of having Shiva for her husband or to attract his attention directly. In these scenes Parvati is portrayed as a devotee who seeks Shiva's attention and blessing, and it is easy to see how she might become the paradigm for devotees of Shiva — which she does, for example, in the devotional hymns of Manikkavacakar, a Shaivite saint from Tamil Nadu.

Parvati appears in Manikkavacakar's hymns in the role of the ideal devotee in at least two ways. First, Manikkavacakar uses the imagery of a love relationship, casting himself in the role of a woman who longs for her lover or husband, Shiva. In placing himself and by extension other devotees in this role, he identifies himself with Shiva's beloved, Parvati, who thus becomes the model, or vehicle, for devotion. In this role Manikkavacakar imagines himself, along with women and goddesses, to be doing domestic chores for her husband. He approaches Shiva by assuming the role and duties of Parvati, and in this way is able to relate to Shiva in a most intimate way. Second, Manikkavacakar refers several times to the half-male, half-female image of Shiva and Parvati and sees in it the ultimate goal of the devotee: to be inextricably united with Shiva. In his longing to be with Shiva, to be consumed by and overcome by his Lord, Manikkavacakar sees in this image his desire graphically realized by Parvati.

Parvati tends to have a calming, civilizing effect on Shiva. Under her influence or in her presence, Shiva is often tamed, distracted from his wild, rude, mad behavior. In Manikkavacakar's hymns, Shiva never appears before Parvati in his outrageous forms, such as the dancer in the cremation ground or the destroyer of Daksha's sacrifice. In her presence such behavior is inappropriate, and Shiva almost always behaves in properly domestic ways. Becalmed, he is more attentive to the needs of his devotees and more prone to grant them blessings. As the domesticator of Shiva, as the one who is able to distract him from his antisocial behavior and make him turn his attention to the world and to his devotees within it, Parvati plays the role of a mediator between the devotee and Shiva. She is the one who awakens his grace, the key to activating it. Immersed in yoga, preoccupied with asceticism, dancing wildly with his ghastly companions in the cremation ground, Shiva is indifferent to his spouse, to the world, and to his devotees. In the presence of Parvati, in his role as her husband, he is attentive to the world and to his devotees.

Although Parvati's role as intermediary is never developed in Shaivism to the extent that Sri-Lakshmi's role is developed in Sri Vaishnavism, Parvati does come to be identified with Shiva's grace in Shaiva Siddhanta, a Tamil Shaivite school of thought and devotion. In this school Shiva's grace (arul) is said to play an active role, almost to have an identity of its own or an independent function. In its active role it is referred to as arul-shakti, the power of grace. This power is sometimes personified, and when it is, it is identified with Parvati. Furthermore, the arul-shakti is often said to reside inherently in every human being. Devotion is the means by which this power is awakened and provoked. The implication is that Parvati, in subtle form, resides within every human being. She is awakened by means of devotion to Shiva, which expresses her most essential nature. Having been awakened, she infuses the devotee with her great devotion to Shiva, and thus the two — Parvati and the devotee — merge into one. In this way Parvati's roles as ideal devotee and as Shiva's personified grace merge into a unity.

Finally, Parvati often plays the role of the student in relation to Shiva. In many texts, especially Tantras, Parvati inquires of Shiva concerning a great many subjects: ritual, meditation, mythology, dharma, and philosophy. At her prompting or in response to her queries, Shiva reveals everything from the particulars of esoteric Tantric rituals to the nondual wisdom of Vedanta. Again, it is Parvati who succeeds in capturing Shiva's attention and awakening his concern for the world, so that his great wisdom and knowledge, gained by his heroic eons of yogic meditation and brooding, can be revealed to humanity. In the role of curious student Parvati represents human beings who are anxious for and will benefit from all that Shiva reveals. In this role she might again be seen as a kind of mediator, one who coaxes from Shiva what is ultimately beneficial for human beings. Although Parvati is not the only one to whom Shiva gives instruction, she is by far the most common figure cast in this role.

Manifestation

She is extremely beautiful but can take many forms from the seductive to the terrible. She may appear as a dark-skinned or fair-skinned woman.
