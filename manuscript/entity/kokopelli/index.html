Kokopelli

◇Culture
Pueblo IndianDinéZuni
◇Type
Deity

Overview

Kokopelli, whose name in Hopi is said to mean "wooden backed," is a figure that first appears in the prehistoric rock carvings of the American Southwest: a hunchbacked little character with antlers on his head, playing a flute. The true age, origin, and meaning of the symbol are uncertain, but Kokopelli is widely understood as a fertility figure, with the antlers and the small bag he sometimes carries, perhaps full of seeds, marking him as the essence of the creative force in whatever form it takes.

A Hopi Legend

One Hopi story tells how Kokopelli wandered from village to village playing his flute. Wherever he arrived he was welcomed with a great celebration, the people singing and dancing through the night. By morning he had shared his powers of fecundity: the fields stood full of healthy crops, and the young women would soon find themselves with child. Kokopelli also marks the end of winter and the coming of spring, carrying with him the sense of hope and new life.

The Flute and Its Power

Earlier, less filtered depictions of Kokopelli portray him with a prominent phallus alongside his characteristic flute, the two forming a paired expression of the male creative force. In one sense the flute is the phallus rendered as music: the generative power transformed into sound capable of chasing away winter, heralding the spring, and calling the rains that make the earth fertile. Kokopelli also presided over the reproduction of the animals that sustained his people, organising the creative forces that governed the lives of the creatures they hunted. His magickal musical gifts were renowned, and young women sometimes feared him because of the babies he was said to distribute from the sack on his back.

An Ancient Presence

The earliest known effigies of Kokopelli date to around 1000 CE, placing his origins in the ancient Pueblo world. In more recent times he has enjoyed a considerable resurgence in popular culture, his dancing silhouette appearing on T-shirts, caps, and souvenirs of the American Southwest.

A Living Tradition

Kokopelli belongs to the living traditions of the Hopi and other Pueblo peoples. These are closed Indigenous practices, so the figure is best approached with respect rather than borrowed or appropriated.
