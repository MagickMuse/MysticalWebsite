Also known as

Carmentes, Carmentis, Nicostrate

Overview

Carmenta is a Roman goddess of childbirth and prophecy, honored in her singular form as Carmenta and in her plural form as the Carmentes, in which register she is joined by her attendants Porrima, who looks to the past, and Postverta, who looks to the future. She is a very old Italic figure, older than the fully developed Roman religion, and her cult is one of the older public cults of Rome; her flamen, the flamen Carmentalis, is one of the minor flamens of the Roman religious college, and her festival, the Carmentalia, was celebrated on the eleventh and fifteenth of January.

Classification

Roman goddess of childbirth and prophecy. Very old Italic figure, honored with her own flamen and her own festival, and grouped in the plural as the Carmentes with her attendants Porrima and Postverta.

Domains

Safe passage of the newborn from the darkness of the womb into the light of the world, prophecy and the reading of destiny, the memory of what has passed and the foresight of what is to come, protection of women in labor, the initiation of the newborn into the community of the living.

Iconography

Represented as a matronly Roman woman, sometimes crowned with beans, which were sacred to her, and holding attributes appropriate to her twin office of childbirth and prophecy. Her plural form as the Carmentes is imagined as a triad of veiled goddesses attending the moment of birth together, one holding the newborn, one looking backward, and one looking forward.

Attributes

Beans, the newborn wrapped for presentation, prophetic tablets or scrolls, the doorway across which the newborn is carried, the offering charms of the birth ceremony.

Sacred Sites

Her chief sanctuary at Rome was the temple at the foot of the Capitoline Hill, near the Porta Carmentalis, the gate that was named after her. The Carmentalia was celebrated at this sanctuary. In the domestic sphere her presence belongs to the birthing chamber alongside Lucina and Candelifera.

Ritual

Honored publicly at the Carmentalia on January 11 and 15, and honored privately in the family cult of childbirth alongside Lucina, whose office is the light of birth, and Candelifera, whose office is the candle carried to welcome the newborn. Iron was forbidden at her sanctuary, a taboo consistent with her old Italic character and with the wider Mediterranean pattern of iron avoidance in birth rites. Offerings appropriate to her cult include beans, cakes, wine, and the small charms brought by women who have safely delivered.

Lore

Carmenta was said in the Roman legendary history to have come to Italy from Arcadia with her son Evander, who founded a small settlement on the future site of Rome. Her Greek name in that story was Nicostrate, meaning victorious army, and she was said to have adapted the Greek alphabet into the earliest Latin letters, a story that gathered her prophetic office into the older office of the maker of writing. In her office as the Carmentes she stands as one of a triad of birth goddesses beside her attendants Porrima and Postverta, so that the moment of birth is watched by a goddess looking at the past, a goddess looking at the future, and Carmenta herself standing at the present threshold. This threefold guardianship gives Roman childbirth its distinctive temporal character, in which the newborn is welcomed not only into the present moment but into the whole line of time on either side.
