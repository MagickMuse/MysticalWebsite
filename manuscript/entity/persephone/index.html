The Abducted Bride

Persephone's modern claim to fame is as Hades' abducted bride. Hades knew Demeter, Persephone's devoted mother, would never agree to their marriage and so went behind her back, made private arrangements with Zeus, Persephone's father, and then just kidnapped the young girl to bring her to his subterranean palace.

They did not figure on Demeter's persistence. She refused to accept Persephone's disappearance but searched for her tirelessly, eventually engaging in a strike that brought people and deities to their knees. (Demeter is goddess of agriculture. When she strikes, there is no food. When people starve, they stop making offerings to deities who then, in turn, starve.) Eventually a deal was negotiated.

Persephone would have returned to the upper world completely except that she had eaten six pomegranate seeds from Hades' garden. Since she had eaten the fruit of the dead, Persephone spends half the year as Hades' queen and half the year with her mother. Together Demeter and Persephone preside over the Mysteries at Eleusis, the most famous Mysteries of the ancient world.

The Ancient Goddess of Death

Some accounts name her parents as Zeus and Demeter; others substitute Styx, the nymph of the underworld river, as her mother, which would make Persephone a creature of the underworld realm by blood as much as by circumstance.

The myth makes Persephone seem like a pawn fought over by the more experienced Demeter and Hades. However, scholars suspect that Persephone, whose name may be interpreted as "destroying face," is an ancient goddess of death. Persephone may be the original ruler of Hades. In order to obtain his throne and establish rulership, Hades may have had to kidnap her in the same manner that Poseidon raped Amphitrite and Zeus tricked Hera into marriage. Persephone may actually be more ancient than Demeter or Hades with some scholars placing her origin as early as the Neolithic era.

When Persephone returns to Earth from Hades, she is accompanied by a procession of dancing Charites, Horae, and Moirae, three groups of three spirits: nine beautiful dancers.

Powers and Blessings

Persephone is invoked to find true, new or lasting love. She owns a box of beauty, which she may lend out to other goddesses and devotees: she is requested to enhance beauty and charisma. Persephone banishes ghosts and facilitates communication with dead souls. She is invoked for painless, peaceful death.

The pomegranate carries layered symbolism: fertility, sexual awakening, and irreversible change. Her eating of the seed has long been read as a euphemism for the loss of her virginity and her binding to adult life and its full weight of consequence.

Persephone's Realm: Darkness and Agricultural Seasons

The abduction of Persephone did not begin underground; it began in a meadow full of violets. It was while she gathered those flowers that Hades emerged from a fissure in the earth and seized her, drawing her down to reign beside him as queen of the dead. Her mother Demeter, goddess of agriculture, was consumed by grief so absolute that she withdrew her gifts from the world. Crops failed. Plant life withered. Humanity edged toward starvation. The devastation was severe enough that Zeus himself intervened and negotiated Persephone's return.

Her freedom, however, came with a condition she had unknowingly set herself: while in the underworld she had eaten pomegranate seeds, and in doing so had bound herself to that realm. She returns to the upper world for seven months of the year, the growing seasons, and retreats to rule the dead for the remainder. Every winter is an echo of Demeter's grief; every spring is the reunion of mother and daughter.

In magickal understanding, this myth reveals the intimate relationship between darkness and fertility. Without descent, there is no return. Without the season of death, there is no season of growth. Persephone's story teaches that darkness is not the opposite of life; it is its necessary threshold. The witch who works with Persephone works with this liminal truth: that power comes from moving between worlds, that beauty can exist in shadow, and that the queen who was taken became, in time, one of the most sovereign figures in the Greek pantheon.

The Lesser Mysteries

The Lesser Mysteries, celebrated each spring and dedicated to Persephone, were understood by the ancient theologians to signify the condition of the unpurified soul clothed in an earthly body and wrapped in material nature. In this reading Persephone is the soul itself, which belongs to the higher worlds of spirit, while the body is what Plato called the "sepulcher" of the soul. Her fall into the Underworld stands for the soul's descent into the lower, material world, and her acceptance of the pomegranate for its choice to be bound by earthly things.

At the heart of these secrets lay a stern teaching: a person would be no better in death than in life if they did not use their time on Earth to rise above ignorance. Whoever failed might be doomed to repeat their mistakes for eternity, their soul sleeping in Hades as they had slept, unaware, while living. The work was for the living spirit to overcome its dead, animal nature. Another of the tenets held the deep evil of suicide, its consequences too awful to be spoken aloud.

After initiation a candidate was named a "Mystes," one whose vision was still clouded, who viewed the world through a veil. It is the root of the word "mystic," and it carried the promise that the veil could one day be drawn aside. That final clarity was reserved for initiation into the Greater Mysteries, which Persephone shared with her mother Demeter at Eleusis.

The Fairyland Parallel

In fairy tales across many cultures the same principle holds: never eat the food of another realm, whether the land of the dead or the world of the fairies, for to accept such food is to accept that world's claim on you. Persephone's story is one of the oldest articulations of this idea, which has carried intact through thousands of years of storytelling.

In Witchcraft

Persephone and Euridice are classical expressions of the same symbolic current that runs through the enchantresses and fays of medieval legend: Melusine, Nimue, Morgan le Fay, Viviane, Brisen, and the Lady of the Lake. All share the pattern of the Lady of Delight, the governing feminine power of love and the threshold between the living and dead worlds. Persephone's descent into the underworld during the first three months of the witches' year (November through January, when the Lord of the Dead holds rule) and her return with spring make her the living image of this polarity. She is associated with the chalice current in love workings and with the symbolism of receptive, lunar, enchanting power.
