Overview

Ratri is the Hindu Vedic goddess of night. Her name and her nature both belong to the dark hours, and the hymns treat her as the night in person rather than as a deity who merely presides over it. People call on her for solace and safety during the stretch of darkness that precedes daybreak. She is, on the whole, a benign figure whose presence keeps creatures at rest, sustains life through the dew, and pushes back the harm that moves under cover of night, though the same texts also remember a colder, more dangerous side to her.

In Vedic literature

Night herself, petitioned for comfort

Ratri is so closely identified with the night that the two cannot really be separated. She is the dark expanse itself, and as the dark expanse she is the presence that worshipers turn to for solace and safety through the hours before the bright return of dawn. Her body and face are seldom described. When her appearance is mentioned at all she is pictured as a lovely young woman, sometimes alongside her sister Ushas, the dawn. She is praised as immortal and glorious, and she is honored for casting light through the dark by means of the uncountable stars that adorn her.

Benign presence

The usual picture of Ratri is a gentle one. She is praised for granting rest to every living thing. She is praised for letting fall the dew that keeps life going. Together with Ushas she is said to bestow and reinforce the powers that sustain life. In each of these roles she appears as a sheltering, life-favoring presence, the quiet counterpart to the busy daytime work of the other gods.

Guardian against nocturnal dangers

Ratri is invoked in a particular way for the dangers that belong especially to nighttime. She is asked to drive off wolves, to ward off thieves, and to keep at bay any being that might bring harm during the dark hours. The hymns address her as a protective presence positioned between sleeping people and whatever might prey on them.

Shadow side

Even though Ratri's nature is mostly kindly, some texts speak of her in negative terms or link her with things hostile to people. In the Rg-veda she is driven off by Agni, the fire god, and also by her sister Ushas. She is called barren and called gloomy when set against the brilliant, generously giving Ushas. On occasion she is identified with the very creatures or dangers of the night from which, elsewhere, she is asked to protect people. Ratri is therefore not only the warden of the night and the keeper of people through the dark span of their rest, but also the night in person, holding within herself both the helpful and the dangerous beings that move through it.

Twin of Ushas

The greater part of the Rg-vedic references to Ratri pair her with Ushas, who is named as her sister. Most often the two are imagined as two beautiful young women, sometimes as twin sisters. Together they are called powerful mothers and strengtheners of vital power. They are also called weavers of time and mothers of eternal law. In their alternating, cyclical, never-ending comings and goings they stand for the steady, recurring rhythm of the cosmic order, in which light gives way to dark and dark to light along a path that can be counted on. Together they illustrate the well-ordered shape of the created world: the patterned shift between activity and rest, between light and dark, and the steady passage of time.

A note on naming

The Ushas page in this grimoire names her night-sister Rati, following the source tradition recorded there. In scholarly Sanskrit usage the goddess of night is properly Ratri, while Rati is a separate Hindu deity, the consort of Kama and goddess of love and desire. Both records are preserved here so the parallel naming is not lost, and readers can hold the two traditions side by side without one overwriting the other.
