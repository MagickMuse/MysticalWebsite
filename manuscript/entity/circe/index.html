Magickal Properties

Circe is the original divine witch, a goddess of tremendous power whose very name is synonymous with 'sorceress.' Daughter of Helios and Perse (an Oceanid), she comes from a family of divine enchantresses including Hekate, Pasiphae, Ariadne, and Medea. She's a master shapeshifter most famous for transforming others, revealing their true animal identity within.

Ritual Uses

Invoke Circe for powerful transformation magick and shapeshifting abilities. She excels at revealing hidden truths and true natures. Work with her for shamanic journeying, especially to underworld realms. She can teach advanced magickal techniques and grant magickal power.

Offerings

Wine (she's known for her hospitality), pork (sacred to her transformations), magickal herbs like mandrakes and enchanter's nightshade, juniper and alder, magickal tools and implements.

Favored People

Sorceresses and witches, shapeshifters and magical practitioners, those seeking advanced magickal knowledge, shamans and psychopomps, anyone needing to reveal or understand true natures.

Petition For

Magickal power and advanced sorcery skills, shapeshifting and transformation abilities, shamanic journeying and underworld work, revealing true natures and hidden identities, protection during magickal work.

Warnings

She transforms visitors into animals that reflect their true nature, approach with honest self-awareness. Her magick is profound and permanent. As descendant of the sun, her eyes glint with brilliant light, that's the telltale sign of her identity.

Additional Notes

Lives on Isle of Aiaia in marble palazzo, spends days singing and weaving like the Fates. Her transformations aren't random, they reveal truth. She taught Odysseus shamanism and underworld navigation. Her sacred animals are pigs.

Circeium, the Mandrake of Circe

The ancient Greeks called the mandrake root Circeium in Circe's honor, marking it as the signature herb of her poison plant path. Alongside deadly nightshade and henbane, mandrake belongs to her dominion, and the root carries her name wherever it grows.

Weaving Destiny at the Loom

Circe spends her days at her loom in her island palazzo, spinning and weaving. This is no mere domestic craft, she is understood to be magickally weaving destiny itself while crafting tapestries. She is frequently portrayed at her loom in classical art, and through her association, spinning and weaving became explicitly linked to witchcraft and the weaving of fate. Like the Fates themselves, her textile work operates on both mundane and magical planes simultaneously.

Divine Origins

Circe's very name is synonymous with "sorceress." Daughter of the Sun (Helios) and an ocean spirit (Perse), she is clearly divine, Homer calls her "the fair-haired goddess." Her name derives from the same root words as "circle" and "falcon" (falcons notably circle in the sky). Circe's name also resembles kerkis, which means "weaver's shuttle."

The Isle of Aiaia

According to Homer's Odyssey, Circe dwells in a marble palazzo on the Isle of Aiaia (also spelled Aeaea), where she was banished after poisoning her husband, the King of the Sarmatians. She does travel though: according to other reports, she lives in Lazio, a region of west-central Italy. She spends her days singing and weaving, habits associated with the Fates.

Transformations and True Nature

Circe is a shape-shifter but is most famous for transforming others. When Odysseus and his crew land on Aiaia while trying to return home from the Trojan War, they discover an island paradise ruled by the goddess and populated by beautiful female handmaidens and strangely human-seeming wild animals.

Circe transforms her male visitors into animals, lions, baboons, and others, but mainly pigs. One might say that Circe reveals the true animal identity hidden within each man. Odysseus alone is saved from this fate when Hermes warns him, offering him an herbal antidote to Circe's magick, a mysterious plant called moly. Hermes also advises Odysseus not to reject Circe's advances.

Shaman and Teacher

Ultimately Odysseus stays with Circe for years, fathering her son Telegonus. Circe initiates Odysseus into shamanism, advising him how to journey to Hades, interview dead souls, and return. She is his primary tutor. Foretelling the future, she offers Odysseus invaluable advice, ultimately enabling him to return home. Without Circe, Odysseus would never have reached his home again.

Teacher of Sorcery

Hekate may be Circe's aunt or her sister. Circe takes the form of a falcon when she wishes to fly, and since she transforms others into earthly beasts, she unites the elements of earth, fire, air, and water. She likewise unites many schools of magick, ranking among the world's masters of sorcery, potions, necromancy, love philters, transformation, and revenge magick. A fierce, clever spirit with a sharp sense of humor, she shares her secrets only with worthy students and must respect a practitioner before she will help. She teaches those ready for advanced study, not beginners. She appears as a beautiful woman or as a circling hawk. See Circe's School of Sorcery.

Ritual

A bouquet of deadly nightshade is said to call Circe and signal that her help is needed to contact those who have passed over. Pair the bouquet with visualization.

⚠️ Deadly nightshade is lethally toxic. Handle it with great care.

Cultural Impact

Circe inspired what is historically considered the first ballet in 1581. Catherine de Medici, mother of the French king and an alleged sorceress herself, sponsored a dance company, La Ballet Comique de la Reine, whose first production was an over six-hour-long extravaganza featuring dance, songs, and elaborate floats devoted to the saga of Circe.

Although there are relatively few myths involving Circe, she captured the heart of artists from the classical era until today. She is a frequent character in literature, movies, and comic books, and perhaps the most popular witch among fine artists. During the nineteenth century in particular, many artists painted portraits of Circe.
