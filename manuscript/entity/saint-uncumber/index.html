Classification

Roman Catholic saint (suppressed 1969).

Life and Legend

The wife of a Pagan king of Portugal bore septuplets. All seven girls secretly converted to Christianity. One sister was betrothed to the king of Sicily, but unbeknownst to her father, she had already taken a private vow of chastity.

Unlike Saint Lucy or Saint Triduana, who found themselves in similar situations and took matters into their own hands, this young princess declined to act. Instead, she prayed to God to be saved. She begged to be made so repulsive that no one would ever wish to marry her.

God heard her prayers and blessed her with excessive facial hair. Her fiancé took one look at her full beard and mustache and withdrew his proposal. The princess's father was so enraged that he had her crucified.

The Deathbed Vow

Her last words before dying on the cross were a blessing: she promised that all who remembered her would be freed from their troubles and encumbrances. They would be unencumbered. That princess is now known as Saint Uncumber.

Origin of Veneration

Veneration of Saint Uncumber first emerged in fourteenth-century Flanders. Scholars suggest that Northern Europeans were confused by an unusual Italian crucifix depicting a gowned, crowned, bearded Christ. The figure on the cross appeared feminine to them and, despite the beard, was assumed to be a young royal woman. Whether or not devotion to Saint Uncumber was initially based on a case of mistaken identity, numerous miracles were soon attributed to her. Her veneration spread, and during the medieval era she was a popular saint throughout Europe.

The Many Names

This saint's actual name is unknown. In addition to Uncumber, she is known as Wilgefortis and Liberata.

Wilgefortis derives from virgo fortis, meaning "strong virgin" or "determined virgin."

Uncumber and its variants derive from her deathbed vow and because, allegedly, in exchange for minor offerings, she was guaranteed to uncumber a woman of her husband.

Churchmen wishing to Latinize "Uncumber" called her Liberata, using "liberate" as a synonym for "unencumber." In the medieval era, the word was "uncumber."

Confusion with Saint Liberata

Saint Uncumber-Liberata was conflated with the Spanish Saint Liberata, and her legend is heavily influenced by that of the earlier Spanish saint. It can be very difficult to distinguish between them. The two share the same feast day.

Domains

She is invoked to protect women against spousal abuse. Officially, she is invoked by those trapped in unhappy unions. Unofficially, she is invoked by women desperate to unencumber themselves of difficult husbands, one way or another.

Sir Thomas More wrote that "for a peck of oats," Saint Uncumber "would provide a horse for an evil husband to ride to the devil upon."

Lore

One man's fascination with Saint Uncumber is a pivotal part of the plot of Robertson Davies' 1970 novel Fifth Business.

Iconography

A crucified woman wearing a crown. Sometimes she is bearded, sometimes not.

Sacred Sites

Offerings were traditionally made to Uncumber at her shrine in Saint Paul's Cathedral in London. She once had shrines throughout Europe, some of which still remain.

Related Entities

Saint Liberata, Saint Lucy, Saint Triduana.
