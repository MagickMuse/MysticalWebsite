Bor is an archetypal god of Nordic cosmogony, standing in the earliest generations of the divine lineage as the son of Buri and the father of the Aesir. According to the account preserved by Snorri Sturluson in the Prose Edda, Bor is the crucial middle figure through whom the line of the gods descends from the primordial ice of Ginnungagap to the great sovereign order of Odin's rule.

The Cosmogonic Line

At the beginning of things a living creature called Ymir was formed in the misty void of Ginnungagap, at the meeting of the fires of Muspellsheimr with the rime of Niflheimr. Ymir was nourished by the milk of the great cow Audhumla, who came into being alongside him. Audhumla in turn fed herself by licking the salty blocks of primordial ice, and through her patient licking she released a second individual, called Buri, from within the ice.

Buri had a son named Bor. Bor took as his wife Bestla, daughter of the frost-giant Bolthorn, and from their union came three sons: Odin, Vili, and Ve. These three, the grandsons of Buri and the sons of Bor, slew Ymir and shaped the ordered cosmos from his body. Bor stands in the mythology as the necessary link between the ice-born ancestor Buri and the great triad of world-makers.

Domains

The generative line by which the gods came into being, the middle ground between the primordial cosmos and the ordered world, and the fatherly authority through which the sovereign order of the Aesir was first brought into being.

Iconography

Bor is not typically depicted in the visual record. He belongs, like his father Buri, to the cosmogonic stratum of Nordic myth rather than to the pantheon of ordinarily worshipped deities. His name is preserved in the poetic phrasing of the Eddas as Borr, and he is remembered principally as the father through whom the Aesir order was made possible.

Precautions

Bor is a cosmogonic figure rather than a devotional deity in the ordinary sense. His mythology belongs to the level of the world's making, and his figure functions in the tradition as the bearer of an origin story rather than as a personal patron to be petitioned.
