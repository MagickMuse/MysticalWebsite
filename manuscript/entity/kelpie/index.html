The kelpie is a Scottish water horse, a shape-shifting spirit who haunts the rivers, lochs, and coastal pools of the Highlands and Lowlands alike. The kelpie shares much with its more dangerous cousin, the Each Uisge, and the two are sometimes confused or conflated in folklore.

Twin Natures

The Bringer of Seaweed

In its gentler aspect, the kelpie carries seaweed up from the sea to inland fields and nourishes the crops along the coast. Farmers along certain stretches of the Scottish shoreline historically counted the kelpie as part of the soil's fertility, the unseen hand that fed the land each season.

The Drowner

That kindness does not extend to riders. A kelpie appearing as a beautiful, prancing horse near the water's edge is rarely as friendly as it looks. Mount the kelpie and it will plunge straight into the deepest part of the loch or river, dragging the rider down to drown and, often, to be devoured.

Detecting a Kelpie

A kelpie in horse form may be detected by the green water-weeds tangled in its mane or about its hooves. In human form, hooves still hide in the boots and damp sand or seaweed still clings to the hair. A wise traveler refuses to approach any strange horse grazing alone beside a still lake at dusk.

Working With Them

Kelpies are not entities to court. The wisest tradition is to leave them their water and their crops, and to teach children to keep clear of the loch's edge at dusk and through the night.

Kindred and Offspring

The kelpie has close kin elsewhere: the Pooka of Irish myth, the Ceffyl Dwr of Wales, and the Nix of Scandinavian story, who shares the same drowning trick. Though it most often appears as a pure white horse, the kelpie may also take the form of a seal. It is said the kelpie can breed with humans, and the child of such a union is a beautiful, fleet-footed creature.
