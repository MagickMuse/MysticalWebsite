Mabon🐰Ostara

Son; Young Man; The Boy

The Divine Child

Mabon, a hunter spirit whose myth appears in the Welsh epic tale of "Culhwch and Olwen," is the son of Modron, whose name literally means "mother." There's no mention of any father. Mabon, child of Modron literally means "Son, child of Mother." He is the divine child, and like so many divine children he was menaced, then rescued.

The Great Captivity

Stolen from his mother at three nights old, Mabon disappeared for many years, held captive in the Underworld. King Arthur and his cousin, Welsh hero Culhwch, searched for him, finally discovering and rescuing him from Gloucester Castle. By the time they released him from his enchanted imprisonment, Mabon was the eldest of all living creatures.

Mabon is a paradox: simultaneously the youngest and oldest of souls.

Connection to Maponus

Mabon is generally considered to be identical to Maponus who was venerated in Britain and Gaul. The Romans identified Maponus with Apollo. His name is invoked on ancient curse tablets, demonstrating his power extended beyond benevolent realms into protective and potentially wrathful magick.

Sacred Sites

  • Roman soldiers venerated Apollo Maponus near Hadrian's Wall
  • Maponus' sanctuary at Chamalières, now modern France, featured a therapeutic spring, connecting him to healing waters

The Harvest Festival

Mabon, the harvest festival and Wiccan sabbat, coincides with the autumn equinox. The festival bears his name because it celebrates themes of loss, search, rescue, and the balance between light and dark, all reflected in Mabon's myth.

Magickal Properties

Mabon embodies the paradox of eternal youth and ancient wisdom. As the divine child stolen into the Underworld, he represents:

  • The cycle of loss and recovery
  • The balance between youth and age
  • The autumn harvest when life descends into darkness but promises return
  • The hunter's skill and connection to wild nature
  • The power of rescue and restoration

Manifestation

Mabon manifests as simultaneously a young boy (the stolen infant, the eternal youth) and an ancient wise being (the oldest of living creatures). This dual nature makes him uniquely suited to understanding both beginnings and endings.

Ritual Uses

Invoke Mabon for:

  • Finding what is lost (particularly children or that which was stolen)
  • Understanding paradoxes and contradictions
  • Autumn equinox rituals and harvest celebrations
  • Hunting magick and connection to wild game
  • Balancing youth and wisdom, beginning and ending
  • Rescue from captivity (literal or metaphorical)
  • Understanding time's mysteries

The autumn equinox celebration of Mabon honors his rescue from the Underworld and the themes of light descending into darkness with the promise of eventual return.

Lore

Mabon's story appears in the Welsh tale "Culhwch and Olwen" as part of King Arthur's quest to help Culhwch win his bride. The search for Mabon involves consulting with the oldest animals: the Blackbird of Cilgwri, the Stag of Rhedynfre, the Owl of Cwm Cawlwyd, the Eagle of Gwernabwy, and finally the Salmon of Llyn Llyw, each older than the last, until the Salmon (the oldest living creature) reveals Mabon's location.

His mother Modron represents the Divine Mother whose name literally means "mother," connecting Mabon to ancient matriarchal deity worship. His story shares similarities with other divine children who are stolen or hidden: Persephone, Dionysus, and others whose loss and recovery mark seasonal cycles.

The therapeutic spring at his sanctuary at Chamalières suggests Mabon/Maponus had healing powers, perhaps related to the concept of the "wounded healer": one who has suffered and been restored can heal others.
