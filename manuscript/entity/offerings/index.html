Offerings

The practice of making offerings forms the third pillar of daily magickal work, alongside meditation and banishing. Where banishing rituals protect through force, offerings protect through peace, extending an olive branch to spirits and elemental forces that share the world.

Why Make Offerings

Human activity can negatively impact the spiritual dimension. Building on sacred ground, polluting natural spaces, or disturbing power spots may provoke local guardians and forces to act in retribution. In traditional cultures, shamans serve as mediators, mending these breaches and maintaining harmony between worlds. Through offerings, the practitioner signals that any harm was unintentional and seeks to make amends.

Beyond protection, offerings are powerful tools for gaining spiritual allies and manifesting magickal work. Regular offerings build bonds with spiritual forces, making the universe more willing to support your practice.

Types of Offerings

Offerings can be physical or mental, though most spirits feed on subtle essence rather than physical substance itself. The exception is smoke from burnt substances. Many spirits take nourishment directly from incense, herbs, and woods. Even with solid offerings like cakes and alcohol, you can amplify their power by mentally multiplying them through will, seeing them fill infinite space.

Informal Offerings

  • A dime or whiskey left on a grave
  • Flowers or water poured near a tree
  • Incense burned and mentally offered to the ten directions

These small acts of generosity build good relationships with spiritual forces around your home and wherever you travel.

Formal Offerings

For structured practice, see the complete ritual below. This working rewards protectors and familiars who assist you while pacifying spirits that might cause obstacles. It speaks to elemental and spirit forces generally, making it safe for regular practice.

Cultural Considerations

When making offerings to specific classes of spirits, research is essential. Certain offerings can offend particular beings:

  • Salt upsets the dead in some Afro-Caribbean traditions
  • Meat enrages Nagas in Himalayan practices

Let research, omens, and dreams guide your offerings to specific entities.

Daily Practice

The ideal magickal regimen includes meditation, banishing, and offerings. Aim for meditation and banishing daily (preferably twice), with offerings at least once weekly. These practices take little time once learned and provide the foundation for all other magickal work.

Ancestor Offerings

In many respects, ancestor veneration is an adaptation of the way love is expressed among the living, adjusted for the circumstances of the dead. Four of Gary Chapman's five archetypal love languages apply directly: spending quality time, words of affirmation, acts of service, and exchanging gifts. Offerings are the gift-giving dimension of that relationship.

The Spirit of Offering

The difference between placing a morsel of food on a plate and making an offering lies in the quality of attention and feeling brought to the act. Offerings made with genuine joy, the selfless kind associated with giving rather than receiving, carry more power than those made out of obligation or habit.

Why Give Offerings to Ancestors

  • Sustenance: In many traditions, the souls of the dead require food and other offerings to remain strong. Without regular nourishment, spirits weaken and eventually fade. Consistent offerings empower the ancestors to work on behalf of the lineage.
  • Communication: Offerings form a symbolic language. The specific items chosen signal intent: coffee implies it is time to work, engage, or discuss; champagne signals celebration. Choosing the right offering sharpens the message and strengthens the petition. A spell is, in this sense, a prayer in three dimensions: the physical ingredients reinforce the spoken petition and ground it in the material world.
  • Thanksgiving: Offerings are given in gratitude when ancestors have interceded. The reciprocity principle applies: give in proportion to what has been received. Lavish gifts offered before lavish gifts have been given can create energetic imbalance. Aim for balance.

A Caution Regarding Ghosts

Offerings should not be given to ghosts. Doing so empowers them and may increase disruption. If a ghost's presence is felt, the appropriate response is prayer for their peace and healing. Through sustained loving prayer, a ghost may eventually rehabilitate and become a true ancestor; at that point, offerings become appropriate.

Food and Meals

Sharing food is among the simplest, least expensive, and most powerful offerings available. A small portion of whatever is being eaten, not a full serving, is offered to the ancestors before the living eat. In African Diasporic traditions, food and drink at religious events are offered to the ancestors first as a matter of course. This creates a bond between the living and the dead: both are, in a sense, seated at the same table.

Cooking as Ritual Devotion

Cooking is alchemy: heat transforms raw materials drawn from the earth into something entirely new. The time spent cooking a meal that will be shared with the ancestors is itself a form of service and devotion, independent of the quality of the result. Learning ancestral recipes, dishes favored by recently deceased relatives, or traditional foods from the regions ancestors inhabited all deepen the relationship. The learning itself counts.

Learning to cook regional, seasonal foods from the area where the practitioner currently lives grounds the ancestral line by putting them in contact with local land spirits. Some nutritionists observe that a healthy diet blends ancestral foods with locally seasonal ones. A prayer of welcome and thanksgiving the first time a new seasonal fruit or vegetable crosses the threshold is a natural extension of this practice.

At Gatherings

Any social gathering tends to attract ancestral spirits. Serving the ancestors a small portion of each dish before serving living guests reflects the etiquette of formal dinners, where honored guests are served first. The ancestors are, by definition, the oldest guests present and merit acknowledgment, even if the gesture is small and private.

Eating Away From Home

For those who eat outside the home frequently, pushing a small bite to the side of the plate and mentally recognizing it as an offering to the ancestors is a valid practice.

Ex Votos

Ex votos (ex voto suscepto: "from the vow made") are special offerings promised at the time a petition is made and given only if the petition is granted. The practice goes back to the ancient world and continues in Catholic and Greek Orthodox traditions, where ex votos typically take the form of paintings depicting the favor received or small sculptures in metal or wax.

Body-part shapes are especially common and map symbolically to the nature of the petition:

FormPetition
FeetGrounding during difficulty; protection while traveling; guidance when lost
HandsWork, influence, and practical matters
EyesFreedom from deception; insight; divination and psychic ability
BrainIntelligence; passing exams; mental challenges
LungsInspiration
HeartLove; emotional healing; affairs of the heart

Personal symbolic connections are valid. An ex voto is meant to capture the feeling of answered prayer; there is no wrong way to express that through a personal symbol.

Tools

Small tools or toy replicas of tools can be given as offerings, serving as a symbolic empowerment that gives the ancestors additional strength for a specific type of working. Unlike ex votos, which are given in response to a fulfilled petition, tools can be given as general encouragement.

How to Give Offerings

Frequency

How often offerings are given is a matter of personal relationship with the ancestors. Some practitioners give offerings once a year on a day traditionally devoted to ancestor veneration (All Souls' Day, the Hungry Ghost Festival) or on a personally significant date (a birthday, an anniversary of someone's passing). Others find a daily rhythm more natural. There is no wrong answer; follow intuition and observe how the practice feels over time.

Before divination or magickal work, making an offering while praying for ancestral blessing is especially recommended.

Frequency by Offering Type

  • Tools and ex votos — Given sparingly and left permanently. It would be discourteous to take back a gift. Because altars grow over time, avoid accumulating clutter; give these only when genuinely moved to do so.
  • Consumable offerings (food, beverage) — Ideally left on the altar overnight, or at minimum until a candle lit alongside them has burned down. Remove before they become moldy.

Disposal

Most offerings can be disposed of in the trash. If something unusual is noticed (a pest found dead in the offering, an unexplained smell or discoloration), dispose of it in a more intentional way: bring it to a crossroads or drop it into running water.

Discernment

If a particular offering seems unwelcome, perhaps because it was taboo within a significant branch of the lineage, that is useful information. Note it and adjust. It is unlikely to cause lasting harm. The ancestors are invested in the relationship and generally patient with honest mistakes.

Journal Prompts: Offerings

  1. The five love languages are: spending quality time together, words of affirmation, acts of service, physical touch, and exchanging gifts. Which do you prefer to use to show love to others? Which do you prefer others use to show love to you? (This prompt is especially fruitful when explored with family members and romantic partners.)
  2. What is the best gift you have ever received? What made it so meaningful?
  3. How do you feel about cooking? If confidence in the kitchen is lacking, what is one small step that might help build it?
  4. Looking back at your list of blood and lineage ancestors:
    • Do you know what any of these ancestors' favorite foods were?
    • Did any of them live through famine or the Great Depression? Do you see patterns in your living family's relationship with food that might trace back to those experiences?
  5. What do you know about your ancestral foodways? What stories have you heard about your ancestral foods? Are there any foods from your ancestral tradition that you feel a particular affinity for?
  6. What do you know about the local, seasonal foods from the land you currently live on? What stories have you heard about them? Do you feel a particular affinity for any?
  7. What fermented beverages were loved by your ancestors? How do you feel about drinking alcohol? Does it feel more like medicine or poison to you?
