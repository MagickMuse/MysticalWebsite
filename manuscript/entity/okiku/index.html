The Story

The wealthy Aoyama family owned an extremely valuable set of ten Delft (Dutch pottery) plates. Okiku, working as a maid in their home, was blamed when one plate broke or disappeared. She drowned in a well.

Every night, she emerges to count plates: "One, two, three..." all the way up to nine. She can count no further, and so she dissolves into tears and wails.

Multiple Versions

The story has many variations, all tragic:

Sexual Coercion:

She rejected the head of the family's sexual advances. He hid one of the plates, threatening to accuse her of theft unless she had sex with him. The accusation would bring the death penalty. In despair, Okiku threw herself into the well.

Actual Breakage:

She really did break a plate and was thrown into the well as punishment.

Scapegoating:

Aoyama's wife broke the plate but blamed Okiku, who was thrown into the well.

Political Betrayal:

Okiku revealed a planned samurai rebellion. When the rebels discovered who betrayed them, they tortured her before throwing her into the well.

The Well as Torture

In some versions, the well is an instrument of torture as well as death: Okiku is repeatedly raised and lowered by her killer who sadistically enjoys her torment. The well is now Okiku's home.

Cultural Legacy

Theater and Film:

  • Story believed based on historical events
  • First dramatized in 1741
  • Kabuki staple: Bancho Sarayashiki ("The Broken Dishes at Bancho Mansion")
  • Subject of several novels and movies

Artistic Depictions:

  • Hokusai created portraits of her
  • Yoshitoshi Tsukioka depicted her in his work

Horror Genre Influence:

Okiku's ghost is the inspiration for the Ring series of novels and movies, and essentially for the entire horror genre of ghost-women climbing out of wells to torment the living.

How to Disarm Her

Shouting "ten!" after Okiku counts "nine" but before she can start wailing will disarm her. Reports say she will vanish on the spot.

The Okiku Bug

In 1795, Japan suffered an infestation of worms found in old wells. The worm was naturally covered with thin threads so that it appeared bound. It was nicknamed Okiku Mushi or "Okiku bug" and was widely considered an incarnation of Okiku or her curse.

Sacred Sites

Different locations claim to be the site of her well:

Himeji Castle:

Now a tourist destination where apparitions of Okiku are still allegedly witnessed

Located on land purchased from the Aoyama family
