La Macarena

Culture
SpanishCatholicRomani
Gender
Female
Type
Saint
Also called
Our Lady of Hope, La Esperanza, The Madonna of Hope
Other Entities1
Scatach

La Macarena is a mysterious, beautiful, powerful Madonna. Her true title is Our Lady of Hope or La Esperanza, but she takes her nickname from her home, the Macarena district of Seville, Spain. This Lady of Hope manifests somewhat differently than others.

The Miraculous Statue

Macarena is now intensely identified with a miraculous statue, credited to sculptor Luisa Roldán (1652 to 1706), who is famed for the lifelike quality of her work. Some devotees insist that angels made the statue or at least assisted La Roldána. The image of Macarena is ubiquitous in Seville, appearing in bars, restaurants, and taxis.

Her Special Devotees

La Macarena's devotees come from all walks of life. She is venerated by various religious societies (confraternities), but she also has two special constituencies. She is beloved by Gitanos, Seville's Roma, and she is adored by bullfighters, not just locally but throughout Spain and Mexico.

Patroness of Bullfighters

Bullfighters traditionally invoke La Macarena's blessings before entering the ring. Her veil is sometimes used to miraculously heal injured matadors. Her most famous devotee, Seville's own Joselito, "Little José," José Gomez Ortega (8 May 1895 to 16 May 1920), was born in the Macarena neighborhood. At age seventeen, he became the youngest to earn the title matador. Joselito spent a fortune buying emeralds for Macarena. When he died, fatally gored in the ring, she wore black for a month, the only time she has ever dressed in black.

Ancient Devotion

Passionate veneration of La Macarena predates the image Roldán was commissioned to create. The image is a reflection of devotion, not the other way around. Confraternities devoted to her were approved in 1595. Because of the intense passion she evokes and because there has always been an unconventional element to her veneration, there has long been speculation regarding Macarena's origins. Some theorize that her roots lie in the mysterious Saint Sara. Before Jews were expelled from Spain in 1492, Seville was the center of Kabbalistic veneration of a now mysterious spirit called the Fallen Widow, possibly Mary Magdalen and possibly an early manifestation of Macarena.

Her Rival, La Triana

La Macarena's rival is La Triana, another beautiful, miracle-working Esperanza statue, this one housed in Seville's Triana neighborhood. La Triana, like Macarena, has a large Roma fan base, but rather than matadors, she is beloved by sailors and flamenco dancers. La Triana is unofficially considered the matron of the gay community. Her statue is housed in the Chapel of the Mariners.

The Miraculous Bruise

The statue of Macarena is damaged. She has a bruise on her right cheek, not as part of the original statue but inflicted by a drunk in the crowd during a Holy Week processional. He threw a glass bottle at her image, striking her, and a bruise emerged. Artists were hired to repair the statue, but each time restoration was complete, the bruise mysteriously reappeared.

When the drunk sobered up and saw the damage, he repented. For the rest of his life, every Holy Week he walked before her statue, wearing chains on his feet and carrying a large cross. After his death, his descendants took up his cross, continuing his penance.

Manifestation

La Macarena is a gorgeous, life-like, life-size statue of a beautiful, sorrowful woman, always exquisitely dressed. Her clothes are on display in a museum devoted to her. She looks like a Fairy-tale queen. Her face glistens with crystal tears, and she wears a massive gold crown of stars.

Sacred Site

The Basilica de la Macarena in Seville, plus countless home altars throughout Spain, Mexico, and wherever her devotees have traveled.

Holy Week Procession

La Macarena is a star of Seville's Holy Week, the week prior to Easter. Thousands throng the streets to greet her passionately when she emerges from her basilica for her annual Holy Week parade through the district. This procession is one of the most emotionally charged religious events in Spain.

Magickal Properties

La Macarena represents the convergence of multiple spiritual traditions: Catholic Marian devotion, Gitano spirituality, possible Kabbalistic roots in the Fallen Widow, and perhaps even traces of Mary Magdalen. Her miraculous nature, including the reappearing bruise, the healing powers of her veil, and the intense devotion that predates her statue, suggests she is more than simply a representation of the Virgin Mary.

Ritual Uses

Invoke La Macarena for:

  • Hope in desperate situations
  • Healing of physical injuries, especially those related to combat or dangerous professions
  • Protection for bullfighters and those in dangerous professions
  • Support for Roma people and marginalized communities
  • Miracles of healing and intervention
  • Passionate devotion and emotional connection to the divine feminine

Lore

La Macarena's possible connections include Saint Sara (Romani patron saint), the Fallen Widow (possibly Mary Magdalen), Black Madonna traditions, and the Black Madonna of Czestochowa (similar sorrowful imagery and miracle-working reputation). Her rivalry with La Triana represents different aspects of the divine feminine: Macarena for bullfighters and traditional masculine courage, Triana for sailors, dancers, and the gay community. Together they show how the same divine energy manifests differently for different communities. The fact that the bruise on her cheek cannot be permanently removed suggests the statue has taken on a life of its own, accepting the wound as part of her identity and using it to demonstrate her miraculous nature.
