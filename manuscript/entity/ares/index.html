Source Notes

Origin and Period

Ares is the Greek god of war. The name has been interpreted as throng of war. His known period of worship runs from circa 800 BC, but probably from earlier times, until Christianization circa 400 AD. No synonyms are recorded.

Centers of Cult

No sanctuaries are known until Roman times, when a temple was dedicated in the Agora in Athens.

Art References

He appears on the Parthenon frieze, in a celebrated statue by Alkamenes, and in other contemporary sculpture.

Literary Sources

Chiefly the Iliad of Homer and the Theogony of Hesiod.

Character and Lineage

Ares is a lesser known member of the Olympic pantheon of great gods, the son of Zeus and Hera, who allegedly lived in Thrace. As a warrior god he is contrasted with the more prominent and successful goddess Athena, who fought and vanquished him in a war between the gods. While Athena stands for victory in battle through glory and honor, Ares epitomizes the evil and more brutal aspects of warfare. In the eyes of Zeus he is the most hateful of gods. His war chariot is pulled by Phobos (fear) and Deimos (terror).

Sons of Ares

Ares' sons were even more barbaric than he. Kyknos was a ferocious killer who, until slain by Herakles, was proposing a temple constructed of human skulls. Another notorious son of Ares was the dragon slain by Kadmos as he sought to found the city of Thebes. Its teeth, which Kadmos sowed in the earth, germinated and sprang up as warriors, the grandsons of Ares, who promptly turned on each other in mortal combat.

Liaison with Aphrodite

Ares entered into a brief liaison with Aphrodite, the goddess consort of Hephaistos, and through her fathered a daughter, Harmonia, whom Kadmos later married, thus paving the way to establish Thebes in an atmosphere of peace and harmony.
