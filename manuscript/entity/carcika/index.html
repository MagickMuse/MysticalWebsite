Carcika

Charchika, Carcika (repetitive chant)

Overview

Carcika is a goddess of the Buddhist Mahayana tradition, whose name means the repetitive chant, from the Sanskrit carcika, which names the act of repeated recitation by which a mantra or a holy text is impressed upon the practitioner's mind. She belongs to the family of red wrathful goddesses whose iconography carries the cup and knife of the tantric feast, a family that stretches from the Hindu Camunda through the Buddhist wrathful mandalas and into the Vajrayana yidam cycles of the Himalayan region.

Classification

Goddess of the Buddhist Mahayana tradition. Red wrathful goddess of the tantric mandalas, sharing the cup-and-knife iconography of the wider family.

Domains

The cutting power of repeated recitation, the impressing of the mantra upon the mind through repetition, the wrathful transformation of ordinary speech into liberating sound, the fierce protection of the practice by the transmitted sadhana.

Iconography

Depicted of red color, in the wrathful stance of the tantric goddesses, holding the skull cup in her left hand and the flaying knife in her right. The red color places her within the padma, the lotus family of the mandala, whose wisdom is the wisdom of discernment and whose transformative fire burns the poison of attachment. The cup and knife belong to the standard tantric feast iconography, in which the cup receives the transformed offerings of the practice and the knife cuts through the roots of ignorance.

Attributes

Skull cup and flaying knife.

Colors

Red.

Sacred Sites

Within the mandalas of the Mahayana and Vajrayana traditions, particularly in Tibetan and Newar Buddhist practice. Her worship belongs to the transmitted sadhana rather than to independent temple cults.

Ritual

Her practice is undertaken within a transmitted lineage of the Mahayana or Vajrayana tantric tradition. The repetitive chant that her name names is the central feature of her sadhana, a mantric recitation carried out under lineage instruction until the sound of the mantra has become the practitioner's own inner voice.

Lore

Carcika's naming as the repetitive chant places her at an important point in the Buddhist understanding of practice, at which the ordinary act of speech is transformed by repetition into a liberating instrument. In this understanding the mantra recited a hundred thousand times is not the same as the mantra recited once, because the many recitations have impressed the mantra upon the mind so deeply that the mind itself has taken on the shape of the mantra. Carcika embodies this transformation as a goddess, and her wrathful red form marks the cutting edge of the transformation as it burns away the ordinary speech that would otherwise remain untransformed.
