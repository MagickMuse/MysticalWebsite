Saint Euphemia is renowned for healing rheumatism.

Classification

Venerated by most branches of Christianity.

Life and Martyrdom

Euphemia (died c. 307) was the daughter of a senator from Chalcedon, now part of modern Istanbul. Her family was Christian. Euphemia was discovered hiding in a house with other Christians instead of participating in a mandated public Pagan ritual. The group was arrested and tortured for days. Euphemia, the youngest, was separated from the group and threatened with the most brutal torture.

Tortures Survived

Euphemia called her torturers' bluff, enduring terrible torture with bravery and refusing to repudiate Christianity:

  • Tied to a wheel studded with sharp knives, which cut her. An angel appeared, removed Euphemia from the wheel, and healed her wounds.
  • Men carrying her to an incinerator saw two fierce angels in the flames and refused; they were killed for refusal. Two other soldiers tossed her in, but she remained unharmed.
  • Thrown into a pit filled with knives and shards of glass, she emerged unscathed.

Death

Finally, she was taken to the arena to be devoured by wild beasts. She remained unmolested, until eventually a female bear swiped at her, leaving a small wound on her leg. Copious amounts of blood flowed, disproportionate to this kind of wound, and Euphemia instantly dropped dead. Simultaneously, an earthquake struck, causing onlookers to flee. Her parents rescued her corpse and provided a golden sarcophagus.

The Chalcedonian Miracle

Euphemia helped decide the future direction of Christianity. The Council of Chalcedon (451) convened in Saint Euphemia's Cathedral to debate the true nature of Christ. No consensus could be reached between those proclaiming Christ's "full humanity and full divinity" and Monophysites insisting Christ was solely divine.

Both parties wrote their stance on separate scrolls, placed in Euphemia's tomb. The emperor sealed the tomb and ordered guards to watch for three days. When opened, the Monophysites' scroll lay beneath the saint's feet, while the Chalcedonian Creed was held in her right hand.

Relics Controversy

In 617, her body was transferred to Constantinople to protect her from Persian invaders. Iconoclasts tossed her silver reliquary into the sea; it was rescued and hidden. By 796, her relics were again on display. Some insist they remain in Istanbul's Orthodox Church of Saint George at the Phanar. Others suggest Crusaders stole her remains, or that the Basilica of Saint Euphemia in Croatia miraculously received some relics.

Iconography

A great favorite of Orthodox icons. Roman Catholic images often portray her surrounded by lions.

Attributes

Cross, wheel.

Feast Days

  • Patriarchal Church of Saint George at the Phanar in Istanbul
