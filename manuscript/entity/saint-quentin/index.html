Saint Quentin was a Christian martyr who died around 287 CE. The son of a Roman senator, his name suggests he may have been a fifth son. He secretly converted to Christianity and traveled to Gaul to spread the faith, settling in Amiens where he gained a reputation as a wonder-worker.

Domains

Quentin is invoked against influenza, colds, and respiratory ailments involving coughing or sneezing. As a famous escape artist, he assists those seeking release from prison or captivity.

Iconography

Quentin appears in the process of being tortured, often shown with the instruments of his martyrdom. His decapitated body may be depicted with a white dove emerging from his severed neck.

Sacred Sites

The Basilica of Saint Quentin in the town of Saint Quentin (named for him) in Picardy, France houses his relics. Saint Eligius expanded and rebuilt the shrine, encasing some of Quentin's relics, including teeth, hair, and the nails used in his torture, in gold.

Lore

Quentin's miracle-working drew the attention of Roman authorities. After his arrest and torture, he was being transported to Reims for judgment when he miraculously escaped his chains. Rather than hiding, he resumed preaching publicly and was recaptured. This time, authorities executed him on the spot rather than risk another escape. His body and severed head were thrown into the marshes of the Somme River Valley.

Fifty-five years later, a blind Roman noblewoman named Eusebia received a dream vision directing her to Gaul. Following the vision's instructions, she found Quentin's remains emerging intact from the waters, emitting the sweet odor of sanctity. She placed them in a chariot, which halted at a mountain and refused to move further. Quentin was buried at that spot, and Eusebia built a chapel over his grave. Her sight was then miraculously restored.

Despite the shared name, California's San Quentin State Prison is named after a Miwok warrior, not this saint.
