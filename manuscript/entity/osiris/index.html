Ancient Culture Hero

Osiris, ancient deity, is a culture hero. Initially a God of Nature, he became identified with other deities who die and are reborn. His power is reflected in the sheer number of his names: more than a hundred are listed in the Egyptian Book of the Dead. He invented agriculture: the sacred rites of grain. He taught people to bake bread and brew beer. Osiris invented wine, built the first temples, and taught the art of sculpting so that the first statues were formed. He taught musical and theatrical arts. After teaching these arts in Egypt, Osiris traveled around the world, transmitting his knowledge. He left his sister/wife Isis home as regent of Egypt. Gentle and just, Osiris was also a lover of music; he invented two different kinds of flute. His civilizing influence had a profound effect throughout Asia. Among his most significant contributions was dissuading his people from the practice of cannibalism.

In addition to being worshipped in Egypt, Osiris was deeply venerated in Libya. Some scholars believe that he may have originated there as a Berber deity. His name may mean "the one enthroned" because Isis is the throne. His name is also interpreted as "throne of the eye" possibly referring to a legend that Osiris' soul shelters in the Eye of Horus.

The Sacred Myth

Osiris is most famous as a central, if passive, figure in a long, complex Egyptian saga. His brother and rival Set killed Osiris and cut his body into fourteen parts, scattering them across Egypt. Isis, Mistress of Magick, together with a posse of spirit allies, painstakingly gathered and reconstructed every piece. The only missing part was his phallus, which had been devoured by a crab; therefore, the crab was forever cursed in Egypt. With the reconstructed body, Isis attempted to resurrect him. Anubis invented embalming, and Osiris became the first mummy.

Two Primary Functions

Osiris has two primary functions:

  • He is the lord of grain, the original John Barleycorn, cut down in his prime every year. The death of Osiris was the subject of annual festivals possibly the prototype for modern Christian passion plays.
  • Osiris presides over the Egyptian realm of death. Although usually envisioned as a passive figure, Osiris does command an army of ghosts.

Greek Identification

Osiris plays such a complex role that the Greeks identified him with three deities:

  • Apollo, Lord of Music, Order, and Civilization
  • Dionysus, inventor of beer and wine
  • Hades, Lord of Death

Manifestation

Osiris is not just the spirit of grain; he is grain. He also appeared in numerous animal forms: as Onuphis, the bull; as the Ram of Mendes, which inspired the curious tale of the Goat of Mendes; and as the Benu bird, which resembles the phoenix as a symbol of death and resurrection.

Iconography

Osiris is portrayed as a crowned mummy. He is sometimes depicted with wheat sprouting from his body.

Attributes

The crook and flail of kingship. Osiris, Lord of Death, is the only Egyptian deity who does not carry the ankh, symbol of life.

Emblem

The Djed pillar is usually understood as Osiris' backbone and represents stability but may also represent his lost phallus or the tree trunk that housed him.

Sacred Sites

Shrines across Egypt commemorated where parts of Osiris' body were located and buried. Abydos, the sacred city where his head was found, was the center of his veneration. His mysteries were reenacted in Abydos for over two thousand years.

The Water Lily of Osiris

The water lily was believed in ancient Egypt to carry anti-aphrodisiacal qualities owing to its association with Osiris, whose body is often shown adorned with them. After his brother killed and dismembered him, Isis collected all his parts, minus one, and revived him just enough to ensure he could pass on to Ra. Those seeking an aphrodisiac instead turned to the blue water lily.

Role in Egyptian Magic

Egyptian magickal practice rests on a mythological cycle in which Osiris plays the central role. Set struck him down, Isis restored him through sorcery, and he became ruler of the dead. This sequence gave Egyptians their foundational beliefs about mortality, transformation, and eternal existence.

Funerary texts address the deceased by combining their name with the god's: "the Osiris [name]." This formula binds the dead person's identity to the deity, securing protection and passage to the afterlife. Vocalized properly, the naming grants real power.

His primary emblem, the djed pillar, appears alongside the tyet (Knot of Isis) in royal tombs, including Tutankhamun's burial shrine. The paired symbols unite masculine and feminine principles, embodying the regenerative forces that conquer death.

Practical considerations for working with Osiris:

  • He embodies passive receptivity within transformation
  • His mythology centers on agricultural cycles: grain must fall to rise again
  • Funerary contexts demand appropriate solemnity
  • Spoken invocations carry weight in Egyptian practice
