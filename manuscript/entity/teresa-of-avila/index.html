Teresa of Ávila is renowned for healing headaches, migraines, and protection against heart disease and cardiac arrest.

Classification

Roman Catholic saint.

Life and Legend

Spanish-born Teresa (28 March 1515 to 4 October 1582) grew up to become a mystic visionary, prolific author, first female Doctor of the Church, and among the most influential Roman Catholic scholars. Unlike many saints who claim early spiritual awakenings, Teresa enjoyed pretty clothes and romance novels. She ate and danced with pleasure. Her mother died when she was twelve, and Teresa entered a Carmelite convent at twenty.

During her first year in the convent, she began suffering ill-health, including heart pains and fainting spells. Teresa thought God was punishing her for her sinful ways. (Some modern scholars suggest malaria.) Her father sent her to recuperate at a sanitarium, but her condition worsened, culminating in a three-day coma. A grave was dug for her, but she awoke.

Teresa was paralyzed and in terrible pain for eight months. Eventually the paralysis subsided, but symptoms did not fully depart until she was forty. Teresa attributed her recovery to Saint Joseph. She returned to the convent but began experiencing searing mystic visions that caused profound spiritual, emotional, physical, and mental anguish, or alternatively plunged her into entranced ecstasy. Her confessors suspected the devil's influence.

The Seraph's Sword

A seraph, a fire angel, appeared to Teresa and repeatedly plunged a flame-tipped golden sword into her heart. Although she knew it was a visionary experience, the pain was genuine and physical. Teresa saw angels frequently. During her visions, she sometimes levitated; occasionally she asked her sister nuns to hold her to the ground so she wouldn't cause a commotion.

The Inquisition's Shadow

Teresa lived in constant fear of the Inquisition. Beyond her unconventional experiences, she was doubly suspect because she came from a Converso Jewish family. Her grandfather had been condemned by the Inquisition. In 1485, long before Teresa's birth, her father and grandfather were permitted to reconcile with the Church only after walking barefoot through the streets of Toledo wearing yellow sambenitos, humiliating penitential garments.

The Discalced Carmelites

Teresa eventually founded her own order, the Discalced (Barefoot) Carmelites, where nuns could follow a more rigid, austere rule. For twenty years she traveled through Spain establishing convents and friaries. Her last years were difficult. Although always controversial during her lifetime, Teresa was canonized within forty years of her death.

Powers and Domains

  • Teresa is the most famous and influential devotee of El Niño, the Divine Child
  • She is invoked to heal and soothe headaches and migraines
  • Teresa is invoked against heart disease and cardiac arrest; those who have survived heart attacks may consider themselves under her matronage
  • She is invoked against the fires of Purgatory

Iconography

Teresa appears as a Carmelite nun, often in ecstasy, with a seraph or angel nearby.

Attributes

Heart, arrow, book.

Sacred Sites

  • The Shrine of Teresa of Ávila in Ávila, Spain
  • The Carmelite convent in Alba de Tormes, Spain, where her heart is preserved as a relic displaying marks interpreted as indicating piercing by a spear

Cultural Influence

Teresa of Ávila plays a central role in Abraham Verghese's novel Cutting for Stone. She is the inspiration for Bernini's masterpiece sculpture, The Ecstasy of Saint Teresa.

Feast Day

15 October
