Saint Gobnait is the patron saint of apitherapists (bee therapists) and beekeepers.

Saint Gobnait is renowned for healing wounds using honey and propolis, and for protecting communities from plague.

Classification

Roman Catholic saint.

Life and Legend

Gobnait was born in County Clare, Ireland in the fifth or sixth century. She fled to the remote Aran Islands, where she encountered an angel who told her to keep traveling. The angel said this was not her resting place. When she saw nine white deer, she would know where to settle.

The Nine White Deer

Gobnait traveled across Ireland. Near Clondrohid, County Cork, she found three white deer and followed them. They led her to Ballymakeera, where she discovered six more. These led her to Ballyvourney. When she saw nine white deer there, she ceased traveling and put down roots.

The Spiritual Community

Gobnait founded a women's spiritual community. When plague threatened Ballyvourney, she designated it sacred ground and the plague stayed away.

Hidden Goddess?

Gobnait is a well saint. A well named for her in Ballyvourney is still the subject of an annual pilgrimage. Although now considered Christian, the origins of this pilgrimage are pre-Christian.

Gobnait's legend is vague. She may originally have been a Pagan goddess absorbed by Christianity, or veneration of the historical saint may camouflage devotion to a now-forbidden deity. The earliest documented reference to Saint Gobnait dates from the 13th century.

Names

Perhaps "Gobnait" sounded too Pagan. The name translates as "brings joy," similar to the biblical name Abigail, so she is sometimes called Saint Abigail. Gobnait is intensely associated with bees; she used honey and propolis to heal wounds. The Hebrew name Deborah means "bee," so sometimes she is called Saint Deborah instead.

The Sheela-na-gig

A Sheela-na-gig at her Ballyvourney shrine is incorporated into rituals dedicated to Gobnait. Sheela-na-gigs are figurative carvings of an emaciated naked woman displaying her exaggerated vulva. Their history and meaning are mysterious. Although pre-Christian, they are incorporated into churches throughout the British Isles.

Iconography

Gobnait stands atop a beehive surrounded by bees.

Sacred Animals

Bees, deer.

Colors

Yellow, black.

Sacred Site

Gobnait's Well in Ballyvourney. Pilgrims leave offerings by the well. Her grave is in the churchyard. A 13th-century statue is traditionally displayed on her feast day and Whit Sunday. Measure the statue with a ribbon or woolen thread and use it for healing. Several other wells throughout Ireland are also dedicated to her.

11 February
