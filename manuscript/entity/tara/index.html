Overview

Tara is a Buddha, Bodhisattva, and Tibet's preeminent goddess. She is the most beloved member of the Tibetan pantheon, the miraculous savior who rescues from suffering. Before she was a Buddhist deity, Tara was an Indian goddess, a Tantric goddess, possibly originally a tribal goddess. In India, Tara is fierce, not serene like Tibet's Tara. She roams forests and battlefields, often in the company of Shiva.

Call on Tara when you need assistance right now! She is the goddess of immediate assistance. Tara vows to carry devotees safely across the oceans of danger. Tara protects against wind, water, fire, snakes, elephants, vicious spirits, thieves, imprisonment, and the power of kings.

Magickal Properties

As Tibetan Buddhist Tara: Tara eliminates dangers and disturbance caused by ghosts and spirits, but she doesn't just banish them. She brings the ghosts and spirits to enlightenment so that they become protectors. They will not be back to bother you because Tara transforms them into benevolent beings. Tara is a Buddha, a fully realized being who vowed to return to Earth continuously in the form of a female Bodhisattva.

As Indian Hindu Tara: India's Tara is Lady Wisdom. She bestows sacred and mundane knowledge. She instills literary talent and teaches palmistry and astrology among other skills. Tara protects against danger, disasters, violence, and illness. She performs feats of healing and fulfills all desires.

Her name derives from a Sanskrit root word meaning to traverse or to cross over. Tara helps devotees cross over to immortality and enlightenment. Tara is also related to root words for star and pupil of the eye.

The Legend of Two Tears

According to legend, the Bodhisattva Avalokitesvara gazing down from Heaven was overwhelmed by the endless human suffering he witnessed. No matter how many people he saved, there were still more. Overwhelmed, in despair, two tears streamed down his face and transformed into two paths of Tara. The two Taras bolstered his courage, soothed his despair, and encouraged him not to give up but to persist. The peaceful white tear from Avalokitesvara's left eye is White Tara. The fierce green tear from Avalokiteshvara's right eye is Green Tara.

Green and White Tara are Avalokitesvara's companions. The two devout Buddhist wives of Songtsen Gampo (died 649), the Tibetan king who first brought the Dharma to Tibet, are considered avatars of Tara. Green Tara is incarnate in Nepalese Princess Brikuti. White Tara is incarnate in Chinese Princess Wen-Ch'eng.

Spiritual Powers

Tara brings devotees to spiritual enlightenment. In addition to protecting from danger, she heals all illnesses and provides fertility to those who wish to conceive. Sacred images of Tara are known to spontaneously appear on bone, stone, and wood.

Manifestation

Tibetan form: Tara has an unlimited number of forms. She appeared to the thirteenth-century Tibetan Buddhist sage Gotsangpa near the top of the Drolma-La Pass in the form of twenty-one wolves whose paw prints are still visible in the stone.

Indian form: Tara is young, small, and although not pregnant has a big belly (considered a sign of power). She wears her locked, matted long hair in a bun, ornamented with Rudraksha beads and skulls. Eight snakes entwine her body.

Iconography

Tara's image is found in virtually every temple and monastery in Buddhist Central Asia. She is a favorite subject of Tibetan Thangka paintings. Different goals are accomplished by meditating on Tara's various forms.

Mantra

This is Tara's basic root mantra: OM TARE TUTTARE TURE SVOHA! (Pronounced: OM TA-RAY TOO TA-RAY TOO-RAY SO-HA!) It allegedly promotes longevity, provides healing and removes obstacles from your path.

As a Mahavidya

Tara appears in the Hindu list of the ten Mahavidyas, the great wisdom goddesses of the Tantric tradition. In this context she is described as nearly identical with Kali: dark, resting her left foot on a corpse, wearing a tiger skin and a necklace of severed heads, laughing terribly, with her hair in a single matted braid, standing on a funeral pyre, and pregnant. This is dramatically different from the gentle, playful Tibetan Tara who cheats death.

Tara's inclusion in the Mahavidyas derives from her fierce Hindu forms rather than her Tibetan compassionate persona. The Hindu Mahavidya Tara is modeled on forms such as Ugratara, Kurukulla, and Mahacinatara. Hindu Tantric texts including the Tararahasyavrttika, the Rudra-yamala, and the Tara-tantra describe her standing with one foot on a dead body in the funeral ground, wearing garlands of fifty fresh human heads bleeding freely. The Tara-tantra emphasizes her delight in receiving the blood of sacrificial animals and the blood of her devotees, drawn from the forehead, hands, breast, head, or the area between the eyebrows. Throughout the text she is worshiped in left-handed Tantric rites involving wine, meat, and illicit sex. In these texts barely any resemblance to the benign Tara of Tibet remains.

Tara's association with Kali in the devotional works of the eighteenth-century Bengali poet Ramprasad Sen confirms that the Tara known in Hindu Bengal was a fierce goddess. In Bengal, Ramprasad used the name Tara as an epithet of Kali, treating the two as more or less the same being. A contemporary lithograph of the Mahavidyas shows Kali and Tara adjacent to each other and almost identical: both dark, nearly naked, with protruding tongues, bloodied cleavers in the upper left hand, a garland of severed heads, disheveled hair, and standing on an identical prone male body.

Tara's presence in the Mahavidyas also connects to the long history of religious contact between Tibet and Bengal. Many important Buddhist missionaries to Tibet came from Bengal. Tara was probably well known in Bengal by the late medieval period when the Mahavidya texts were written.

Tara Kurukulla

Tara Kurukulla is a particularly fierce form associated with subjugation rituals. Red in color, she wears a diadem of five skulls, bears fangs, wears a tiger skin, and has a necklace of fifty human heads. Her special power lies in the ability to subjugate and destroy evil spirits or personal enemies. Rituals invoking her require the practitioner to dress in red, visualize taking on the goddess's form, and recite her mantra ten thousand times. The target is then bound by a noose from Kurukulla's lotus flower, drawn forward by an iron hook stuck into the heart, and laid helpless at the practitioner's feet.

Mahacinatara (Ugratara)

Mahacinatara is described in both Buddhist and Hindu sources. She stands in the warrior's stance, awe-inspiring, with a garland of heads. She is short with a protruding belly, complexion like the blue lotus, three-eyed, laughs horribly, stands on a corpse, is adorned with snakes, wears a tiger skin, has protruding tongue and bare fangs, carries a sword and the kartri in her right hands and a blue lotus and skull-cup in her left. A seventeenth-century Hindu Tantric compilation, the Tantrasara, includes an almost identical description, retaining even the reference to the Buddhist Aksobhya image in her crown.

Additional Notes

There are various forms of Tara, each associated with a different color. Officially these are considered different aspects or paths of one Tara, but in terms of folk religion they may be considered distinct spirits. In some cases (Blue or Red Tara, for instance) these spirits were originally independent goddesses who are now-identified with Tara. The most popular forms of Tara are Green Tara and White Tara.
