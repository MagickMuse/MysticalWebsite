Overview

Maneki Neko, the Japanese Beckoning Cat, virtually unknown one century ago, may be the most ubiquitous amulet on Earth today. The standard image depicts a Japanese bobtail cat holding up one paw in invitation. In Japan, the gesture of placing an open-palmed hand up to one ear indicates "come here." Maneki Neko is perceived as exerting a magnetic come-hither effect.

Important Understanding

It is important to appreciate that Maneki Neko is not just a doll or statue but radiates the power of a spirit in the same way that a Daruma doll radiates the power of that Bodhisattva. Maneki Neko is a benevolent cat spirit of abundance, wealth, and protection.

Paw Meanings

  • Left paw raised: Invites business
  • Right paw raised: Invokes cash
  • Both paws raised: Particularly hard-working Maneki Nekos
  • With bells: Cleanses the aura while beckoning prosperity
  • Leopard-spotted: Prized by politicians and those seeking votes (the word for leopard in Japanese is a homonym for vote)

Placement

The image should be placed near a door or window, looking out to exert its magnetic effect.

Legendary Origins

Various legends attest to the roots of this image:

  • A cat saves a samurai's life
  • A cat brings prosperity to a monastery
  • A cat brings economic success to its namesake cathouse (bordello)
  • A cat transforms into a courtesan to raise funds for the impoverished people she loves

Historical Context

Maneki Neko first emerged as a lucky amulet after previously popular phallic images were banned during the Meiji era (1868 to 1912). Cute Maneki Neko was the substitute and quickly proved her worth. Maneki Neko may secretly and discreetly evoke the power of female genital imagery, coming out of the closet just as her more explicit male counterpart was banished within.

Color Meanings

  • White: Brings luck and happiness
  • Gold: Beckons wealth and prosperity
  • Black: Serves as a spiritual guardian; small black charms protect against stalkers
  • Red: Fosters good health
  • Pink: Attracts romance and protects children
  • Tri-colored: Considered especially auspicious
  • Small Maneki Neko worn around the waist: Protects against pain and illness, especially arthritis

Collections

No need to limit yourself to one Maneki Neko. Many fans accrue massive collections. Maneki Neko comes in all sizes and is ornamented with assorted good luck charms to enhance power.

Iconography

  • Maneki Neko statues are commonly manufactured with a slot in back so they can serve as piggy banks
  • Allegedly feeding the statue some coins on a regular basis makes Maneki Neko work even harder
  • Maneki Nekos housed in bars frequently clutch bottles of beer and are positioned to gaze at customers
  • Maneki Neko frequently masquerades as other spirits, including Okame and the Shichi Fukujin

Standard Attributes

  • Holds a koban (a gold coin common during the Edo era, 1603 to 1867)
  • Wears a red collar with a bell
  • More ornate versions wield attributes associated with other spirits, especially Daruma dolls, Daikoku's hammer, and Ebisu's red fish, implying Maneki Neko wields their powers

Spirit Synergy

Sometimes Maneki Neko is costumed as Daruma or portrayed holding a Daruma doll in one hand, thus blending and magnifying their powers.
