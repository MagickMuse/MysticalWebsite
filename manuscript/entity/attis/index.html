Magickal Properties

  • Vegetation god bound to seasonal dying and rising mysteries
  • Intimately linked to Cybele’s ecstatic cult and rites of mourning and renewal

Ritual Uses

  • Seasonal vegetation rites for grief, release, and return to vitality
  • Workings for renewal after loss and for sacred restraint before rebirth

Offerings

  • Quiet, seasonal observances aligned to his mysteries
  • Respectful participation in vegetation rites under qualified guidance

Favored People

Devotees seeking renewal through seasonal mysteries and those within Cybele’s lineage observing proper forms.

Petition For

Renewal after loss, endurance through the fallow season, and the return of life’s vigor.

Warnings

Honor Attis with seasonal rites in connection with Cybele. Do not attempt self‑harm or initiation practices without proper guidance; his mysteries are intense and boundary‑sensitive.

Additional Notes

  • Cult: Phrygian origins, adopted in Rome alongside Cybele (Magna Mater)
  • Associations: Ritual mourning followed by return to life in vegetation cycles

The Birth Saga of Attis

Attis was the young lover of the great goddess Kybele, born through miraculous conception. Zeus lusted after Kybele but she disdained him and escaped his rape attempt by transforming into a rock. In passion's throes, Zeus ejaculated onto the rock. Even in stone form, Kybele remained fertile and conceived a hermaphroditic child named Agdistis.

Agdistis was immensely powerful and violent, perhaps expressing Kybele's rage. The gods, led by Dionysus, decided to eliminate this dangerous being. Dionysus transformed a freshwater spring into wine; Agdistis, intending to drink water, became intoxicated and fell into a stupor. Dionysus had meanwhile collected strands of Kybele's fallen hair, braided them into rope, and fashioned a noose which he slipped over Agdistis' genitals. When Dionysus or Pan let out a bloodcurdling scream, Agdistis jumped awake and castrated himself.

The Virgin Birth

A river of blood poured from Agdistis' wound, from which emerged either an almond or pomegranate tree (sometimes described as a red flower, perhaps a poppy). A passing virgin woman picked the nut, flower, or fruit and instantly conceived.

Her father scoffed at virgin birth claims and punished her by locking her in a tower, depriving her of food to induce miscarriage and starve her to death. Every night, however, Kybele slipped into the locked room bearing apples and water as sustenance for the woman she had chosen as her sacred vessel.

Birth and Rescue

The magical child was born on December 25th. His grandfather rejected the baby, who was then brought to the river and placed in a basket among the reeds to die (an alternate version places him abandoned on a mountaintop). Either a mother goat found and rescued him, or a shepherd found him and brought him home to nurture him on the milk of a freshly-delivered goat. The baby was named Attis, derived from the Phrygian word "attagi" meaning goat. Though called the goat-god, Attis was envisioned as the most handsome man on Earth.

Death and Resurrection

This complex saga eventually concludes with Attis' resurrection three days after his death, coinciding with the vernal equinox, a pattern that mirrors other dying-and-rising deities of seasonal renewal.

Attis and the Midwinter Sun

Because he was said to be born on December 25, Attis was once honored as a sun god of the deep midwinter, venerated far and wide across the land that is now Turkey. His feast on that date was later eclipsed by Mithra and the Dies Natalis Invicti Solis, the Birthday of the Unconquered Sun, and in time it was absorbed into Christmas. The recurring pattern, a divine child born to a virgin in the darkest stretch of the year, passed from one figure to the next while the longing it answered, to mark the return of the light, stayed the same.

Witch Connection

Some early modern French witch-hunters suggested that the goat worshipped by witches was none other than Attis, the consort of Kybele, and that devotees of Kybele and Dionysus served as prototypes for the witches of their day.

Encyclopedia Source Notes

Attis is a dying-and-rising fertility god modeled on the Mesopotamian Dumuzi. He is considered to have originated as a shepherd. His worship is known from circa 500 BC, and probably earlier, until circa 400 AD, with cult centers in the Anatolian region and later throughout Greek and Roman areas of culture. Roman writers, especially Virgil, preserve his literary record, and sculptures and reliefs preserve his image.

In alternative traditions, Kybele, the great mother, is either his mother or purely his consort. Another legend suggests he was conceived immaculately by the demigoddess Nana when she placed a ripe almond in her bosom. According to one legend he met his death gored by a wild boar. In a more popular alternative, he castrated himself under a pine tree to offer his vitality to Kybele.

The latter legend became enshrined in spring rites during which Greek, and later Roman, priests, the Galli, wearing effeminate costumes, castrated themselves or gashed themselves with knives and offered blood sacrifices to the goddess by burying them in the earth. The main center of cult was at Pessinus in Phrygia. The cult was brought to Rome in 204 BC when the stone symbolizing the presence of Cybele was carried from Pessinus and installed in the Temple of Victory on the Palatine Hill. The day sacred to Attis was March 22, when a pine tree was carried into the Temple of Cybele and decorated with flowers and models of Attis. In Christian times the Easter festival took over the date of the Attis rites.
