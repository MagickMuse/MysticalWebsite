Classification

Venerated as a saint by most branches of Christianity.

Life and History

Saint Hilda (614-680) played a significant role in Britain's conversion to Christianity. She was approximately thirteen years old when she became a Christian.

Hilda came from a noble family closely related to royalty. Her father, an Anglo-Saxon, was the nephew of Edwin, king of Northumbria. Her mother was Celtic. When Hilda was an infant, her father died by poisoning, victim of political machinations. She may have been raised at King Edwin's court. Edwin and his entire court, including young Hilda, were baptized in the river at York on Easter Sunday of 627.

Whitby Abbey

Described as an energetic woman and great administrator, Hilda became the abbess of several monasteries. In approximately 656, King Oswy of Northumbria founded Whitby Abbey. Hilda was its first abbess and it was her home for the rest of her life.

Whitby Abbey originally followed Celtic Christianity, but in 664 it hosted the Synod of Whitby, which marks Britain's adoption of Roman rites.

Death

Hilda died peacefully at Whitby. Church bells rang spontaneously at the moment of her death and a local nun experienced a vision of Hilda being carried to Heaven, borne aloft by angels.

The Snakes of Whitby

Hilda was an extremely prominent woman. Kings sought her advice. Considered a great miracle worker, she is credited with ridding Whitby of its snakes, using her magical whip to lop off their heads.

The fossil ammonites (also called snakestones) found on local shores, and on Whitby's coat of arms, are said to be the petrified bodies of these headless serpents.

Powers and Domains

Officially, Hilda is the matron saint of learning, culture, and poetry. Less traditionally, she is Whitby's spiritual guardian. Hilda may be invoked for protection against vampires of all kinds, including "psychic vampires" who prey on the energies and emotions of others.

Connection to Dracula

Until the late 19th century, Whitby's fame derived from the synod and from the ammonites and jet found there. Since Bram Stoker's 1897 novel Dracula, however, Whitby is renowned as the destination of Count Dracula's ship.

Iconography

Hilda is traditionally depicted with a pastoral staff in one hand and a miniaturized version of Whitby Abbey in the other.

Sacred Sites

  • Ruins of Whitby Abbey (seabirds allegedly tip their wings flying over it; her ghost has been witnessed walking nearby)
  • Saint Hilda's Well (Hilderwell), approximately eight miles from Whitby, her retreat near a fresh-water spring
  • Saint Hilda's Church in Griffithstown, Wales
  • Persistent rumors suggest some relics may have been transferred to Glastonbury

Feast Days

  • 17, 18, or 19 November (depending on tradition)
  • 23 June
