Shiva's First Wife

Sati, Shiva's first wife, is the first incarnation of Parvati. The youngest of Daksha's sixteen daughters, Sati fell in love with Shiva, although he was not her father's idea of the perfect husband (or son-in-law). Hidden within her myth is a description of tensions between the new Vedic religion and the older indigenous traditions of Shiva.

Sati and Shiva married and were very happy. They have an intensely erotic relationship. She lured him away from ascetism and encouraged him to use his powers creatively. Sati was infuriated when her father insulted Shiva (and by extension, her) by snubbing him and not inviting him to a major sacrifice. She attended alone, arguing that a daughter doesn't require an invitation to her father's house. At the ceremony, Daksha's insults toward the missing Shiva continued. Sati killed herself in protest via an act of spontaneous combustion.

Sati, the essence of the ideal wife and mother, is petitioned by women seeking longevity, good health, and good fortune for their husbands (and by extension, themselves).

The Siva-purana account

The fully elaborated Sati mythology is found in the plays of Kalidasa and, in greatest detail, in the Puranas, particularly the Siva-purana. There Sati is the daughter of Daksha, and her quest to win Shiva as her husband is presented with two distinct motivations in different versions of the myth. In some accounts the drive comes from Brahma, who wants to humiliate Shiva: Shiva had laughed at Brahma when Brahma lusted after his own daughter, and Brahma resolved to bring Shiva into the suffering of sexual passion. In other accounts the motivation is cosmological rather than personal. The Siva-purana's Rudra-samhita states that if Shiva remains outside the created world, creation will lack auspiciousness (Shiva's name itself meaning auspicious) and may not be able to continue. These cosmological reasons are the more illuminating ones for understanding what the myth is actually about.

Sati is described as beautiful, but in most versions of the myth it is her devotion and asceticism rather than her appearance that attract Shiva's attention. She persists through tests and obstacles until Shiva, touched also by desire, grants her a boon for her austerities. She asks to marry him. He agrees, and despite his impatience she insists on a proper wedding with rituals and guests. Brahma serves as the officiating priest. The two are married and retire to Shiva's mountain retreat, where they spend many years together.

Tension eventually arises between Shiva and Sati's father Daksha. Daksha disapproves of Shiva's unconventional appearance and his disregard for worldly norms: as an ascetic and world-renouncer, Shiva behaves in ways that the established order finds transgressive. Daksha plans a great sacrifice, invites all the important divine beings, and pointedly excludes Shiva and Sati. Shiva is unbothered. Sati, infuriated at the insult to her husband, goes to her father's home uninvited. Daksha publicly snubs her. Outraged, she kills herself.

Virabhadra and the sacrifice

On hearing of Sati's death, Shiva creates the fearsome being Virabhadra and in some versions other fierce forms as well. These beings descend on Daksha's sacrificial arena, defeat the assembled divine hosts, and destroy the sacrifice. Daksha himself is killed in the battle. Most versions of the myth then describe the sacrifice being reinstated and Daksha being resuscitated. Shiva is included among those who receive a share of the sacrifice, and it proceeds smoothly. The antagonism has resolved into accommodation.

Shiva's Dance of Destruction

Shiva, fearing the worst, came anyway, leading a small army of ghosts and spirits but arrived too late to save her. Mad with grief, Shiva flew into the air with her corpse, caressing her, making love to her. The deities tried to stop him but were powerless. Shiva's dance of destruction had been activated; the other deities feared for the survival of Earth. Finally Vishnu threw his discus at Sati's corpse repeatedly, slicing her to fifty-one pieces, which fell to Earth, many at locations Shiva and Sati had visited together or where they had made love.

Meaning of the myth

The Sati mythology is built on the central tension in Shiva's nature between asceticism and erotic engagement, between world-renunciation and worldly participation. His stored-up potency, accumulated through austerity and withdrawal, is understood in the logic of this mythology to be meant for release into the world, where it can invigorate and sustain creation. Sati's role is to accomplish that release. By drawing Shiva into desire, marriage, and eventually family, she brings his energy into the service of the created world rather than allowing it to remain locked in isolation.

The Daksha-Shiva conflict carries a historical dimension alongside its mythological meaning. In the Vedic tradition Shiva is an ambiguous, partly inauspicious deity whose offerings are made outside inhabited areas rather than at the central sacrificial fire. He was almost certainly a non-Aryan deity absorbed into the Hindu tradition over time, and the Brahman custodians of the sacrificial cult regarded him with suspicion. His association with world-renunciation, asceticism, and phallic symbolism placed him at the fringes of established Brahman religion from their point of view. The antagonism between Shiva and Daksha in the myth likely reflects this underlying historical tension. The destruction of the sacrifice followed by its reinstitution with Shiva included among the participants probably represents the eventual incorporation of Shiva into mainstream Brahman religion. He was too powerful a deity to remain excluded.

Sati is the agent who makes that incorporation possible. She comes from the world of dharma, established religion, and orderly society, and she marries into the world of asceticism and wilderness. In combining both worlds in herself she is uniquely positioned to bring them into relation. When she kills herself over the insult to her husband, she forces the confrontation between Daksha's world and Shiva's. What begins as destruction ends as accommodation: Shiva is drawn within the sacrificial circle, made accessible within the very cult that had excluded him.

The dispersal of Sati's body repeats and deepens this theme. Wherever a piece of her falls, the earth is sacralized and a goddess shrine comes into being. The Indian subcontinent is thus understood as the body of Sati herself, and she becomes accessible to her devotees across the entire sacred landscape. When Shiva follows her to earth and unites with her yoni in the form of the linga, he is literally brought down from his mountain retreat into the earth. The conjunction of linga and yoni at Kamarupa and at the other pithas enacts precisely the earth/sky divine-pair pattern that appears elsewhere in Hindu cosmology: a female deity identified with the earth and a male deity associated with the heights and sky, whose union generates and sustains life. In the Sati mythology Shiva moves from indifference to the created world to full engagement with it, and it is Sati who accomplishes every stage of that transformation.

Fire and Suttee

Sati is associated with fire, the avenue of her death. The practice of ritual suicide by a widow either on her husband's funeral pyre or later on a pyre lit from embers taken from his pyre is named sati, anglicized as suttee, in honor of the goddess, although this is not why she chose to immolate herself. Shiva was very much alive. Rather than burning herself to join him, Sati's actions led to separation: she left him to his complete devastation. Sati was avenging an insult. The practice of suttee has always been controversial: some Tantric scholars condemned it as sinful. Whether "suicide" is voluntary or physically or psychologically forced is questionable. Suttee was outlawed in 1829 but still sometimes occurs.

The Mahavidyas

One of the most significant episodes in Sati's mythology is the origin of the ten Mahavidyas. When Shiva refuses to permit Sati to attend her father Daksha's sacrifice, she loses her temper. First she assumes a dreadful form, and then she multiplies herself into ten terrifying forms that surround Shiva on all sides: Kali, Tara, Chinnamasta, Bhuvaneshvari, Bagala, Dhumavati, Kamala, Matangi, Sodasi, and Bhairavi. Shiva, completely surrounded and terrified, relents and gives her permission to go.

The Mahabhagavata-purana account adds that once she has his permission, Sati takes the form of Kali, attends the sacrifice, and kills herself. Daksha weeps at the sight of his daughter as Kali: dark, naked, and disheveled.

This episode dramatically alters the traditional Sati myth, in which she remains loyal and subordinate throughout her life. These later versions show Sati exerting her will over Shiva, scaring him into submission when he resists her. The Mahavidyas make explicit what is latent throughout her story: Sati's submission to Shiva is chosen, not required. When she chooses otherwise, Shiva has no answer for her.

In the Brhaddharma-purana, Sati in her dreadful form commands Shiva to give mantras and instructions for worshiping the Mahavidyas. Both that text and the Mahabhagavata-purana state she appeared in these forms so that devotees may achieve not only ultimate release (moksha) but also desires and power over others: marana (destruction of enemies), uccatana (causing a person to cease their actions), mohana (causing infatuation or delusion), and stambhana (causing paralysis), among others.

Shakti

Sati is the epitome of Shakti, divine feminine power. She is associated with fire-walking rituals.

Sacred Sites

Temples were erected where Sati's body parts fell. Called Shakti Pithas, these shrines, scattered throughout Pakistan, India, Bangladesh and Sri Lanka, are important Hindu pilgrimage sites. Sati is also associated with Satisar, the lake of the goddess Sati in Kashmir.

The number of these seats differs widely from one account to another, ranging from a mere handful to over a hundred. The most celebrated of them lies in the region of Kamarupa in Assam, where her yoni is said to have fallen and where the goddess is worshipped as Kamakhya. Each seat is held to be a part of her body or one of her ornaments, so that the many local goddesses honored at them are understood as living forms of a single great goddess whose body is the land itself.
