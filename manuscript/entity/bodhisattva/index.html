Bodhisattva, whose Sanskrit name means one whose essence is perfect knowledge, is a generic title of the Mahayana Buddhist tradition, applied to beings who stand within the long path toward buddhahood. The title covers the earlier stages of a future buddha, the successive phases through which a practitioner ascends before the final release of full enlightenment. As a class rather than a single figure, the bodhisattvas populate the great celestial pantheon of northern Indian, Tibetan, Chinese, and Japanese Buddhism, each with a particular function, iconography, and following.

Domains

The long path toward buddhahood, the vow to defer final release for the sake of all beings, the compassionate labor of the enlightened for the unenlightened, and the whole class of celestial figures who embody the different modes of that labor.

The Great Bodhisattvas

Among the countless bodhisattvas of the tradition, the most significant include:

  • Avalokitesvara, whose name means merciful lord, the bodhisattva of compassion whose office is to hear the cries of the world; in East Asia he is known as Kwan Yin, in Japan as Kannon, and in Tibet as Chenrezig, and he is the patron bodhisattva of Tibet itself.
  • Maitreya, the buddha of the future, currently residing in the Tushita heaven and awaiting the appointed time when he will descend to teach the dharma after the memory of the present buddha has faded.
  • Manjusri, the bodhisattva of transcendent wisdom, wielder of the flaming sword that cuts through ignorance and holder of the scripture of the perfection of wisdom; in Japan he is known as Monju, in China as Wen Shu.
  • Samantabhadra, the bodhisattva of universal practice, keeper of the ten great vows, whose mount is the six-tusked white elephant; in Japan he is known as Fugen.
  • Kshitigarbha, the earth-store bodhisattva, savior of beings in the hell realms; in Japan he is known as Jizo.
  • Mahasthamaprapta, the bodhisattva of great strength; in China venerated as the female figure Dai Shi Zhi.
  • Akasagarbha, the space-store bodhisattva, whose essence is likened to the sky; in Japan he is known as Kokuzo.

Iconography

Bodhisattvas are typically depicted wearing regal dress and trappings, including a crown, marking their character as princely figures rather than as the ascetic form of the buddha proper. The crown, the ornaments, the silks, and the jewels of a bodhisattva signal their remaining engagement with the world of forms, their unbroken participation in the ordinary categories of persons, so that they may reach the beings they have vowed to save.

The Bodhisattva Vow

What distinguishes a bodhisattva from a solitary aspirant to enlightenment is the vow: the great commitment to postpone the final entry into nirvana until all beings have been brought across with them. This vow is the animating principle of Mahayana practice, and it is worked out in the ten great vows preserved in the Avatamsaka Sutra and attributed to Samantabhadra. Every bodhisattva figure in the pantheon is understood as a particular embodiment of this vow, a specific mode through which the great compassion is exercised in the world.
