Ovid's Strigae Demon Defying Spell

The Striga (plural: Strigae or Striges) is a malevolent flying night-spirit from Roman and later European folklore, believed to prey on sleeping infants and young children, consuming their blood, flesh, and life force. The word derives from the Latin strix, meaning screech owl, and the creature is associated with that bird's form.

Nature and Origins

The Striga is believed to have originated among Germanic peoples of northern Europe and was well established in Roman belief by the early Common Era. The poet Ovid writes of them directly, prescribing folk remedies to ward them off from a baby's room.

Strigae are closely related to other child-threatening night spirits including Lamiae, Lamashtu, and Lilith.

Form

The Striga is depicted as a screech owl or as a witch-like flying figure. It enters homes at night through windows or by slipping past thresholds, targeting unprotected infants.

Protection

Ovid records that touching thresholds and lintels with arbutus and placing hawthorn (whitehorn) wands at windows will keep Strigae at bay. A meat offering left at the threshold serves as a decoy should a spirit slip past the wards.

Later Folklore

The Striga concept persisted into medieval and early modern European folklore, eventually merging with witch beliefs in Romanian, Slavic, and Italian traditions. The Romanian strigoi and Italian strega (witch) both derive from the same root.
