Ravana is the ten-headed demon king of Lanka and the primary antagonist of the Ramayana epic. Despite his role as villain, he is revered in some Hindu and Sri Lankan traditions as a great scholar, devotee of Shiva, and master of the arts.

Overview

Ravana is the ruler of Lanka and king of the Rakshasas (demons). In the Ramayana he abducts Sita, the wife of Rama, and holds her captive in a garden prison in Lanka, keeping her surrounded by demonesses. Despite repeated attempts to persuade Sita to accept him as her husband, she refuses. He is ultimately defeated by Rama with the aid of Hanuman and an army of monkey warriors.

Ravana is depicted as having ten heads and twenty arms, symbolizing his immense power and knowledge. He is said to have mastered all four Vedas and the six Upanishads, and is considered a great musician and scholar. He is also a devoted worshiper of Shiva.

Role in the Ramayana

Ravana's abduction of Sita is the central conflict of the Ramayana. He carries her to Lanka in his flying chariot. During her captivity, Sita refuses to yield to Ravana's advances, sustained by her purity, her devotion to Rama, and the inner fire (tapas) generated by her chastity — a force she tells Ravana she could use to burn him to ashes (5.22), held back only by the fact that Rama has not given her permission to do so.

Ravana attempts to deceive Sita by having his court magician create a false severed head resembling Rama's, claiming Rama has been defeated in battle. Sita is not permanently deceived. Ravana is ultimately slain by Rama.

Dual Nature

Although Ravana is the villain of the Ramayana, Hindu tradition acknowledges his complexity. He is a Brahmin by birth (his father was the sage Vishrava), a devoted worshiper of Shiva, a master of the Vedas, and a gifted musician said to have invented the instrument known as the Ravanhattha. In some regional traditions, particularly in Sri Lanka and parts of South India, Ravana is venerated rather than condemned.

Source

Kinsley, David. Hindu Goddesses: Visions of the Divine Feminine in the Hindu Religious Tradition. University of California Press, 1986.
