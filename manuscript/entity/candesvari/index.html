Candesvari

Favored People
Practitioners of the wrathful yidam cycles in Mahayana and Vajrayana Buddhism, those who work with the fierce protectress goddesses of the mandala
Culture

Fierce protection within the Mahayana Buddhist frame, cutting through obstacles to compassionate action, the wrathful aspect of the awakened feminine

Female

Chandesvari, Candesvari (fierce lady)

Also known as

Chandesvari, Candesvari (fierce lady)

Overview

Candesvari is a minor goddess of the Buddhist Mahayana tradition, whose name means fierce lady, from candi, the feminine of canda meaning violent or burning, and isvari meaning sovereign lady. She belongs to the wide company of wrathful goddesses of the Mahayana and Vajrayana mandalas, and her iconography places her at the point where the fierce Buddhist protectress meets the emblems of pastoral offering and animal companion. Her name is one of a family of Buddhist counterparts to the Hindu Sakta forms whose names sound the canda root, so that in the Buddhist mandalas she stands as an answer to the Hindu Candi, Candika, and Camunda.

Classification

Minor goddess of the Buddhist Mahayana tradition. Fierce lady of the wrathful mandalas.

Domains

Wrathful protection under the Mahayana frame, the cutting edge of compassionate action, the fierce aspect of the feminine awakened mind, the pastoral offering of grass and antelope carried into the mandala from the wider Indian iconography of the wilderness sage.

Iconography

Depicted of yellow color, standing upon a corpse in the wrathful stance, holding grass in one hand and an antelope in the other. The corpse beneath her feet marks her cutting of ignorance and the extinguishing of the deluded self; the yellow color links her to the wisdom of equanimity and the ratna, the jewel family of the mandala; and the grass and antelope, which are the attributes of the wandering sage in the Indian iconographic vocabulary, mark her as a goddess who carries into her wrathful office the peaceable emblems of the forest ascetic.

Attributes

Grass held in one hand, antelope held in the other, and the corpse beneath her feet.

Colors

Yellow.

Sacred Sites

Within the mandalas of the Mahayana and Vajrayana traditions in which she appears as a retinue goddess of the wrathful cycles, particularly in Tibetan and Newar Buddhist practice traditions.

Ritual

Her worship is undertaken within the transmitted sadhanas of the mandalas in which she appears, and outside those transmissions she is not approached as a private deity. In her proper office as a retinue figure she is invoked to hold the fierce protective ground on which the central yidam of the mandala works.

Lore

Candesvari holds a distinctive place in the Buddhist iconographic imagination as a wrathful goddess whose emblems are peaceable rather than martial. Where other fierce Mahayana goddesses carry the standard tantric weapons, Candesvari carries the grass and the antelope of the forest sage, so that her ferocity is understood not as opposed to but as continuous with the peaceable wisdom of the ascetic. Her yellow color places her in the ratna family of the mandala, whose wisdom is the wisdom of equanimity and the recognition of every being as a jewel, and her wrathful stance on the corpse is that recognition given cutting form.
