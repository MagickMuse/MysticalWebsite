Overview

Ushas, Goddess of Dawn, is the twin sister of Rati, Goddess of Night. Their kinship is variously remembered as partnership, friendship, or bitter rivalry. Ushas brings the light that heralds each new day, driving a chariot drawn by seven cows or seven mares whose wings are stained saffron. Her name may be rendered as to burn. Demons and malignant spirits flee and hide from her the way roaches scatter when a kitchen light flicks on. She wears saffron-colored robes and a veil, and she reveals herself in full only to enlightened adepts.

In Vedic literature

Goddess of dawn

The Rg-veda presents Ushas as the daily arrival of light itself. She shows herself in the first colors of morning, imagined as a young woman riding forth in a great train of chariots. The sun god Surya rides behind her, driving her on through the sky. The hymns praise her, and petition her, for sweeping away the heavy darkness, and they call on her to scatter and banish evil demons.

Rouser of life

Dawn is the moment when the world begins moving again, and the hymns credit Ushas with that work directly. She is said to rouse every living thing, to set the world into motion, and to dispatch people to their daily duties. She lifts the curled-up sleepers from their beds and sends them out to perform their sacrifices, which in turn means she serves the other gods by delivering them their offerings. She grants strength and fame to her devotees and is named the impulse behind life itself, tied to the breath of every living creature.

Keeper of rta

Ushas is bound up with rta, the order that runs through cosmos, society, and ethics. Her predictable daily return is itself a sign and an enactment of that order, which makes her an enemy of the chaotic forces that would tear the world apart. Because the light she brings exposes whatever darkness was hiding, she is also treated as a witnessing presence and given the title eye of the gods. She is called she who sees all, though her vigilance is not usually appealed to as a way of asking forgiveness for sin; petitioners more often ask her to use her gaze and her power to drive away or to punish their enemies.

Auspicious and bountiful

Ushas is consistently approached as an auspicious goddess, linked to light and to wealth, and she is repeatedly likened to a cow. The Rg-veda names her the mother of cows, and the comparison is made literal in the imagery of her body: just as a cow lets down her udder to feed people, Ushas bares her breasts to release light for the sake of humankind.

Maiden and mother

Although she is most often portrayed as a young, beautiful maiden, the hymns also assign her clearly maternal roles. She is named the mother of the gods, the mother of the Asvins, a mother to her own petitioners, and a matron who tends to every household task. She is even named the goddess of the hearth.

Huntress of life and mistress of time

Alongside her brightness, Ushas carries a sober and almost grim aspect. She is called the goddess, the reality, or the presence that wears away youth. The hymns describe her as a skilled huntress who slowly consumes the lives of those she touches. In keeping with rta she wakes everything that lives, but she does not disturb the one who sleeps in death. Her recurring return is therefore both a gift and a measurement: petitioners ask her for long life because each new dawn is itself a quiet reminder of how little time mortals have. She is, in the end, the mistress of time and the marker by which it is counted.

Standing among the Vedic deities

Ushas is the most popular goddess of the Rg-veda by the measures the corpus itself supplies. She is named in more hymns than any other goddess, and a larger number of hymns are addressed specifically to her than to any of her sisters. Even with that distinction, the Vedic pantheon ranks her only as a deity of the third order. The most powerful figures in the corpus are male, and none of the goddesses, Ushas included, rises to the standing of the great male gods.

Fate in later Hinduism

Ushas is one of the Vedic goddesses who does not carry forward into the later Hindu tradition. Where Prthivi and Sarasvati persist and grow, Ushas simply fades from view: she is rarely found in post-Vedic texts, and the dawn no longer concentrates into a personified goddess of her stature. Her devotional weight in the Rg-veda is therefore best appreciated within the Vedic horizon itself rather than in any continuous line of later worship.
