Life and History

Margaret, known as The Pearl of Scotland, was born around 1045 in southern Hungary, daughter of the German princess Agatha and the exiled English prince Edward Atheling (also known as Edward the Exile or Edward the Outlaw). Her paternal grandfather was English king Edmund Ironside.

Royal Lineage and Exile

Edward the Outlaw was exiled from England after the Danish conquest in 1016, but many countrymen considered him the true heir to the throne. Margaret was raised at the court of King Stephen, Hungary's first Christian king.

In 1057, the family moved to England after being summoned by the childless reigning king, expecting Edward to be made heir. However, Edward died of mysterious causes, possibly poison, just two days after arriving. Margaret's brother Edgar was widely considered heir presumptive, though never officially declared.

Storm-Driven to Scotland

The family remained in England until the Norman Conquest of 1066. Feeling vulnerable, Agatha decided to return to Hungary with her children. Fierce gale storms drove their ship up the Firth of Forth to Dumfermline, Scotland, where King Malcolm III welcomed them and soon requested Margaret's hand.

Queen of Scotland

King Malcolm Canmore was the son of King Duncan, whose assassination inspired Shakespeare's Macbeth. Malcolm defeated Macbeth in 1057 and married Margaret between 1068 and 1070. They had eight children. Several sons became kings of Scotland, while daughter Edith (Matilda) married England's King Henry I.

Charity and Religious Reform

Margaret devoted herself to charity, sponsoring the elderly, orphans, and the ailing. She fasted so extremely that she developed permanent digestive disorders. In 1069, King Malcolm, encouraged by Margaret, officially abolished the Celtic Church in Scotland in favor of Roman Catholicism.

Death

Margaret died in Edinburgh Castle on 16 November 1093, three days after her husband and eldest son fell in battle against English forces.

The Immovable Body

Canonized in 1250, officials decided to move her relics from Dumfermline. When her tomb was opened on 19 July 1297, all attempts to lift her body failed. She was impossibly heavy. Stronger and stronger men tried without success. Finally, someone suggested Margaret did not want to be separated from her husband. Immediately her body became light enough to lift. Both Margaret and Malcolm were moved together under the high altar.

The Wandering Relics

When Scotland became Protestant, the bodies were allegedly moved to Spain, where King Philip II enshrined them. Margaret's head was possessed by Mary Queen of Scots and traveled to Fife, Antwerp, and back to Scotland, displayed for veneration as late as 1785. The whereabouts of her head and both bodies are currently unknown.

Sacred Sites

Margaret's empty grave at Dumfermline, Saint Margaret's Chapel (Edinburgh's oldest building), Saint Margaret's Hope (the Fife bay where her ship landed), Margaret's cave in Dumfermline (her spiritual retreat), and the Wemyss Caves in Fife.

Feast Days

10 June (Vatican since 1693), 16 November (still celebrated in Scotland)
