Spirit Procession

The Wild Hunt is the name given to a wild procession of spirits that rides on windy, stormy nights as well as on specific dates: Halloween, May Eve, and Midsummer's Eve. It is particularly active during winter, especially the Yuletide season. Not only the Hunt's spirits are roaming. Trooping Fairies ride in cavalcade especially on full moon nights and on Halloween, May Eve, and Midsummer's Eve, and their territories often overlap.

The tradition of a parade of spirits, which may or may not be accompanied by the spirits of the dead and living devotees, appears around the world. Legends of Hawaiian Night Marchers predate European contact; Japan's Hyakki Yako names the Night Parade of at least one hundred spirits.

Nature and Behavior

Sometimes the Hunt troops and marches like soldiers; sometimes its riders are wild revelers or marauders. Dionysus and Shiva are among the most famous leaders of spirit parades. Some processions welcome participation from anyone who likes reveling with ghosts and spirits; other processions, like the Night Marchers, are more exclusive. Human reactions to the Wild Hunt vary. These spirits are powerful and unpredictable, and it is usually considered advisable to stay out of their way. Magical practitioners often seek to observe or join it.

Dead Souls and Divine Escorts

In some European traditions, dead souls periodically travel in procession to visit their families, led by deities who bridge thresholds. Freya, Berchta, Herta, and Hulda all serve as leaders. The Hunt may also enforce justice: a Danish runestone concludes with the warning “A rati be he who destroys this stone,” where a rati is a person whose soul has been taken and is driven forever by the Wild Hunt.

Under Christianity

Under Christianity, the nature of the Hunt changed. No longer sufficient to merely avoid it for fear of being swept up, it became considered sinful even to watch it pass. The Hunt became associated with witchcraft, and those who sought to participate or were perceived as trapped by it were considered wicked witches or damned souls.

Once associated with souls of the dead seeking brief family reunions, the Wild Hunt became associated with the punishments of Hell. The spirit who heads the Hunt became a literal headhunter, searching for transgressors and forcing them to join a host of imprisoned souls forever. That host included those who fell outside Church sacraments: unbaptized babies, illegitimate children, major sinners, suicides, and those deprived of funeral rites. Heathens, Jews, and witches were allegedly among those riding. The traveling souls of shamans were allegedly fated to join the Hunt if they were unable to rejoin their bodies.

Leaders

Spirits who lead the Wild Hunt include Arawn, Berchta, Diana, Freya, Frau Gaude, Herodias, Herta, Hulda, and Odin.

The Nocturnal Parade

The Wild Hunt is a nighttime procession of spirits that rides several times a year, usually on power dates such as May Eve (Walpurgis Night), Halloween, or Midsummer's Eve. Because most accounts were filtered through Christian sources, and because many of the spirits involved may truly hold a grudge against people, meeting the Hunt is considered very dangerous; the Spirit Hunters are said to hunt human souls. People are told to stay indoors when the Hunt is expected, or to lie flat and silent in its presence and hope to go unnoticed. Witches, by contrast, traditionally rush out to join the revelry and are apparently welcomed by the Hunters.

The Parsley Password

Anyone who meets the Hunt may avert danger by politely asking the Hunters for parsley, a plant sacred to Hunt leaders such as Holle and Odin. "Parsley" is said to be the secret password to safety.
