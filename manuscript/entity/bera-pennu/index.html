Bera Pennu is a vegetation goddess of the Khond people, worshipped in Bengal in what is now northeastern India. She presides over the fertility of the land and the health of the community, and was historically petitioned to ensure a good harvest, especially of the spice turmeric, and to protect the community from disease and infirmity.

Domains

Vegetation, agricultural fertility, the harvest, and protection from illness.

Sacred Sites

Bengal and the surrounding Khond territories of northeastern India.

Historical Practice: The Meriah Rite

Historically, Bera Pennu was the recipient of the meriah rite, a form of human sacrificial offering practiced by the Khond community of Bengal until it was suppressed under British colonial administration in the mid nineteenth century. The rite was understood locally as the goddess's due, exchanged for continued fertility of the land, protection from famine, and the community's safety from disease and infirmity.

The meriah, or sacrificial person, was traditionally youthful and was often kept for years, sometimes an entire lifetime, as a consecrated holy figure within the village before their death. The community treated the meriah with veneration during this period, and the person's status marked them as belonging wholly to the goddess. A meriah was drawn from one of two sources: either the child of a previous meriah, inheriting the sacred role by birth, or a child purchased from an impoverished family who consented to the arrangement in exchange for support.

The rite itself was preceded by several days of communal festivities, feasting, music, and ceremonial preparation. The meriah was then put to death by one of several traditional methods. In many cases the person was strangled, sometimes in the fork of a tree specially chosen for the purpose. In other regional variants the meriah was killed by the community with edged tools while still living, the wounds inflicted so that each family or clan received a portion of the goddess's offering. Following the death, the remains were divided and buried across the fields of the community so that the fertility of Bera Pennu's body would pass directly into the soil, ensuring the harvest, especially the harvest of turmeric, and protecting the village from disease.

British suppression of the meriah rite in the 1830s and 1840s, undertaken by the Meriah Agency, forcibly ended the practice through a combination of intervention, negotiation, and rescue of intended meriah children. Khond devotion to Bera Pennu continued in transformed form, with animal offerings, especially buffalo, taking the place of human sacrifice, and with the goddess still honored as the sustainer of the harvest and guardian of communal health.

This section is presented as ethnographic and historical record. It documents a practice that belonged to a specific community and era, and it is neither endorsement nor a devotional model for modern petitioners.

Ritual

Contemporary devotion to Bera Pennu emphasizes offerings of turmeric, first fruits of the harvest, and gratitude for the fertility of the land. Petition her for abundant crops, resilient health, and protection of the household from disease.
