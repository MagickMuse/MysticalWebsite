Magickal Properties

Mysterious figure from Knights Templar trial accusations whose actual existence remains uncertain. Accounts derive solely from torture-obtained confessions during 1312 heresy trials. Contradictory testimony describes variously as skull, bearded head, multi-faced head, black cat, or goat-headed figure. Modern occult understanding based primarily on Eliphas Levi's 1854 image depicting hermaphroditic goat-headed figure symbolizing balance of opposites.

Ritual Uses

Historical ritual uses unknown and likely fabricated under torture. Modern practitioners work with Baphomet symbol for integration of opposites, balance of masculine and feminine energies, and hermetic principle of "as above, so below." Levi's image serves as prototype for many Tarot depictions of The Devil card.

Offerings

No traditional offerings known from medieval period. Modern practitioners determine offerings based on personal understanding of the symbol.

Favored People

Modern occultists, Tarot practitioners, those studying Templar mysteries, members of Ordo Templi Orientalis. Aleister Crowley adopted magickal name Baphomet when joining OTO.

Petition For

Balance of complementary opposites, integration of masculine and feminine principles, hermetic wisdom, understanding of duality and unity.

Warnings

Existence highly uncertain. All medieval accounts obtained through severe torture of Knights Templars. Twelve of 231 knights examined acknowledged idol's existence under torture. Inquisitors perceived Baphomet as Satan and were uninterested in distinguishing Pagan traditions from devil-worship. No idol ever presented as physical evidence.

Additional Notes

The Templar Mystery

The Knights Templar began in the twelfth century as an order devoted to protecting pilgrims traveling to Jerusalem. Because the Knights were exempt from taxation, they amassed enormous wealth and, with it, power, until they became a threat to the establishment and were persecuted. In 1312, the Knights Templars were accused of heresy and the order violently suppressed. Central to accusations was that knights adored an idol named Baphomet in initiation ceremonies. Whether Baphomet ever existed, whether Templars adopted other spiritual traditions, or whether multiple idols existed remains unknown. Contradictory testimony suggests men were not describing the same thing.

Name Etymology Theories

Standard explanation was that Baphomet corrupted "Mahomet" (archaic spelling of Prophet Muhammad's name), suggesting Christian-Islamic syncretism. However, this ignores Islamic total abhorrence of idolatry. No Islamic tradition resembles Templar accusations. Three possibilities: Baphomet did not exist; Templars engaged in Christian devil-worship; Templars engaged in Pagan practices related to horned gods.

Levi's Iconic Image

Eliphas Levi's 1854 drawing created the modern image: masculine body with female breasts topped by crowned goat head. Upright pentacle on forehead. Right hand points to white crescent moon, left to black crescent moon ("as above, so below"). Scaly belly like snake or dragon. Black feathered wings (angel wings, not bat wings). Upright caduceus placed phallically between legs, obscuring genitalia. Figure combines male and female anatomy indicating perfect balance. Black and white crescents reminiscent of yin-yang symbol. Some depictions also place a blazing torch between the horns, a flame of illumination raised between the polarities.

The Sigil of Baphomet

Distinct from Levi's full figure is the Sigil of Baphomet, also called the Goat of Mendes or the Sabbatic Goat. It features an inverted pentagram containing the head of a goat, with the upward V of the star framing the horns. The image became an icon of modern occultism and has often been treated as a representation of the Devil, an association amplified when Anton LaVey adopted it in the 1960s for the Church of Satan. Some versions encircle the symbol with a double ring of Hebrew letters spelling Leviathan.
