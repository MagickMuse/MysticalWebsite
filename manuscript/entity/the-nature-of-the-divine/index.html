Entity Grimoire

The Nature of the Divine

Setting aside the notion of a single Supreme Being for a moment, it seems logical to suppose that the vast pantheon of Gods and Goddesses found across all cultures and societies is an extrapolation of human potential, one more way we have discovered of defining the Universe and our place within it. We project our own qualities onto the sky and the landscape around us, amplifying them into divine beings.

Considering how much time our ancestors must have spent gazing up at the Heavens, from which came the rain and Sun that nurtured the crops they depended upon, it is not surprising that so many Gods and Goddesses emanate from the sky. Every planet in our solar system carries the name, and the supposed soul, of an immortal personage. Other aspects of the natural world are deified too. Animistic belief holds that every tree, rock, mountain, and body of water has its own presiding spirit. Pan offers a striking example of how the old Gods endure and change: the powerful Nature God, venerated for thousands of years, still bears a distinct resemblance to the popular image of the Devil.

Archetypes and Projection

Our Gods represent archetypes and work on two levels. They manifest within us, and we also project ourselves onto them as external phenomena. Just as an isolated mind will conjure a face and a personality onto a simple object in order to have company, our ancestors projected characters and thoughts onto the effigies of their Gods.

The adventures and escapades of many deities read like the biographies of superheroes. They walk on water, they fly, they hurl thunderbolts across the Heavens, and they shape-shift into animals and birds. It is notable, too, that the qualities of the Gods repeat themselves in figures from different cultures. The great, multi-talented divinity the ancient Egyptians called Thoth appears in Celtic myth as Lugh, in Greek mythology as Hermes, and as Mercury for the Romans.

Attributes and Symbols

While the Gods may be symbols in themselves, the objects and animals associated with them reveal their talents. The Goddess of the Hunt carries a bow and arrow; the Goddess of the Harvest, her sheaf of corn. Athena, known to the Romans as Minerva, is the Goddess of Wisdom and appears with the owl, whose large-eyed gaze and access to the hidden places of the night give the bird itself a link to concealed or occult knowledge. Wings imply communication between humankind and the Heavens.

Powers and Obeisance

Many deities hold jurisdiction over the elements and the weather, as well as over areas of human endeavor that carry a life-or-death element, such as fishing, the hunt, and the harvest. It was therefore important to keep these beings content, something achieved through ritual obeisance and sacrifice. The influence of our divinities is far reaching, informing a great deal of our language and culture and surfacing in fictional characters, brand names, art, and literature. The Gods also inspire allegiance, becoming the focus of whole peoples and groups.

The Universal Spirit

Returning to the matter of a Supreme Being: overriding all these divine powers, for many, is the idea of a universal spirit, a pervading life-force that we are each a part of and that is a part of us. For the sake of argument, we might call it The Thing. The difficulty begins the moment we try to give The Thing a name, because the very thing that cannot be compartmentalized then becomes segregated by the nuances of language, even assigned a sex as male or female, mother or father. The idea is that The Thing lies beyond personification, and yet we personify it and put words into its mouth.

References to a nameless God appear again and again. The Celtiberians, who lived in north-central Spain and northern Portugal both before and during the Roman Empire, went out every full Moon and danced before their doors in homage to this nameless God. The Aztecs, too, worshipped a God so powerful as to lie beyond any name we could comprehend. Because names carry such power, the fact that there are either many names for God or none at all is a reminder of the paradoxical nature of a concept that wordy description only serves to confuse further.

The Word Pagan

There need be no great divide between those who worship many Gods and those who venerate only one. Worshippers of more than one God fall into the category of pagans, a word whose origins are quite innocuous: it comes from the Latin paganus, meaning "villager" or "rustic."

Religious Associations

  • Many of the old Gods were absorbed into the Christian faith as saints, since a multitude of different Gods was at odds with a single jealous God. Those that could not be absorbed were demonized, turned into malevolent, ungodly beings.
  • In the Book of Exodus, the unnameable is personified when God is quoted as saying, "I am that I am."
  • In the Hindu pantheon, the many Gods are understood as separate aspects of the One, parts of a single mosaic whose full picture appears only to the observer who stands back far enough to see it. Such devotion can also inspire strong allegiance, as among the Shaivites who follow Shiva.
