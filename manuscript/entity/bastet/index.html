Magickal Properties

  • Great Egyptian cat goddess with dominion over sex, fertility, marriage, magick, music, childbirth, prosperity, joy, dance, and healing
  • Protects humans against infertility, dangers of childbirth, evil spirits, illness, and bodily injuries (especially from venomous creatures)
  • Matron of magicians and healers who bestows "life, prosperity and health every day and long life and beautiful old age"
  • Her annual festival in Bubastis was Egypt's most popular, celebrating female sexuality and generative power

Ritual Uses

  • Petition for fertility, safe childbirth, and protection of women and children
  • Invoke for magickal workings, healing rituals, and celebrations of joy and prosperity
  • Her festival involved boats, dancing, sexual celebration, and ritual anasuromai (lifting skirts)

Offerings

Precious cream perfumes (her name may relate to "perfume"), offerings that benefit cats as her sacred animals

Favored People

Women, children, magicians, healers, pregnant women, those seeking life's pleasures

Petition For

Fertility, safe childbirth, healing, protection from evil spirits and venomous creatures, joy, prosperity, musical/dance inspiration

Warnings

Approach with respect for her protective nature. Her cult celebrated sexuality, not appropriate for those uncomfortable with such themes.

Additional Notes

  • Depicted as cat (often bejeweled) or woman with cat's head, sometimes with kittens
  • Attributes: Basket and ankh (symbol of life)
  • Planets: Sun and moon
  • Sacred animals: Domestic cats
  • May be wife/daughter of Ra; sons include Maahes, Khonsu, Nefertem

Role in Egyptian Magick

The Hunter and Protector

In Egyptian magickal practice, cats were admired as hunters, and hunting served as a metaphor for overcoming dangerous forces. A tawny wildcat helped the sun-god Ra defeat the evil serpent Apep. In illustrated versions of the Book of the Dead, a cat looking rather pleased with itself slices a snake's body in two with a knife.

This spell animates a cat amulet (specified as lapis lazuli, though faience was more common):

"Oh cat of lapis lazuli, great of forms, mistress of the embalming house, grant offerings to So-and-so in the Beautiful West!"

Being "great of forms" meant having multiple manifestations or bau, a sign of how powerful a deity was.

Fertility and Maternal Care

Without veterinary sterilization, a healthy female cat can give birth to a litter of kittens every few months. This made the cat a powerful symbol of fertility and maternal care. Bronze cat sculptures and cat amulets proliferated as protective and healing symbols, particularly for women and children.

Sacred Cat Mummies

Thousands of mummified cats and kittens were deposited at temple sites and cemeteries as offerings to Bastet. Most were hastened to the afterlife by human hands, snapped necks or drowning provided embalmers with bodies for mummification. Some "cat mummies" contained only earth and pebbles. It was the wrapping and priestly blessing that mattered when offering a cat mummy and asking for divine intervention.

From Lioness to Cat

In her earliest forms, Bastet had a lioness's head like her sister Sekhmet. The two may represent different aspects of the same primordial power, Sekhmet the fierce, destructive heat of the sun, and Bastet the gentler, protective warmth. Later, Bastet appeared as a beautiful woman with the head of a domesticated cat, embodying the cat's qualities of maternal care, sensuality, and grace.

Historical Context

Bastet's worship spans from circa 2700 BCE to the end of Egyptian history, around 400 CE. Her cult centers are at Bubastis in the Delta region of Lower Egypt and probably at the Karnak temple complex in Upper Egypt. In the sanctuary of Khafre at Giza, her name is engraved on the facade beside that of the goddess Hathor, together symbolizing the protectresses of north and south respectively. In Hellenic times she was partly syncretized with Artemis. Art references include sculptures, wall paintings, and papyrus illustrations, with literary attestation reaching back to the Middle Kingdom coffin texts.

Iconography and Etymology

The hieroglyph in her name depicts a sealed alabaster jar containing perfume. She was first depicted as a lioness, and then in the guise of a cat from circa 1000 BCE onward, when her character becomes more peaceable. Texts recounting battles may describe the pharaoh's enemies being slaughtered like the victims of Bastet, invoking her as the instrument of the sun god's vengeance, the "rage in his eye." Cat cemeteries containing mummified animals have been found at various sites.

Parentage and Family

Bastet is the daughter of the sun god Re and is regarded as his instrument of vengeance. In an alternate tradition she is the eldest daughter of Amun. Her son is the lion-headed god Mihos, also called Maahes.
