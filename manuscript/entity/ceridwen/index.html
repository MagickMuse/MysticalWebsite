Ceridwen

Overview

Ceridwen is a goddess of inspiration in the Celtic tradition of Wales, honored as the hag-aspect of the mother goddess, the fierce dark face of the maternal divine. She is the consort of Tegid Foel and the mother of Creirwy, a daughter said to be the most beautiful girl in the world, and Afagddu, a son said to be the ugliest boy in the world, so that the two extremes of physical form are gathered under her as their mother. She is the keeper of the cauldron of knowledge and inspiration, and her myth is the central Welsh tale of the creation of the awen, the divine poetic breath that gives the poet the gift of inspired speech.

Classification

Goddess of inspiration in the Welsh Celtic tradition. Hag-aspect of the mother goddess. Keeper of the cauldron of knowledge.

Domains

Inspiration and the poetic gift, the awen or divine creative breath, the cauldron of knowledge and transformation, the hag-aspect of the mother in which fierce wisdom replaces gentle nurture, the turning of darkness into illumination, the initiation of the poet or seer through a process of death and rebirth.

Iconography

Depicted as an old woman, the hag-mother, sometimes bent and dark, sometimes with the dignity of a queen. Her most important attribute is the cauldron, which she tends over a year and a day, stirring the brew that will confer the gift of awen or divine inspiration. She is also depicted in her transformed shapes, particularly the hawk that pursues the transformed boy who has stolen a drop of her potion, and the hen that swallows him whole.

Attributes

Cauldron, herb-gathering basket, the drops of awen, the sow or pig as her most ancient animal (reflecting a pre-bard-tradition layer of the goddess), the hawk and hen of her shape-shifting pursuit.

Sacred Sites

The Welsh landscape of Tegid's Lake, Llyn Tegid in what is now Bala in Gwynedd, is traditionally associated with Ceridwen and her household. The cauldron itself belongs to no fixed site but moves through the narrative as the mobile center of her mystery.

Ritual

Honored by Welsh Celtic reconstructionists, bards, and devotees of the awen. Her cauldron is one of the great symbols of the Welsh bardic tradition, and the Three Cauldrons of tradition (including Ceridwen's) were understood as the vessels in which the great gifts of the culture were prepared. Offerings to her include grain, herbs, dark berries, and the gifts of the harvest placed near water. She is invoked at the dark of the year and at the turning of the bardic year.

Lore

The central myth of Ceridwen is the story of the creation of the bard Taliesin. Ceridwen prepares her cauldron of knowledge over a year and a day, intending the gift of awen for her ugly son Afagddu to compensate for his lack of physical beauty. A boy named Gwion Bach is set to stir the cauldron; three drops of the hot potion fall on his thumb, he licks them off, and instantly receives the gift of all knowledge. Understanding that Ceridwen will destroy him for this theft, he flees. She pursues him through a long shape-shifting chase in which he becomes a hare, a fish, a bird, and a grain of wheat, while she becomes a greyhound, an otter, a hawk, and finally a hen. As a hen she swallows the wheat, and nine months later gives birth to a child so radiant that she cannot bring herself to kill him. She sets him adrift on the sea in a leather bag; he is found and becomes the great bard Taliesin, whose name means shining brow. This myth is among the most important narratives of the Welsh bardic tradition and holds the theology of the awen in its story-form, in which the gift of divine inspiration is something stolen from the fierce mother-goddess and transformed through initiation into a new life.
