The Egyptian deity Isis has countless epithets: "Mistress of Magic," "She Who is Rich in Spells," "Great of Sorcery," "Speaker of Spells," "The Great Witch," and "The Many Named" are just a few. Magick and witchcraft are central to her myth and identity. Isis casts spells and utters incantations. According to the Egyptologist E.A. Wallis Budge, her "mouth was trained to perfection and she made no mistake in pronouncing her spells."

Regarded as the supreme Mother Goddess and the parent of all other deities, Isis personifies all the qualities of the Goddesses. Her contributions to civilization were far-reaching: she taught the women of Egypt to spin, weave, and grind corn, and taught the men the art of healing the sick. She introduced the concept of marriage and domesticity. Drops of her blood gave life to every living creature in the Universe.

The Ankh, the magickal symbol of rebirth, belongs to Isis. Her name has long been associated with mystery cults and secret organizations, among them the Fellowship of Isis.

Isis was not an obscure goddess but was worshipped as a primary deity for thousands of years. Originating in Egypt, her worship eventually stretched from East Africa throughout Western Asia and Europe as far as the Rhine and England's Thames River.

Birth and Magickal Origins

Magick enters Isis' life even before her first breath. Because her mother's pregnancy breaks a spiritual injunction, she is cursed and unable to give birth. Lord Thoth, Egypt's baboon-headed inventor of magick, secretly loves Isis' mother. He creates the magickal device of dice and gambles with the moon god, who controls the calendar. Thoth wins and is able to magickally reconfigure the calendar, enabling his beloved to deliver quadruplets: Isis, her sister Nephthys, and brothers Set and Osiris.

Love and Soulmates

Osiris and Isis fell in love in the womb; their love will transcend death. The two epitomize soulmates, albeit star-crossed ones. Thoth adores Isis and serves as her godfather, instructing her in the magickal arts until her powers outshine his.

The Mistress of Magick

Isis repeatedly proves herself to be the supreme Mistress of Magick:

  • She learns Ra's true name, the ineffable name of power, with which she can stop the sun in the sky
  • She resurrects her brother-lover Osiris from the dead to magickally conceive their son, Horus
  • She performs miraculous acts of healing magick throughout her myths

Sacred Associations

Sacred Animals: Snakes, crocodiles, cows, scorpions, and kites (a type of raptor)

Sacred Minerals: Bloodstone

Botanicals: Vervain and myrrh

Celestial Associations: Water, the moon, and the constellation Virgo. She protects travelers at sea.

Agricultural Role: Isis is a grain spirit, a Corn Mother who ensures abundance and sustenance.

The Corn Mother

At the very root and basis of her myth, Isis is identified as a Corn Mother. According to legend, Isis was the first to discover wild barley and wheat. At her festival, stalks of these grains were carried in procession. After Isis' discovery of grain, her brother-lover Osiris traveled around Egypt (and eventually the ancient world) introducing the concept of its cultivation. Because of these actions, Osiris is credited in Egyptian mythology as the founder of civilization.

Osiris does not just teach about grain: he is the grain and thus is eventually cut down in his prime, his body is chopped up and scattered throughout Egypt. Although Isis mourns him, she is also actively involved in the process of the harvest: she unearths each piece of Osiris' body, collecting it in her winnowing sieve. Isis' actions may have served as role models for later harvest rituals. Harvesting was a solemn occasion; one mourned for the grain even though it was necessary to cut it down, winnow, and grind it. At harvest-time, when ancient Egyptian reapers cut their first stalks, they beat their breasts in lamentation while calling upon Isis.

Worship and Offerings

No blood sacrifice existed among Isis' rites. She accepts offerings of milk, honey, flowers, herbs, and incense.

The Cult of Isis in Rome

The cult of Isis was officially introduced to Rome in 86 BCE and became very popular because, unlike other cults, hers was open to all: not only free men but women and slaves. The tradition developed a bad reputation in conservative Rome because of the alleged licentiousness of its rites, and it was suppressed at least five times between 59 and 48 BCE.

Temple Closure and Underground Survival

The last official temple of Isis stood on the southern Egyptian island of Philae. In 537 CE Narses, Commander of Emperor Justinian's Egyptian troops, ordered the temple closed.

But Isis went underground. Devotees refused to abandon her and found masks with which to camouflage their rituals, including those of Kwan Yin and the Virgin Mary.

Survival in Christian Iconography

Statues of the Madonna and Child are identical in form to those of Isis and baby Horus. One interpretation of Europe's Black Madonnas is that they represent Isis in disguise.

Images of Isis, Horus, and his elder half-brother Anubis (the "fore-runner") may be understood to survive in images of the Madonna, Baby Jesus, and his elder cousin, John the Baptist (the "fore-runner"). Through these sacred masks, worship of the Great Goddess continued unbroken across millennia.

Weret Hekau: Great Lady of Magick

Isis holds the title Weret Hekau, translated as "Great Lady of Magick." The plural form hekau indicates mastery over multiple magickal disciplines rather than a single art. She obtained this supreme authority through calculated deception.

The aging sun god Ra had grown feeble. Isis collected saliva that fell from his mouth, combined it with earth, and fashioned a serpent. She animated the creature and positioned it where Ra would walk. The serpent struck. Poison flooded his divine body.

Ra summoned help. Isis arrived and offered healing, demanding one thing in return: his true name. Ra recited title after title, epithet after epithet. Isis rejected each. Only when blindness overtook him did Ra surrender. The name passed from his essence into hers. From that moment, no being possessed greater magickal authority.

The Tyet: Knot of Isis

The tyet symbol, also known as the Knot of Isis or Blood of Isis, serves as her primary sacred emblem. Its shape resembles an ankh whose horizontal arms fold downward. Red coloring traditionally represents blood and vital force.

Embalmers placed tyet amulets on wrapped bodies to invoke her guardianship. The associated incantation (Book of the Dead, Spell 156) affirms: "You have your blood, Isis; you have your power, Isis; you have your magick, Isis."

Royal burial chambers display tyet symbols alternating with djed pillars sacred to Osiris. Together they represent feminine and masculine generative energies working in concert against death.

Protector in the Marshes

Set murdered Osiris and seized power. Isis escaped, carrying her unborn son. Deep in the Nile Delta's papyrus swamps, she delivered Horus and raised him in hiding. Every magickal skill she possessed went toward shielding him from Set's hunters and natural dangers: venomous creatures, predators, disease.

Egyptian protective magick draws heavily on this narrative. Healing spells reference her fierce defense of the vulnerable child. The underlying logic is straightforward: magick strong enough to guard a divine infant from the lord of chaos can guard any child from any harm.

Recovering Osiris from the Water Lilies

Egyptian lotus and water lily blooms adorn the body of Osiris in tomb art, where the flowers carry his anti-aphrodisiacal current. After Set killed Osiris and chopped him into pieces, Isis collected all his parts, minus one, and revived him just enough to ensure he could pass on to Ra. Early Egyptian tomb paintings show these lotus flowers cultivated in ponds alongside Papyrus.
