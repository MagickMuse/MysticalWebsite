Radha is the most beloved of Krishna's devotees and, in much of Hindu devotional theology, the embodiment of his essential divine power. She is the supreme lover — passionate, selfless, and unconventional — whose adulterous devotion to Krishna transcends social law and becomes the paradigm of the human soul's longing for the divine.

Overview

Radha, like Sita, is understood primarily in relation to a male consort. Throughout her history she has been inextricably associated with the god Krishna. Unlike Sita, however, Radha's relationship to Krishna is adulterous. Although she is married to another, she is passionately attracted to Krishna. Her illicit relationship with Krishna breaks all social norms and casts her in the role of one who willfully steps outside the realm of dharma to pursue her love. In contrast to Sita — the model of wifely devotion and loyalty — Radha invests her whole being in an adulterous affair with the irresistibly beautiful Krishna.

This relationship takes place during Krishna's youth, before his adult years when he marries Rukmini, Satyabhama, and others, and before his part in the Mahabharata war. Radha and Krishna's love affair takes place in the cowherd village of Vraja and in the woods and bowers of Vrindavana, a setting idyllic and removed from the pragmatic world of social duty — eternal spring, natural beauty, and the vigor of youth. In most ways Vraja is heaven on earth.

But all is not blissful fulfillment for Radha. Her liaison with Krishna is brief, and even at its passionate height Krishna arouses Radha's jealousy by consorting with other lovers. The theme of love in separation (viraha) is dominant in their relationship, particularly from Radha's point of view, and counterbalances the frenzy and ecstasy of their union.

Radha's popularity develops primarily in the context of devotion to Krishna. In religious movements in which devotion to the cowherd god is central — such as the Bengal Vaishnavas and the Vallabhacharins — Radha becomes the model of love for the Lord. The love affair of Radha and Krishna becomes a metaphor for the divine-human relationship: Radha represents the human devotee who gives up everything to cling to the Lord, and Krishna represents God, irresistibly beautiful and attractive. The aim of Krishna devotees is to develop or uncover the Radha dimension within themselves — that tendency within all human beings to devote themselves entirely and passionately to Krishna.

The Early History of Radha

Radha does not appear as a fully developed figure until quite late in the Hindu tradition. Prior to Jayadeva's Gitagovinda (twelfth century) she is mentioned in only a few brief references. The Padma-, Brahma-vaivarta-, and Devi-bhagavata-puranas, which describe her affair with Krishna in detail, are all considered late texts. Nevertheless, even in the early references her character is clearly suggested.

In these passages Radha is a lovesick girl or woman who is overcome by her emotions. The Venisamhara of Bhatta Narayana (before 800 CE) describes her as being angered while making love to Krishna and as leaving him while choking on her tears. In the Dhvanyalokalochana of Abhinavagupta (early tenth century) Radha weeps pitifully when Krishna leaves Vraja for Mathura. She is described in Ksemendra's Dasavataracharita (1066) as barely able to speak when Krishna leaves:

With tears, flowing away like life in Madhava's desertion, / Falling on her breasts' firm tips, Radha was like a laden kadamba tree / As tears were strewn by her endless sighing and trembling gait — / Darkened by the delusion that was bound to all her hopes, / She became like the new rainy season engulfed in darkness.

Although the adulterous aspect of her love is not mentioned explicitly in any of these early references, the centrality of love in separation (viraha) is clear in almost every one. It is probable that the authors of these earlier passages understood Radha to be married to another man — to be parakiya (belonging to another) in her relationship with Krishna. Another characteristic is also clear: she is always associated with Krishna. None of the references shows interest in Radha per se. In several texts Krishna is as hopelessly impassioned and maddened by love as Radha. From the Siddhahemasabdanushana of Hemachandra (1088–1172):

Though Hari sees every person with full regard, / Still his glance goes wherever Radha is — / Who can arrest eyes ensnared by love?

The Gopi Tradition

An important historical thread for understanding Radha is the mythological tradition surrounding Krishna's sojourn in Vraja and his dalliance with the gopis, the cowherd women of the village. This tradition dates back to the Harivamsha (ca. 500 CE) and is a central part of most later Vaishnava Puranas. The most detailed rendering prior to Jayadeva is found in the Bhagavata-purana (tenth century, South India).

According to this tradition, Krishna's father spirits him away from Mathura to escape his murderous uncle Kamsha. The infant Krishna is raised by the cowherd couple Nanda and Yasoda in the village of Vraja. On reaching adolescence, Krishna dallies with the women of the village. The cowherd women dote on him as a child, but their interest changes to passionate longing as he grows older. All are married women, but none can resist Krishna's beauty and charm. He retires to the woods and plays his flute on full-moon autumn nights. Hearing the music, the women are driven mad with passion and abandon their domestic roles entirely — jumping up in the middle of applying makeup, leaving food to burn on the stove, running out of their homes so frenzied that their clothes and jewelry come loose and fall off (Bhagavata-purana 10.29.3–7).

In the woods the gopis sport and play with Krishna, making love in the forest and in the waters of the Jumna River. In some accounts Krishna multiplies himself so that each woman has a Krishna to herself. The mood is joyous, festive, and erotic. The Bhagavata-purana is quite explicit that the gopis serve as a model for devotion: true devotion is highly emotional and causes horripilation, tears, loss of control, and frenzy (11.14.23–24). Those who love the Lord truly behave like the gopis — abandoning everything to be with him, incurring social censure, letting nothing come between themselves and the Lord.

No mention is made of Radha in any of these early accounts of the gopi tradition; the gopis are usually treated as a group. Radha, as we shall see, inherits this role in the later devotional movements, particularly in Bengal.

Radha as Belonging to Another (Parakiya)

Not until Jayadeva's Gitagovinda (twelfth century) do we find a sustained rendition of Radha as the central figure in the love drama. In this poem the tradition of Radha as Krishna's favorite and the tradition of the gopis came together. The poem is dominated by the lovesick Radha, who ventures out at night to search the woods for her lover. The whole flavor of the poem — written almost entirely from Radha's point of view — is one of love in separation (viraha).

The Gitagovinda shifts focus from the exterior landscape of the gopi tradition (lush pictures of Krishna's dalliance with hundreds of smitten women) to the interior landscape of a specific woman — her obsessive love, jealousy, and sorrow. Although Radha's marital status is not specified in the poem, hints throughout make clear her love is illicit: the drama takes place at night in secret, she has no formal claim on Krishna, and to be with him she must risk the dangers of the night, the woods, and public censure.

When spring came, tender-limbed Radha wandered / Like a flowering creeper in the forest wilderness, / Seeking Krishna in his many haunts. / The god of love increased her ordeal, / Tormenting her with fevered thoughts, / And her friend sang to heighten the mood.

Radha's illicit love for Krishna is central in the poetry of Vidyapati (1352–1448) and Candidas (ca. fourteenth to fifteenth century). Both make clear she is married to another man and risks social ostracism by pursuing her affair. Vidyapati portrays Radha as a woman of noble family who sacrifices her status and reputation by loving Krishna, a common villager:

I who body and soul / am at your beck and call, / was a girl of noble family. / I took no thought for what would be said of me, / I abandoned everything.

Candidas describes Radha as forthright and strong-willed, not secretive about her illicit love despite its dire social consequences. Realizing that adultery entails social censure, she knowingly and willfully chooses to love Krishna:

Casting away / All ethics of caste / My heart dotes on Krishna / Day and night. / The custom of the clan / Is a far-away cry / And now I know / That love adheres wholly / To its own laws.

Radha is rebellious in her attitude, cursing her fate and the society that would keep her married to her husband — whom she describes as a dolt — and away from Krishna:

I throw ashes at all laws / Made by man or god. / I am born alone, / With no companion. / What is the worth / Of your vile laws / That failed me / In love / And left me with a fool? / My wretched fate / Is so designed / That he is absent / For whom I long. / I will set fire to this house / And go away.

In the sixteenth century, Caitanya (1486–1533) arose in Bengal as a great devotee of Krishna who in his own devotion imitated the emotional traumas of the lovesick Radha. In the Bengal Vaishnava movement that followed, a devotee may approach the Lord in a variety of moods: contemplative, as servant, as friend, as parent, or as lover. These moods are ranked, with the mood of the lover affirmed as the best approach — the mood most cherished by the Lord himself — and of all lovers of the Lord, Radha is affirmed to be Krishna's favorite. The parakiya (belonging to another man) status of Radha is maintained in the mythology, worship, and theology of the Bengal Vaishnavas.

The Bengal Vaishnava theologians argued that the adulterous nature of Radha's love is appropriate as a devotional metaphor. Their main point is that illicit love is given freely, makes no legal claims, and as such is selfless. Married love (kama) functions according to rights and obligations in which both partners have specific expectations. Radha's love — illicit and adulterous though it may be — is characterized by prema (selfless love for the beloved), and selfless love is what Krishna desires. Furthermore, the long periods of separation and risk involved in illicit love serve only to intensify Radha's emotion; there are no obstacles in married love to heighten feeling.

The illicit nature of Radha's love was declared the orthodox position in a formal debate in 1717. The proponents of the parakiya position were declared the winners.

Radha as Belonging to Krishna (Svakiya)

Although Radha's position as Krishna's legal divine consort never became very popular in Krishna devotional movements, there is a sustained rendition in the Brahma-vaivarta-purana (considered late, ca. fifteenth to sixteenth century) in which Radha is cast in this role. Here she is inextricably associated with Krishna philosophically as his shakti — his underlying power, that dimension of himself that enables him to create the world and display himself in his various forms. Several passages affirm that her status is equal or even superior to his own. Krishna says to Radha:

As you are, so am I; there is certainly no difference between us. / As whiteness inheres in milk, as burning in fire, / so do I inhere in you always.

The text describes the two as initially forming an androgynous figure. Radha says:

I have been constructed by someone out of half your body; / Therefore there is no difference between us, and my heart is in you. / Just as my Self, heart, and life has been placed in you, / So has your Self, heart, and life been placed in me.

In several passages Radha is declared superior to Krishna himself — his support, his atman, the mother of the world. In one version of creation in the Brahma-vaivarta-purana, Krsna desires to create and divides himself into male and female. The two engage in cosmic sexual intercourse; eventually Radha gives birth to a golden egg, the universe itself. As cosmic queen, she is called mother of Vishnu, mother of the world, and mother of all.

However, Radha's love in this text takes on a possessive quality absent from earlier portraits. When she discovers Krishna dallying with other women, she is outraged, jealous, and vengeful. She terrifies Krishna in her rage and curses her rivals to miserable fates. Her love is described as demanding and insecure. The Brahma-vaivarta-purana Radha has gained metaphysical promotion at the expense of losing her intrinsic appeal as the lovesick girl of Vraja who can make no claims on her lover.

Radha as Hladini Shakti

Radha's status as a being worthy of worship by Krishna devotees is explained in the philosophic teachings of the Bengal Vaishnavas. Krishna, the ultimate godhead, includes within himself various shaktis (powers). His essential nature is displayed through his svarupa shakti (own-form power), within which is the hladini shakti — the power of bliss, the most refined essence of the godhead. This hladini shakti is identified with Radha herself.

If Radha is Krishna and Krishna is Radha, then Radha is essentially divine and worthy of the devotee's adoration. She herself is part of, indeed the essence of, the godhead. In this theological vision Radha has assumed the position of Krishna's eternal shakti. On the model of other divine couples — Shiva-Parvati, Rama-Sita, Vishnu-Lakshmi — Radha assumes the position of a heavenly deity whom the devotee supplicates.

The most sustained expression of Radha's divinity is found in the sixteenth-century dramas of Rupa Gosvamin, the leading theologian of Bengal Vaishnavism, who portrays Radha as the foremost lover of the Lord, her mind totally obsessed with Krishna:

Seeking to meditate for a moment upon Krishna, / The sage wrests his mind from the objects of sense; / This child [Radha] draws her mind away from Him / To fix it on mere worldly things. / The yogi yearns for a tiny flash of Krishna in his heart; / Look — this foolish girl strives to banish Him from hers!

In Rupa's plays, Krishna himself is pictured doting on Radha with the single-minded attention of a yogi: "Radha appears before me on every side; how is it that for me the three worlds have become Radha?"

The Radhavallabhins and the Sakhibhavas — movements arising in the sixteenth century in and around Brindaban — place Radha in a position of supreme centrality. The Sakhibhavas express their piety by becoming servants and friends of Radha, dressing as women and serving her in every way. As one member of these movements expressed it: "Krishna is the servant of Radha. He may do the coolie-work of building the world, but Radha sits as Queen. He is at best but her Secretary."
