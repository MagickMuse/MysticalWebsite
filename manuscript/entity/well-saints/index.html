Well Saints are renowned for healing through sacred wells and springs. They heal visual disorders, reproductive issues, and remove bad luck through therapeutic waters.

Classification

Category of saints associated with sacred wells and healing springs.

Overview

Since time immemorial, wells and fresh-water and mineral springs have been associated with healing and with spirits. People travel great distances to "take the waters" and receive healing or a therapeutic boost. Spas and medical complexes are built around these springs. Water may be drunk, or people may splash themselves with it or immerse in it. Sometimes physical contact is not necessary or possible; instead rituals are performed at wells, typically involving circumambulation (ritualized circling) or tying rags and ribbons to the well or nearby trees.

Wells and springs may have specialties. Some are acclaimed for healing visual or reproductive disorders, or for removing bad luck.

The Well Saint Phenomenon

Whether spirits are attracted to wells (hence the associations) or wells receive their power from the presence of spirits remains debated. A high percentage of saints are connected to healing wells and categorized as "well saints." This phenomenon transcends religious boundaries. There are Christian, Jewish, Muslim, and Zoroastrian well saints.

Many saints are credited with miraculously producing springs, which then become sources of further miracles. According to legend, Rome's Tre Fontane (Three Fountains) emerged where Saint Paul's severed head bounced three times after his execution. However, Paul's Roman springs existed in pre-Christian times as Aquae Salviae.

Lourdes is a significant exception. Saint Bernadette genuinely uncovered a previously unknown spring. However, scholars theorize that in most cases, traditions associated with wells were merely transferred to new religions so people could maintain spiritual practices important to them. Many Celtic saints are associated with miraculous wells because therapeutic water shrines were highly significant to pre-Christian Celtic traditions.

Common Practices

  • Drinking or bathing in the water
  • Circumambulation (ritual circling)
  • Tying rags, ribbons, or cloths to the well or nearby trees
  • Leaving offerings at the well
  • Collecting water for home use
  • Pilgrimage on the saint's feast day
