Saints Sergius and Bacchus were young Roman nobles and high-ranking legionnaires in the Roman army during the late third and early fourth centuries. They are venerated by most branches of Christianity.

Domains

Sergius and Bacchus are the official patron saints of the Byzantine army and remain patrons of soldiers. They are closely associated with wandering warriors like Crusaders, French legionnaires, and mercenaries. Saint Sergius specifically is the patron of those who work professionally with anise in any capacity, whether as growers, distillers of anisette, or creators of food products. He also patronizes desert nomads and hermits.

Iconography

The two are often depicted together as Roman soldiers. Bacchus appeared after his death to Sergius in the guise of an angel wearing military garb.

Sacred Sites

The Basilica of Saint Sergius in Rasafa, Syria (once called Sergiopolis in his honor), is a ruined fifth-century Byzantine church built over the grave site where Saint Sergius died. It was once a major pilgrimage site.

The Church of Saints Sergius and Bacchus in Istanbul, formerly Constantinople, is now a mosque known as the Ayasofya Camii or the Little Hagia Sophia because of its resemblance to the famous Hagia Sophia.

The Coptic Orthodox Church of Abu Serga in Cairo is built over a cave traditionally believed to have served as a refuge for the Holy Family during their flight to Egypt.

Lore

As secret Christians, Sergius and Bacchus refused to make offerings at the Temple of Jupiter, a treasonous offense. They were punished, humiliated, and tortured. Stripped of their military gear, they were paraded through the streets dressed in women's clothing and wearing heavy chains.

In prison, they sang, prayed, and claimed to be visited by angels. The two were brutally tortured, scourged so severely that Bacchus died in the process. He appeared after his death to Sergius as an angel to encourage his friend and senior officer to remain strong.

Sergius's feet were nailed to boards and he was forced to walk. Then he was beheaded. Following his death, numerous miracles were attributed to him and his tomb became a shrine.

Sergius and Bacchus called themselves 'brothers' and claimed that in their union they had become one with Christ as well as with each other. In the medieval era, their relationship was understood officially as the highest expression of 'brotherly love' or agape, though many interpret their bond differently.
