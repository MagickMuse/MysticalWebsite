Martin of Tours (c. 316 to 8 November 397) is venerated as a saint by most branches of Christianity. Born in Sabaria, Pannonia (now Szombathely, Hungary) to a Roman military family, he was named for Mars, the soldiers' patron deity. He began going to church at age ten against his Pagan parents' wishes and joined the Roman army at fifteen.

His most famous legend describes encountering a beggar while still a soldier. Having nothing else to give, he sliced his heavy officer's cloak in half and shared it. Later, he experienced a vision of Christ wearing his cloak. Finding Christianity incompatible with military service, Martin left to become a cleric, founding the first European monastery and becoming Bishop of Tours.

Hailed as a wonder worker, he healed lepers, raised the dead, and performed exorcisms. Martin actively converted Pagans in fourth-century Gaul, destroying shrines and sacred trees. He condemned executing heretics, arguing excommunication was sufficient. He is considered the first Christian saint who was not a martyr, sometimes called the "thirteenth apostle."

Saint Martin of Tours is renowned for healing alcoholism, offering strength to those battling addiction.

Domains

Martin is invoked to protect horses and geese, triumph over overwhelming odds, obtain needed clothing, heal alcoholism, strengthen resolve, cure the common cold, and relieve poverty.

Iconography

The classic image depicts him as a Roman soldier on horseback dividing his cloak for a naked beggar. His emblem is a globe of fire.

Attributes

Cloak, horse, globe of fire.

Sacred Sites

Basilica of Saint Martin on Rome's Esquiline Hill. Basilica of Saint Martin in Tours, France. Saint Martin Ludgate in the City of London.

Ritual

His festival is celebrated with bonfires. Throw a party in his honor and serve goose and wine, reserving some for Martin.

Lore

Martin's November feast may derive from ancient Samhain rites. It also coincides with an old Roman feast of Bacchus. He is the saint of geese and wine; legend says he could turn water into wine like Dionysus and Jesus.

It was traditional to eat goose on Saint Martin's Day across England, France, and Germany. The Martinmas goose's wishbone was used to predict winter weather. In France, Beaujolais Nouveau is released shortly after his feast. Special biscuits called Biscotti di San Martino, hard anise-flavored cookies dipped in new wine, are served on his feast day. Saint Martin was witnessed descending from the heavens to fight beside Austrian forces against the Ottomans in the 16th century.
