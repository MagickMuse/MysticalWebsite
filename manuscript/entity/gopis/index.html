The Gopis are the cowherd women of the village of Vraja, famous in Hindu mythology for their passionate, all-consuming devotion to the young Krishna. They serve as the paradigm of selfless, ecstatic bhakti (devotion) and have had a formative influence on the entire tradition of Krishna worship in India.

Overview

The Gopis are married women of Vraja whose interest in Krishna transforms from fond maternal attachment during his childhood to passionate longing as he grows older. None is able to resist his beauty and charm. When he retires to the woods and plays his flute on full-moon autumn nights, the women are driven mad with passion — they abandon their families, leave food burning on the stove, and run out of their homes to be with Krishna in the bowers of Vrindavana. They are so frenzied in their rush to him that their clothes and jewelry come loose and fall off (Bhagavata-purana 10.29.3–7).

In the woods the gopis sport and play with Krishna, making love in the forest and the waters of the Jumna River. In some accounts Krishna multiplies himself so each woman has a Krishna to herself. The mood is joyous, festive, and erotic. The Bhagavata-purana makes no attempt to deny the impropriety of the gopis' behavior; Krishna even teases some of them for acting illicitly.

As Models of Devotion

The Bhagavata-purana is explicit that the gopis serve as a model for true devotion to Krishna. True devotion, the text states, is highly emotional and causes horripilation, tears, loss of control, and frenzy (11.14.23–24). Those who love the Lord truly behave like the gopis — they let nothing come between themselves and the Lord, abandon everything when they hear his call, and incur the censure of society without hesitation. The gopis' willingness to transgress social and domestic duty in pursuit of the divine is understood not as moral failure but as the supreme expression of devotion.

Connection to Radha

In the earlier Gopi tradition (Harivamsha, Bhagavata-purana), the cowherd women are treated as a group rather than as named individuals. Radha is not mentioned. The tradition shifts with Jayadeva's Gitagovinda (twelfth century), in which Radha emerges as the named, specific heroine who inherits the gopi role and becomes the foremost model of divine love. The Gopi tradition is thus the collective ancestor and devotional prototype out of which Radha's singular mythology developed.

Source

Kinsley, David. Hindu Goddesses: Visions of the Divine Feminine in the Hindu Religious Tradition. University of California Press, 1986.
