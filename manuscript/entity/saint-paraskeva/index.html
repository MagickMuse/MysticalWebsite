Saint Paraskeva is renowned for healing, as women (but not men) may perform acts of healing on her twelve sacred Fridays.

The Masked Goddess

St Paraskeva is venerated in Russia as well as in Greece and the Balkans. She is depicted as a tall, thin woman with long, flowing hair carrying a spindle. Though officially a Christian saint, it is generally believed that she was a Pagan deity of such importance and popularity that the Church was obligated to incorporate her as a saint. Beneath the mask of the saint lies a Slavic deity and protector of women and their sacred arts.

Sacred Names and Epithets

St Paraskeva literally means "St Friday," and Friday is her holy day. Among her epithets are "The Flaxen One," "The Muddy One," and "The Dirty One." Paraskeva is described as black as fertile Earth, connecting her to both the earth's fertility and the dark, rich soil from which flax grows.

Goddess of Flax and Spinning

Paraskeva is associated with growing and spinning flax, but she transcends the role of mere "botanical spirit." Her connection to flax—one of the sacred fiber plants that can be spun—links her to the ancient tradition of spinning goddesses and their association with the sacred plants that were later demonized as "witch plants."

The Twelve Sacred Fridays

Her ritual consists of 12 Fridays spread throughout the year when her devotees cannot work. Those who keep her 12 fasts from work are said to be guaranteed abundance, happiness, and prosperity. On those days:

  • Men are forbidden to work the soil (day of respite for Earth)
  • Hunting, fishing, and gathering berries are forbidden
  • Cooking and washing are forbidden
  • One may only do what revitalizes: dance, sing, and have sex
  • Women (but not men) may perform acts of healing
  • These Fridays are considered very auspicious days for marriage and childbirth

The Forbidden Festivals

The sixteenth-century Stoglav Council complained that Paraskeva's festivals featured men and women, old and young, all naked with loose, unbound hair, engaged in jumping and shaking. Devotees claimed Paraskeva ordered them to honor her feast days with dancing. However, in 1589, the Patriarch of Constantinople banned her cult. This persecution demonstrates how the sacred practices of spinning goddesses and women's mysteries were suppressed.

Triplicity of Spinning Goddesses

St Paraskeva sometimes appears with two other saints: St Sreda (Wednesday) and St Nedelia (Saturday). Together they fulfill the concept of a triplicity of spinning goddesses, echoing the pattern of the Fates and Norns.

Magical Properties

Paraskeva embodies the sacred connection between flax cultivation, spinning, earth's fertility, and women's spiritual power. She represents the preservation of Pagan goddess worship through the mask of Christian sainthood.

Ritual Uses

Honor Paraskeva on Fridays, especially her 12 sacred Fridays. Work with her for spinning and flax magic, earth fertility work, women's mysteries, and connection to pre-Christian goddess traditions. Observe her sacred rest days as times of revitalization through dance, song, and pleasure.

Offerings

Flax and flax products, spinning tools, rest from work on her sacred Fridays, dancing and celebration, offerings honoring women's sacred arts.

Favored People

Women, spinners and those working with flax, people seeking abundance and prosperity, couples seeking marriage, those maintaining pre-Christian goddess traditions.

Petition For

Spinning skills and flax cultivation, abundance and prosperity, marriage and fertility, women's mysteries and sacred arts, connection to earth's fertility, understanding of pre-Christian goddess worship.

Warnings

Her worship was banned by the Church. Remember that beneath the saint's mask lies a powerful Pagan deity. Her naked festivals and connection to sexuality and earth were specifically targeted for persecution.

Manifestation

Paraskeva takes various forms. She manifests as a young woman with long, loose, unbound hair who roams the countryside ensuring women observe her Friday fasts. She may appear as an elderly woman spinning. Paraskeva is described as being as black as fertile earth, possibly a reference to Mokosh, Moist Mother Earth. Her distinctive identifying physical characteristic is long, loose hair, unusual in a culture where mature women have traditionally braided or otherwise bound their hair.

Spiritual Allies

Saint Paraskeva has twelve handmaidens, her apostles. She is sometimes associated with two other saints, Sreda (Saint Wednesday) and Nedelia (Saint Saturday). The three form a triad of spinning goddesses.

Sacred Sites

Wells and crossroads are sacred to Paraskeva. A chapel dedicated to her once stood in Moscow's Red Square, where only women were allowed entry. In Greece, her chapels are built over springs so that water gushes into the church or into a subterranean crypt.
