Magickal Properties

  • Egyptian spirit of domestic happiness, lord of love, music, dancing, among most beloved popular deities
  • Spirit of pleasure and woman's best friend, protects family especially children and pregnant women
  • Guards against evil spirits, trouble, venereal disease, and shields against Evil Eye
  • His distinctive dance with knives is intended to provoke laughter which helps chase away malicious spirits

Ritual Uses

  • Petition for fertility, safe childbirth, and protection of women and children during crucial moments
  • Use music, dancing, and drumming to raise protective laughter that scatters malicious spirits
  • Place his image on bedposts, walls, and household items for ongoing protection

Offerings

His name may relate to "perfume", offer precious cream perfumes or offerings that benefit cats as sacred animals

Favored People

Families, pregnant women, women, children, entertainers, those tattooed with his image

Petition For

Domestic happiness, fertility, safe childbirth, protection from evil spirits and Evil Eye, healing, joy, prosperity

Warnings

Keep household shrines clean and respectful. His image was ubiquitous in Egypt, carved on bedposts, painted on walls, tattooed on left thigh of female entertainers.

Additional Notes

  • Manifestation: Small man with lion's mane, wears lion/leopard skin, crown of tall plumes, dances with drum/tambourine
  • Attributes: Knife for defense, sa (protection instrument), musical instruments (harp, lyre, drums, tambourine)
  • Consort: Taweret (wonderful marriage, happiest if venerated together)
  • Only Egyptian deity consistently depicted full face
  • Place: Rumored to dwell in southern gate of Karnak Temple

Role in Egyptian Magick

Protector of Childbirth

Bes was an important aid to women as they labored. His leonine face and exposed genitals frightened away evil spirits. Some images show him wielding a short sword or knife for the same reason.

Bes was prone to bursts of unrestrained dancing and musical performances during labor. Alone or with the hippo-goddess Taweret, he can be depicted strumming a stringed instrument, shaking a tambourine, or banging a drum, whether to frighten off spirits, celebrate the occasion, or (more pragmatically) cover the cries of the woman in labor.

The Dwarf's Association with Children

Although it may sound insulting to modern sensibilities, in ancient Egypt a dwarf's "childlike" physical proportions suggested eternal youthfulness, they grew without growing up. This association invited Bes's connection with children and their protection.

Childbirth Spells Featuring Bes

One spell to ease childbirth pain instructs the magician to prepare a clay dwarf figure (representing Bes) and recite: "His wife called out for an image of a dwarf, made of clay. Let someone hurry to Hathor, the lady of Dendera, to fetch her healing amulet, so that Hathor will make this woman give birth!"

Appearance on Magickal Objects

Bes appears prominently on birth tusks (hippopotamus ivory wands) alongside other protective spirits. His image decorates the Metternich Stela and other healing monuments, usually directly above the vulnerable young Horus.

At Deir el-Medina, elevated platforms in homes were decorated with images of Bes and young female musicians, likely spaces connected to fertility, lovemaking, and childbirth.

Historical Context

Bes appears in Egyptian art from circa 1500 BCE and probably earlier, remaining a beloved household presence until the end of Egyptian history around 400 CE. He never received grand sanctuaries of his own; instead he lived where people lived, guarding doorways, bedchambers, and above all birthplaces, including the birth houses of royalty.

Art References

Bes is attested on the walls of temples at Thebes, on the curved ivory birth-tusks and protective batons of the Middle Kingdom, and on the painted walls of birth houses (mammisi) throughout Egypt. He is one of the very few Egyptian deities consistently shown in full frontal view rather than in profile, staring directly at the viewer to fix his protective gaze on all who approach.

Iconographic Details

He is dwarfish and deliberately hideous, his ugliness itself a weapon against evil. His face is large and thickly bearded, barely human in cast, with a mouth held open and the tongue protruding to startle harmful spirits. His body is thick, his arms short, and his legs short and bandy. He wears a tall plumed crown and often brandishes a short sword. He possesses a lion's mane, ears, and tail, and as a god of birth he frequently carries the SA symbol, a knot of protection.

Greco-Roman Reception

Bes was enthusiastically adopted by Greco-Roman culture as his protective virtues traveled far beyond Egypt. Greek craftsmen rendered him in strongly ithyphallic guise, depicting him with a disproportionately large and erect phallus as an emphatic sign of his fertility and apotropaic power, and from the time of the Roman occupation he appears in the mode of a soldier wearing a short military tunic, guarding the household in the armor of the imperial legions.

Guardian of the People

Much of the Egyptian mythology that survives comes from cults run by the Pharaoh and the elite priesthood, but among ordinary people the most beloved deities may well have been Bes and his wife Taweret. Bes was worshipped from Nubia to Mesopotamia. The pair live to serve and are especially protective of women and children. Either can be called alone, but they are strongest together, and besides protecting they inspire bravery and help you reach your own courage. They read the placement of their images as a call for protection.
