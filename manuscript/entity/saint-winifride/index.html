Saint Winifride is renowned for healing skin diseases, leprosy, neurological ailments, and lameness. Her sacred well is also invoked for fertility.

Classification

Roman Catholic saint.

Overview

Winifride (c. 600-c. 655), a beautiful young noblewoman of Wales and niece of Saint Benno, had taken a private vow of chastity and pledged to become a nun. This did not deter Caradog, a local chieftain who pursued her aggressively. When Winifride fled toward her uncle's church seeking sanctuary, Caradog caught her and cut off her head. She may also have been raped.

Winifride's head rolled down a hill. Where it stopped, a healing well sprang up. Saint Benno witnessed this miracle. Retrieving her head, he reattached it to her body. Winifride revived, good as new except for a permanent thin red line around her throat. She joined a convent and lived many more years, eventually dying of natural causes.

Pre-Christian Origins

Although the spring is attributed to her assault and the healing powers to her sanctity, the spring and its pilgrimage may be significantly older than the era in which Winifride lived. Her legend may cloak a pre-Christian spirit or derive from Celtic traditions regarding sacred heads. The myth was first documented in the twelfth century.

Powers and Domains

Saint Winifride's Well is described as the Lourdes of Wales, though far older than the French shrine. Its waters are reputed to cure leprosy, skin diseases, and neurological ailments. Many claim to have entered the waters lame and left their disabilities behind.

When King James II's wife, Mary of Modena, failed to conceive, he brought her to Holywell. Shortly after, she conceived their son, James Stuart (the future Old Pretender).

Saint Winifride is invoked by those receiving unwanted advances.

Manifestation

She may carry her head under her arm, or it may be attached. If attached, she will have a thin red line around her throat.

Iconography

Depicted as a young woman in traditional Celtic dress, or as an abbess with a sword in one hand and her head in the other. Often posed beside her well or a fountain.

Attributes

Sword, chopping block, axe, her head.

Ritual

Traditional petition: take three dips in the well, then make your request of Saint Winifride.

Sacred Sites

  • Saint Winifride's Holy Well, Holywell, Wales: Counted among the Seven Wonders of Wales and the oldest continuously operating pilgrimage site in Britain. The town's name pays homage to her well.
  • Shrewsbury Abbey: In 1138, her relics were brought here and enshrined.
  • Winifred's Well, Woolston, Shropshire: When the party carrying her relics to Shrewsbury stopped overnight here, a new healing well allegedly sprang up where her body was laid down. This well heals bruises, wounds, and broken bones.
