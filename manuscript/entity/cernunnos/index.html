Etymology and Name Origins

Cernunnos is the Latin name given this mysterious Celtic deity whose original name is now unknown. The name is usually translated as "the horned one" and derives from an Indo-European root word ker meaning "growth" or "to become large and hard." It may be a name or it may be a title, the word appears on an altar found at Notre Dame that also depicts his image.

Archaeological Evidence

Images of this god appear throughout Celtic Europe. His worship seems to have been widespread throughout Celtic territory, from Ireland to Romania. Cernunnos appears on the Early Iron Age Gundestrup Cauldron, which was found in a peat bog in Denmark in 1891, as well as on over thirty surviving ancient depictions.

On the Gundestrup Cauldron, he sits cross-legged surrounded by forest animals, holding a ram-horned serpent in one hand and a torc in the other. He is attended by a boar, an animal the Celts revered for its speed, pugnacity, and magickal connotations, and is also associated with a bull on the same vessel. In a relief found at Reims, France, Cernunnos sits cross-legged with a stag and bull at his feet. He has a large sack from which he distributes what appear to be either coins or grain, emphasizing his role as dispenser and distributor of wealth.

In Sex Magick

In the traditional witchcraft of this source, Cernunnos is the presiding power over sex magick, lust, and bewitchment, functioning as the consort of Habondia and her counterpart in the more aggressive, carnal dimension of the Craft. Where Habondia governs the gentle, romantic, and twilit side of love magick, Cernunnos rules the darkness of the Wildwood, the trampling and snorting of the god of beasts, and the raw forces of lust. He is invoked under his witch-name Cernunnos, also called Old Horny, and his symbol in this work is the goat, age-old representative of lust and debauchery.

His symbolism is related to that of the Greek Pan with satyrs and silenoi, and to the prehistoric phallic giants carved into the turf in England, including the Cerne Giant and the Long Man of Wilmington. As god of the dead, winter, and chaos, he holds the title Lord of Misrule and King of Debauchery and Licentious Frolic. The Saturnalia, the mid-winter festival of unbridled merriment, is dedicated to him.

In ritual, his altar triangle is decked with animal horns, evergreens, pinecones, and teeth or hooves of animals sacred to him. The instruments of power are the athame and chalice. Sex magick workings in his name are best performed on Thursdays at 8AM, 3PM, or 10PM.

A stone relief from Cirencester in England shows him with snakes bearing rams' horns, and his legs may be replaced entirely by snakes in some depictions. At Sommerecourt in Haute-Marne, a relief was found depicting him in the company of an unnamed goddess holding a basket and feeding a snake. The snake symbolism across these depictions is generally understood to represent rejuvenation and the cyclical renewal of life.

The Warfare and Destructive Aspect

Cernunnos holds a darker face alongside his roles as lord of animals and wealth: he is also a god of war, strife, and destructive power. In traditional witchcraft, he presides over offensive and vengeance workings, taking on associations with fire, wrath, and martial force. When approached in this aspect, he is sometimes called by the title Lucifer, meaning "light-bearer" or "torch-bearer," imagery that connects to his ancient iconography of a flaming torch or candle held between his antlers.

His alternate names and titles across folk traditions include Mamilion, Robin, Dumus, Hu, Janus, Dianus, Barabbon, and Barabbas. These names reflect his absorption into diverse European folk cultures as the ambiguous trickster-lord who presides over crossroads, reversals, and the dark half of the year.

In the destructive aspect he is associated with Thunor (the Germanic thunder god), Vulcan (the Roman forge god), Wayland Smith (the divine blacksmith of English legend), and Dionysus. The fire element is strongly emphasized in this face of the god: he scorches, blasts, and breaks rather than nurturing and sustaining. A flaming torch or candle placed between antlers or antlered imagery serves as his altar symbol in offensive workings.

His title Lord of Misrule reflects his dominion over chaos, inversion, and the wild forces that overturn the established order. He holds the power to upend circumstances and strip away protective structures from those who stand against the witch's will.
