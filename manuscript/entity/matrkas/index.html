The Matrkas (also called the Sapta Matrikas or Seven Mothers) are a group of ferocious battle goddesses in Hindu mythology, most commonly numbering seven. They are created from Durga herself during her cosmic battles against demons and embody her fury in its most primal, unrestrained form.

Overview

The Matrkas arise in the context of Durga's great battles against demons. They are said to emerge from Durga, sometimes from her body and sometimes as emanations of her rage, when the conflict demands helpers of extraordinary ferocity. They are wild, bloodthirsty, and intensely fierce. Unlike Durga herself, who combines martial power with cosmic authority, the Matrkas represent the raw, uncontrolled aspect of divine fury.

The seven most commonly cited Matrkas are: Brahmani (from Brahma), Vaishnavi (from Vishnu), Maheshvari (from Shiva), Indrani (from Indra), Kaumari (from Kartikeya), Varahi (from Varaha), and Chamunda (a form of Kali). Each derives her power and iconography from a male deity but acts independently on the battlefield.

Role in Battle

In the Devi Mahatmya, the Matrkas appear during Durga's battle with the demons Sumbha, Nisumbha, and their general Raktabija. Raktabija had been granted the boon that each drop of his blood that fell to earth would spawn a new demon identical to himself. As the goddesses wounded him, the battlefield multiplied with his clones. It was the Matrkas, particularly in their form as Kali (who spread her tongue over the battlefield to drink every drop of his blood before it could fall), who enabled Durga to finally defeat him.

Nature and Significance

The Matrkas occupy a liminal position in the Hindu goddess tradition. They are simultaneously protective (they fight on behalf of cosmic order) and dangerous (they are wild, intoxicated with battle, and difficult to control). Several texts describe them as needing to be pacified after the battle is over, lest they continue to rampage unchecked. They are closely associated with peripheral spaces, cremation grounds, and crossroads, liminal zones outside civilized order.

They are venerated together as a collective force of protective fury, particularly in Tantric traditions. Their iconography typically shows them holding weapons and drinking blood, intoxicated by battle.

Early History (Mahabharata, c. 1st Century AD)

The earliest clear descriptions of the Matrkas appear in the Mahabharata. Their number is unspecified in these early accounts; the implication in many passages is that they are innumerable. From the beginning they are characterized as inauspicious and dangerous.

In the Vana-parva, a group called the mothers of the world is sent by Indra to kill the newborn Karttikeya. When they approach the child, their breasts ooze milk and they ask him to adopt them as his mothers instead. Two of the described goddesses exemplify their nature: one is born of anger and carries a spike; the other is bad-tempered, red in complexion, a daughter of the sea, and lives on blood.

In a subsequent episode, a host of ferocious goddesses (including Kaki, Halima, Malim, Brhali, Arya, Palala, and Vaimitra) are born from Karttikeya when Indra strikes him with a thunderbolt. Karttikeya adopts them as his mothers but divides them into good and evil spirits; as a group they are characterized by stealing children.

Later in the Karttikeya narrative, the wives of six sages, unjustly accused of adultery and divorced, come to Karttikeya and petition him to adopt them as his mothers. As the Mahamatrkas (great mothers), they make two requests: to be recognized as great goddesses, and to live off the children of men, since they themselves have been denied the ability to have children. Karttikeya is reluctant, asks them to protect rather than harm children, and they agree, but in his closing mandate he commands them to torment children until the age of sixteen, after which they act as positive influences.

Another list in the same section names ten spirits: Vinata, Putana, Raksasi, Aditi (or Revati), Diti, Surabhi, Sarama, Kadru, Lohitayani, and Arya. All but two devour or afflict children or pregnant women. They drink strong liquor, eat flesh, and lurk in the confinement chamber during the first ten days of a child's life.

The Salya-parva chapter on Karttikeya's elevation to commander of the divine army lists ninety-two named Matrkas among his hosts. As a group they are described as having long nails, large teeth, and protruding lips; they inspire terror; they live in trees, at crossroads, in caves, on mountains, at springs, and in burning grounds; they speak many languages; some are dark.

Related Child-Afflicting Goddesses

The Matrkas are part of a broader category of goddesses inimical to children across Indian tradition.

Hariti is a Buddhist goddess known in the Magadha area who steals children and feeds them to her many sons. The Buddha transformed her into a benign protective being.

Jara, described in the Mahabharata's Sabha-parva, feeds on flesh and blood and lurks at crossroads. She inadvertently combines two half-children abandoned at a crossroads into one whole child, giving it to the local king; in gratitude he establishes her worship throughout the region.

Putana, named in one Mahabharata list of Matrkas, is described as a stalker of the night. She disguises herself as a beautiful woman and attempts to kill the infant Krishna by poisoning her breasts and asking to suckle him. Krishna drains the life from her instead, revealing her true form as an ugly hag.

Jyestha (also called Alaksmi, the inauspicious one) is a goddess born when the gods and demons churn the ocean of nectar. Physically grotesque, she cannot bear the presence of pious activity. She resides in homes where family members quarrel and where children's hunger is disregarded.

Behind all these child-afflicting goddesses is likely the belief that women who die childless or in childbirth linger as inimical spirits jealous of other women's children. Worship of the Matrkas is primarily aimed at keeping them away. Practices such as not calling one's children beautiful and marking them with collyrium to obscure their attractiveness are probably related to avoiding the Matrkas' notice.

Non-Brahmanical Origins

The Matrkas' association with peripheral, non-Aryan traditions is clear in the Mahabharata. They speak a variety of languages, are dark, and live in marginal geographic zones: mountains, caves, crossroads, cremation grounds. Their association with Karttikeya and later with Shiva reinforces this: both deities carry strong non-Brahmanical attributes. The Matrkas are almost certainly identifiable with the many village goddesses throughout India who are widely worshipped by common people and associated with disease, particularly diseases affecting children.

The Later Tradition (Post-400 AD)

In the Puranic period the Matrkas' number is standardized, usually to seven. Their names become linked to major male deities: Brahmani (from Brahma), Vaishnavi (from Vishnu), Maheshvari (from Shiva), Indrani (from Indra), Kaumari (from Kartikeya), Varahi (from Varaha), Chamunda (from Kali/Durga). They are Brahmanized and domesticated in appearance, though their underlying wild nature persists.

Literary references multiply in this period. The Natya-sastra recommends worship of the Matrkas before setting up a theater. Carudatta and Mrcchakatika both refer to offerings made to the Matrkas at crossroads. The Harsacarita mentions a matr-grha (house of the Mothers) where young nobles burn themselves with lamps to propitiate them. The Kadambari describes the queen Vilasavati visiting a Matrka shrine to petition for a son.

The Devi-Mahatmya Episode

The best-known Puranic account of the Matrkas appears in the third episode of the Devi-mahatmya, during Durga's battle against the demons Sumbha and Nisumbha. The male gods, watching from the sidelines, create saktis (female counterparts) of themselves to help the Devi on the battlefield; these seven saktis are collectively called the Matrkas. After the battle the Matrkas dance drunk on the blood of their victims.

When the demon Sumbha challenges Durga to single combat and objects to her many female allies, she absorbs all the Matrkas back into herself, demonstrating that they are simply extensions of her own being, not independent saktis of the male gods but forms of the Devi herself.

The Matsya-Purana Episode: Defeat of Andhaka

In the Matsya-purana the Matrkas are created by Shiva to combat the demon Andhaka, who duplicates himself from each drop of blood that falls from his wounds. The Matrkas are instructed to drink his blood before it can fall to earth and spawn new demons, which they gladly do. After defeating Andhaka, the Matrkas announce that they will now devour all gods, demons, and humans. Shiva commands them to stop, but they ignore him and begin a rampage of destruction. Only the appearance of Narasimha (Vishnu's man-lion avatara), who creates a host of benign goddesses to calm them down, ends the carnage. This episode vividly preserves the Matrkas' essentially uncontrollable nature: even Shiva cannot command them once their bloodlust is aroused.

Theology and Meaning

Throughout their history the Matrkas remain primarily a group; their individuality is not significant. Their restriction to seven and their association with male deities is artificial and to some extent arbitrary. At their core they are an independent group of goddesses with violent natures, associated with diseases affecting children, closely linked to village goddess cults throughout India. They represent the dangerous, uncontrolled face of female divine power: simultaneously protective (fighting for cosmic order) and threatening (bloodthirsty, intoxicated, difficult to stop). Born from lack of control (in one account they arise from the Devi's distracted mind), they embody uncontrolled power itself.
