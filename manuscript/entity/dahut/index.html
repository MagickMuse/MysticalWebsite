Magickal Properties

Breton witch-princess-mermaid-goddess, only one tale about Dahut survives and it derives from Christian sources. The story is hazy and has many gaps. Dahut was the daughter of Gradlon, King of Cornwall and a Druid, and Malgven, Queen of the North, a magickal character and perhaps another witch-goddess. Her father and mother spent a year at sea where Dahut was born, but her mother died.

The City of Ys

Dahut loves the sea and was inspired to build a miraculous crystal-walled city in Brittany named Ys, built below sea level so that it seems to emerge from the sea. To prevent flooding, a high dyke was built and locked with a unique brass key. Only one copy of this key existed and King Gradlon had it. The legend implies that Dahut is a sea spirit or a priestess of the sea.

The Femme Fatale Legend

The tale is not particularly complimentary: Dahut is portrayed as a femme fatale who seduces a different man each night. According to the story, Dahut insisted her lovers wear a black silk mask that transformed into deadly metal claws in the morning, killing him so that she could feed his body to the sea. Ys was fabulously wealthy. Dahut had a sea dragon who did her bidding and brought the treasures of the sea to her.

Ruler of Ys

Dahut ruled the city, maintaining Celtic traditions and Pagan deities. Eventually she had a confrontation with Corentin, Bishop of Quimper, and shortly afterwards disaster struck.

The Destruction of Ys

Dahut was always on the lookout for new lovers/sacrificial victims. One day a stranger dressed in red rode up to the palace; she fell madly in love with him and wished to keep him rather than kill him. Unbeknownst to her, according to the standard version of the story, it was Satan on a mission of punishment from God. Because of the sins of Ys, the magickal crystal city was about to be transformed into the equivalent of an underwater Sodom and Gomorrah.

In order to prove her love, the mysterious red stranger demanded the key to the dyke. Dahut stole it from her father and Satan opened the dyke, flooding the city. St Guenolé, a resident of the area, appeared ordering King Gradlon to repent. Gradlon tried to escape from Ys on horseback together with Dahut whom he wished to rescue. Guenolé however struck him, insisting that he abandon Dahut as the price of survival, which ultimately Gradlon did. Gradlon and Guenolé were the only two survivors.

Transformation and Survival

Following the deluge, Gradlon renounced Druidry and Guenolé converted him to Christianity. Gradlon became ruler of the city of Quimper where a statue of him gazing in the direction of Ys still stands at Corentin Cathedral. Dahut was allegedly transformed into a mermaid, in which guise she survives.

Historical Basis

Although the story was told to emphasize the powers of St Guenolé, it may be based on an actual fifth-century disaster. Several Roman roads now leading into the sea allegedly once led to Ys.

Ritual Uses

Invoke Dahut for sea magick, protection of ancient traditions, and connection to Celtic Pagan deities. She represents the suppressed feminine divine power of the sea.

Offerings

Treasures returned to the sea, offerings honoring Celtic traditions and ocean spirits.

Favored People

Sea spirits, those who honor Celtic traditions and Pagan deities, practitioners of ocean magick.

Petition For

Sea magick, wealth from the ocean, protection of sacred traditions, connection to mer-folk and ocean deities.

Warnings

Christian sources portray Dahut negatively as part of a morality tale. The true nature of this goddess has likely been obscured by centuries of demonization. Approach with respect for the suppressed Pagan traditions she represents.

Additional Notes

Dahut's tale is retold in Abraham Merritt's 1934 pulp novel Creep, Shadow, Creep. Her story connects to the Korrigans, Breton nature-spirit fairies.
