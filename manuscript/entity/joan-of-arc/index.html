The Peasant Girl Who Crowned a King

Joan of Arc emerged from rural Domrémy, France to lead French troops to victory during the Hundred Years' War. Born January 6, 1412 to an illiterate peasant family, she crowned Charles VII despite being captured and burned at the stake at age 19. Five hundred years later she was canonized, though the question of whether she was saint, witch, or devotee of the Fairy Faith continues to generate passionate debate.

The Voices

At age 13, Joan began hearing voices she described as from God. Over five years these voices spoke to her several times weekly, identifying themselves as Archangel Michael, Saint Catherine, and Saint Margaret. They commanded her to save France and ensure Charles VII's coronation.

Twelfth-century chronicler Geoffrey of Monmouth recorded Merlin's prophecy that "a marvelous maid will come from the Nemus Cenutum for the healing of nations." During Joan's time, this mysterious Nemus Cenutum was identified with the Bois Chenue forest near Domrémy where a Fairy Tree stood.

The Fairy Tree and Witchcraft Accusations

Joan first heard her voices at the Fairy Tree near Domrémy. The tree stood beside a healing well linked to the fairies. Local tradition held that fairies and witches danced around the tree together. As a child, Joan sang and danced around the tree with other village children and hung garlands from it, though she claimed this honored Our Lady of Domrémy rather than fairy traditions. She denied all dealings with Fairies.

Among the accusations made against her was that Joan attended Friday night witches' sabbats at a fountain near the oak. The English forces perceived her as a witch, while both sides recognized her possession of or access to supernatural powers. Shakespeare's Henry VI Part 1 expressed the contemporary English view: "Bring forth the sorceress condemned to burn."

Signs of Magickal Activity

Joan's campaign featured odd occurrences interpreted as magickal:

Clairvoyance: A man on horseback swore at Joan, who despised foul language. She retorted, "In God's name, why do you swear and you so near your death?" An hour later, the rider fell from his horse into a moat and drowned.

Recognition of the True King: At Chinon, Charles VII hid among approximately 300 bystanders while someone else wore royal garb. Joan, who had never seen Charles, went straight to the true dauphin, saying "The King of Heaven sends words by me that you will be anointed and crowned."

Healing Powers: Joan possessed the power to heal, considered by some as evidence of witchcraft. She allegedly performed miracles of healing both during her life and following her death.

The Title La Pucelle

Joan's title "La Pucelle" (The Maid) could be interpreted as having witchcraft significance. In some covens, Maid or Maiden is a title for a high-ranking individual. Margaret Murray interpreted this title to indicate Joan's position as Maiden of a coven, postulating that Joan was a "divine victim" who served as a substitute for a royal victim.

Trial and Execution

Captured at Compiègne on May 23, 1430, Joan was charged with heresy and witchcraft. The bishop was intent on proving her a witch so the English could challenge Charles VII's divine right to rule by claiming he gained the crown via witchcraft.

Her enemies charged that she spoke with evil spirits (demons) not saints or angels. They claimed Joan induced the voices by chewing on a mandrake root tucked into her bosom. During trial she denied possessing a mandrake root but admitted hearing of the practice.

Joan was imprisoned for one year and one week, often chained to a wooden block. At her formal ecclesiastical trial before 37 clerical judges, she faced 70 charges including being a witch, diviner, sorceress, false prophetess, conjurer, and invoker of evil spirits. She was accused of being "given to the arts of magick."

She represented herself and held her own with this powerful group of educated men. Most charges could not be substantiated and were dropped. The Inquisitors continued with 12 charges, including the ability to see visions, heresy in refusing to submit to Church authority, her insistence that she was responsible only to God, and the one for which she was finally convicted: wearing men's clothing.

Joan refused to say the Lord's Prayer, which years later would be considered equivalent to a confession of witchcraft.

Controversial Associations

Joan was reputedly the friend or even lover of King Rene d'Anjou of Provence, who narrowly escaped charges of heresy himself. She chose Gilles de Rais as her patron and protector. Nine years after Joan's death, he too was charged with witchcraft and executed.

Death and Canonization

On May 30, 1431, Joan was publicly burned at the stake. The authorities wished to destroy her mystique and prevent rumors of last-minute supernatural rescue. When her clothes burned off, the executioner was instructed to reduce the flames so the crowd could see "all the secrets which can or should be in a woman."

Her remains were thrown in the river so no sacred relics could be taken, though the crowd collected ashes. She wore a paper hat reading "Heretic, Relapsed Apostate, Idolatress," was burned alive without the mercy of a preceding strangulation, and remains the only person condemned by a church court for crimes against religion to later be declared a saint by that same church. According to tradition, a dove flew from her lips at the moment of her death, interpreted either as a sign of the Holy Ghost or as a visible wandering double or fetch.

Joan became an unofficial saint immediately. Peasants set up shrines and carried votive images. In June 1456, Joan was declared a martyr in France; her oppressors were described as heretics engaging in a political vendetta. After five hundred years of popular pressure, Joan was canonized on May 16, 1920 by Pope Benedict XV as part of an effort to restore diplomatic relations between France and the Holy See after World War I.

Protection Ritual

  1. Designate a small gray candle for each member of your household for whom you seek Joan's protection. (Silver or white if gray unavailable.)
  2. Using a sharp-pointed tool, scratch each person's name into their candle.
  3. Petition Joan, invoke her protection, and light the candles.
  4. While burning, petition some more.
  5. Repeat this ritual eight more times, for a total of nine consecutive spell days.

Matron of Radio

Joan is the matron of those who work in radio and guardian of radio itself. She may be invoked for protection against hostile EVP (electronic voice phenomena), mysterious voices transmitted over media believed by some to be portals for ghosts, demons, or other harmful beings.

The Witchcraft Debate

Gerald Gardner claimed that questions of heresy would have been very easy to prove without need of questions regarding Fairies and witchcraft, suggesting the witchcraft charges had substance beyond political convenience.

Three interpretations exist regarding Joan's garland-hanging at the Fairy Tree:

  • Joan genuinely honored the Virgin Mary, though hanging garlands from trees is not orthodox Christian practice
  • Joan refused to admit partaking of Fairy traditions
  • Through Identification, traditions once associated with a Fairy Queen or Goddess were now performed in honor of Our Lady of Domrémy

Iconography

A young girl in battle armor with characteristic bowl haircut. She may be depicted on horseback, kneeling in prayer, or surrounded by flames. The image of the Anima Sola is also used to represent her unofficially.

Attributes

Suit of armor, sword, banner.

Gray.

Sacred Animals

Horse, white dove. Bystanders attested that a dove hovered around her banner as she rode into battle; witnesses to her execution claimed to see a white dove emerge from her mouth with her dying breath.

Sacred Day

Tuesday.

Feast Days

  • May 30 (Vatican-decreed, anniversary of death)
  • May 8 (Joan of Arc Day, French national holiday celebrating withdrawal of English troops from Orleans)

Sacred Sites

  • La Maison de Jeanne d'Arc in Orleans (museum in house where she lived)
  • Cathedral of Saint Croix in Orleans (stained-glass windows depicting her life)

Spiritual Allies

Very devoted to Black Madonnas. Invoked the Black Madonna of Blois in April 1429 and attended Mass before the Black Madonna of Orleans daily, crediting her with her victory of 7 May.
