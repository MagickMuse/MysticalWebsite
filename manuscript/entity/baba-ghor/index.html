Overview

Baba Ghor is the most beloved of the Siddi saints and the single most popular saint of African descent for South Asian Muslims in India and Pakistan. His veneration transcends the Siddi community, drawing devotees from many spiritual traditions, especially the Parsi (South Asian Zoroastrians).

Historical Background

The historical Baba Ghor is believed to have lived in the 14th and 15th centuries, dying in either 1416 or 1431. A shrine dedicated to him may have been established by 1452, when the new king of Gujarat, Qutbuddin Ahmed Shah, visited "the saint of God, Baba Ghor."

The Legend

According to his most famous legend, Baba Ghor was a prominent military commander in Abyssinia (now Ethiopia) when he received a profound spiritual message ordering him to vanquish a "female demon" in India. He assembled a small army and traveled to India accompanied by his siblings, including Baba Habash and Mai Mishra.

Ultimately, it was Mai Mishra who defeated Makhan Devi, the spirit described in Siddi legends as a demon or sorceress. The honorific Devi indicates she was a Hindu or Jain goddess, the "Butter Goddess" who ensnared men via poisonous buttermilk potions. Baba Ghor was ordered "to break the magick of the goddess and to light the lamp of Islam."

The Bead Trade

Baba Ghor was a soldier and mendicant before arriving in India, but there he became a trader in beads. He is credited with founding Gujarat's bead industry and extending the agate trade to East Africa. By 1505, the name Babaghoria commonly identified local agate. He remains the patron of the bead industry, and agate miners invoke him for safety before entering the mines.

Sacred Sites

His primary pilgrimage site is his durga (tomb) in Ratanpur, Gujarat, where Mai Mishra and Baba Habash are also buried. A memorial shrine in Mumbai was built by a Parsi devotee in 1947. He also has a shrine in Karachi, Pakistan.

Attribute

Bedi, the iron ankle-rings of truth.
