Entity Grimoire

Spirit Categories

An overview of the major categories of spiritual entities encountered in magickal practice, their origins, how they are traditionally approached, and what distinguishes each type.

Angels

The word "angel" translates directly as "messenger" in Greek, itself derived from the Hebrew. Unlike most other spirit categories, angels do not traditionally accept offerings. They are called through prayer, petition, and fragrance. High Ritual Magick, drawing on traditions attributed to King Solomon, compels angelic presence through sigils and talismans.

Angels oversee all areas of life. There are angels of conception, angels of specific countries, angels assigned to thieves, and individual guardian angels. The Talmud states that each person has eleven thousand guardian angels.

In Jewish mysticism, angels are described as beings of light. When an angel wishes to take physical form, it gathers earth, fashions it into clothing, and transforms itself. They are generally understood as dynamic, fierce beings of overwhelming radiance.

Angels and the Origin of Magick

According to Islamic and Jewish traditions, angels are directly responsible for humanity's ability to practice magick. The Creator generated magickal power, but angels taught humans to recognize and use it.

The Book of Enoch names specific teaching angels and their areas of instruction:

  • Semjaza: enchantments and root cutting
  • Armaros: resolving enchantments
  • Baraquiel: astrology
  • Kokabel: constellations
  • Ezequeeh: knowledge of the clouds
  • Araquiel: earth signs
  • Shamsiel: sun signs
  • Sariel: phases of the moon

Moral Status Across Traditions

Christian theology positions angels as purely good beings, with fallen angels losing their status entirely. Demons occupy the opposite end as purely evil. Islamic, Jewish, and Pagan traditions take a different view: angels are beings of power, not inherently good or evil. Some are benevolent, some are harmful, and some can be influenced in either direction.

Genesis 6:1-4 describes angels who desired human women and fathered children with them. According to the Book of Enoch, these angels shared knowledge of astrology, magick, witchcraft, and cosmetics, which was considered a significant magickal art in the ancient world.

Angelic Hierarchy and the Archangels

Angels are organized into ranks including Archangels, Throne Angels, Watchers, Seraphim, and Cherubim. The archangels command hosts of spirits, and many spells request their assistance, though whether the archangel appears personally or delegates varies by interpretation.

The four widely recognized archangels are Rafael, Gabriel, Michael, and Auriel (also spelled Uriel or Ariel). This grouping appears across Christian, Islamic, and Jewish traditions and likely predates all three. Some traditions recognize seven archangels, corresponding to the seven visible planets. Candidates for the additional three include Cassiel (Kafziel), Zadkiel, Samael, Chamuel, Jophiel, Raziel, Metatron, and Iblis. Most angelic names end with "-el," the ancient pan-Semitic name for the Creator, meaning "the Lord."

Enochian Magick

Enochian magick is a system of angel magick transmitted to Dr. John Dee through the medium Edward Kelley. Dee was a prominent astrologer and magickian who selected Queen Elizabeth I's coronation date and served as her advisor. He sought direct angelic communication but could not achieve it alone. Kelley, an alchemist with strong shamanic abilities, contacted the angels through mirrors and crystal balls. The angels reportedly identified themselves as the same beings who communicated with the biblical Enoch. Working with Dee's angelic system requires substantial familiarity with classical magick.

Demons

The modern understanding of demons as evil spirits stems from a reinterpretation of the Greek "daemon," which originally referred to a personal guardian spirit that could be benevolent, malevolent, or both. As Christianity gained institutional power, any spirit interaction not sanctioned by the Church was classified as evil, transforming the daemon into the demonic.

Within the classical grimoire tradition, "demon" typically refers to a low-level malevolent spiritual entity. These beings are generally anonymous, jealous, mischievous, and lacking in intelligence.

Orisha and Lwa

Origins

The orisha and lwa are closely related spirit beings originating from West Africa. The orisha come from Yoruba-land in modern Nigeria. The lwa derive from Fon traditions in Dahomey (modern Benin). The word "Vodou" means "spirit" in the Fon language.

These spirits have been venerated in their homelands for thousands of years. When their devotees were enslaved and transported to the Western Hemisphere, the spirits traveled with them. Traditional African religions were outlawed under slavery and devotees faced persecution, but the spirits endured. By the late twentieth century, they had re-emerged as the foundation of influential African-derived spiritual traditions.

Traditions and Variants

The orisha preside over Santeria and the Afro-Brazilian traditions of Candomble, Umbanda, and Quimbanda (where the Portuguese spelling "orixa" is used). Many of the same spirits appear across these traditions. Names and spellings vary by location, and personalities may manifest somewhat differently, but the core nature of each spirit remains consistent. Oya, for example, Spirit of storm winds and the Niger River, keeps that name in Cuba but is known as Iansa in Brazil.

The lwa preside over Haitian Vodou, New Orleans Voodoo, and Dominican Vodo. ("Lwa" is the modern Kreyol spelling; older French texts use "loa.") While the lwa represent a largely distinct pantheon, a few spirits are shared with the orisha traditions, and the behavior and manifestations of both groups follow similar patterns.

Spirits Common Across the Diaspora

Certain spirits were so widely distributed across Africa that they appear in virtually all African Diaspora traditions. Eshu-Elegbara, the trickster Master of the Crossroads, and Ogun, Spirit and Embodiment of Iron, are two of the most prominent. Their names shift depending on location, but their core essence remains consistent.

Thousands of orisha are said to exist, though only approximately forty maintain active interest in human affairs.

How Orisha and Lwa Operate

Vodou and Santeria are monotheistic faiths centered on a supreme creator deity. In Yoruba tradition, Olodumare created the orisha to supervise creation. Each orisha or lwa governs specific domains of life, and every area of life has its own presiding spirit. Each person also has presiding orisha or lwa, typically one male and one female, known as the "masters of your head," who serve as patrons, protectors, and advocates.

Each spirit has a distinct personality along with specific colors, numbers, foods, plants, and objects that carry its essence. Spells invoking a particular spirit draw upon these correspondences. Oshun, Spirit of Love and Beauty, is associated with the color yellow, the number five, water, cinnamon, and honey. Oya is associated with the color purple and the number nine. Spirits communicate through these same symbols and correspondences, and this system can be applied when working with spirits from other pantheons as well.

The Seven African Powers

The orisha most actively involved in daily human life are sometimes invoked collectively as the Seven African Powers:

  1. Elegba
  2. Ogun
  3. Obatala
  4. Oshun
  5. Oya or Orunmila (varies by tradition)
  6. Chango
  7. Yemaya

Petitioning the group as a whole ensures comprehensive coverage of blessings and protections. Commercial Seven African Powers products often depict them in their syncretic guise as Roman Catholic saints.

Ritual Possession

The orisha and lwa also work with independent practitioners outside formal religious structures and have been incorporated into some Wiccan, Pagan, and Goddess traditions. However, ritual possession, in which a spirit uses a person as a medium, occurs only within formal religious settings. This is a shamanic practice requiring proper initiation and supervision, not a magick spell.

The Exus and Pomba Giras

Eshu-Elegbara as Gate Guardian

Eshu-Elegbara is traditionally the first spirit petitioned in any ritual or spell. Because he controls all doors and access to roads, he determines whether a petition will be blocked or will reach its intended recipient. Similar to the Greek god Hermes, devotees contact Eshu first, make offerings, then request that he invite and escort any other desired spirits. Some traditions bypass this step, but many consider initial contact with a gate guardian to be proper spiritual protocol.

Eshu manifests different aspects of himself depending on location and audience. In his original West African form, he appears young and handsome. In the Western Hemisphere, he more commonly takes the guise of a frail old man with a cane or a playful child.

The Brazilian Transformation

In Brazil, Eshu evolved into a distinct class of spirit: the exus (singular: exu, pronounced "Eh-shoo"). Powerful, volatile, and dangerous, the exus emphasize the extreme trickster aspects of the original spirit. Because exus are closer in nature to humans than the other orixa, they are the spirits most frequently petitioned for practical matters such as money and love.

Every orixa has a personal exu who serves as that spirit's messenger. All Afro-Brazilian spiritual paths include this concept by definition.

The Pomba Giras

The female counterparts of the exus are known as Pomba Giras (singular: exua, pronounced "Eh-shoo-ah," though Pomba Gira, meaning "whirling dove," is the more commonly used term). The Pomba Giras represent a fusion of West African spirituality with Portuguese Romany traditions, as Romani people were deported to the Brazilian colonies during the same era as the African slave trade.

The most prominent Pomba Gira is Maria Padilha, a deified historical figure: Maria de Padilla, wife of Pedro the First of Castile and Leon. She also appears in European magick traditions.

Working with Exus and Pomba Giras

Beyond their role as orixa messengers, exus and Pomba Giras can be petitioned independently, particularly for workings that the orixa themselves may decline to perform.

Perception of these spirits varies by tradition. From African or Pagan perspectives, exus and Pomba Giras are no more volatile than many other spirits, and Maria Padilha in particular is recognized as a being of great power and generosity. Within traditions influenced by Roman Catholicism, exus are sometimes associated with the Christian devil, with emphasis on qualities like crossroads encounters and the scent of brimstone.

The Pomba Giras prefer T-crossroads as gathering places. They favor working with women but will also work with men upon request.

Traditional Offerings

Exus traditionally receive plates of yellow manioc flour cooked or drizzled with oil. They drink wine, rum, and cachaca. Pomba Giras prefer flowers over food and favor anisette or champagne.

Djinn

The djinn are spirits of Arabia and North Africa whose origins predate Judaism, Christianity, and Islam. They have since traveled the world alongside Islam and can be found wherever that religion has been established. The word "djinn" (also spelled "jinn") derives from old Arabic and is thought to mean "covert" or "darkness." These are the spirits known in Western popular culture as genies.

Origin Stories

In one tradition, the djinn were created before humans to serve as intermediaries between angels and people. In another account, Adam and Eve had thirty children rather than just those named in scripture. When God asked to see them all, Eve brought out only fifteen and hid the rest. God declared that the hidden children would remain in shadow forever, while those presented would live in the light. The visible children became the ancestors of humanity; the hidden ones became the djinn and other spirits.

Nature and Temperament

Some djinn are benevolent and willing to help humans. Others are temperamental, treacherous, jealous, and consistently malevolent. Many djinn may be pre-Islamic divinities whose status was diminished over time. The prominent djinn Aisha Qandisha, for example, has been speculatively linked to the ancient goddess Astarte.

Unlike the gregarious orisha, djinn tend to be cautious, secretive, and quick to withdraw. They are most active at night and generally avoid daytime appearances.

Aversions and Preferences

Djinn hate salt and fear iron and steel, all of which can be used to expel them. They are attracted and pacified by storytelling. They prefer heat, deserts, natural springs, ruins, and wild places, and they despise cold and snow. Their traditional offering involves pouring oil over flour.

Spiritual Orientation

Djinn are believed to hold their own spiritual affiliations. There are reportedly Islamic, Jewish, Christian, and Pagan djinn, and each prefers to assist humans who share their orientation. Islamic djinn in particular are said to resent petitions from anyone other than devout Muslims.

The Gnawa Tradition

The human custodians most versed in djinn lore, including summoning, appeasing, dismissing, and ritually channeling them, are traditionally the Gnawa people of North Africa. The Gnawa (sometimes spelled Gnaua, believed to derive from "Ghana") are themselves descendants of enslaved sub-Saharan Africans. Their relationship to the djinn parallels in some ways the relationship between orisha devotees and their spirits.

Fairies

Fairy is a broad, somewhat generic category encompassing a vast range of spirits that may or may not be related. The category includes devas, flower fairies (miniature winged plant spirits), and human-sized Fairy Folk. Unlike "orisha" or "djinn," which refer to specific types of spirits, "fairy" functions as an umbrella term.

Types and Cultural Variants

Spirits classified under this category span many traditions: dakini from Tibet, apsaras from India, peri from pre-Islamic Iran, Eastern European vila, and Romany keshalyi, urme, and ursitory. Some of the more beautiful female djinn are also sometimes categorized as fairies.

What connects these diverse spirits are shared associations with wild nature, forests, flowers, love, sexuality, women's power, and the birth process.

Fairies as Ritual Judges

Fairies often function as enforcers of proper ritual behavior. They reward those who conduct rituals correctly and punish those who do not. The vila, for instance, are known to punish deceitful men and those who abandon commitments. In the tale of Sleeping Beauty, the offended fairy's wrath stems from an improperly set offering table with an incorrect number of place settings.

This reflects a broader dynamic in spirit relationships: once a mutual relationship is established, the spirit expects continued offerings and interaction. With the rise of European Christianity, many of these customary payments and tributes ceased, leaving formerly honored spirits without sustenance.

Demotion of Deities to Fairy Status

Many once-powerful spirits were demoted to fairy status as Christianity expanded, particularly in Ireland. Aine, a powerful solar spirit, came to be classified as a Queen of Fairy, as did Maeve. These spirits retained their power despite the diminished classification.

In parts of Europe, particularly France, witches were accused of consorting with fairies rather than with the devil. During the French witch craze (roughly from the era of Joan of Arc through the end of the seventeenth century), a conviction for fairy association carried a sentence of hard labor. Attempts were even made to depict Joan of Arc as a devotee of the Fairy Folk during her heresy trial.

The Romany Connection

Anthropologists and historians have noted parallels between descriptions of fairies and early European encounters with the Romany: nomadic people dwelling in forests, associated with magick and divination, described as dark and small among predominantly tall, fair populations. Both groups share accusations of child theft in folklore. The Romany themselves maintain their own complex fairy traditions in which fairies are clearly spiritual beings.

Elementals and Related Spirits

Other spirit types sometimes grouped alongside fairies include elementals (salamanders, undines, and similar beings) and mermaids, all of which may be summoned to assist in spell-casting.

Offerings and Attraction

Full-sized fairies are said to prefer milk and berries. Thyme is strongly associated with fairy attraction. Flower fairies, the butterfly-sized winged nature spirits, can be petitioned to bestow and enhance botanical powers. They are drawn to gardens containing birdbaths, water sources, charms, statues, witch balls, and crystals. Plants they favor include berries, chamomile, Corsican mint, forget-me-nots, foxglove, hibiscus, hollyhocks, honeysuckle, lavender, lilac, milkweed, morning glories, pansies, peonies, primroses, vincas, and violets. Fairies generally prefer wildflowers over cultivated blooms.

A circle of ash saplings serves as a fairy dance ground. Circles made from clear quartz crystals work as an alternative. Stinging nettles are said in Romany tradition to guard the doorways to fairyland. Burning equal parts dried hollyhock and rosemary as incense outdoors attracts flower fairies.

Saints

Deification and Sainthood

The line between spirit and human is not always clear. Many spirits across traditions, from the orisha to figures of ancient Greece and Hawaii, may be deified humans. Chango, for instance, was once a king in Yorubaland who was deified after death and took his place among the orisha. The ancient Greeks demonstrated this process when Heracles and Psyche, both originally mortal, were permitted to enter the divine realm.

Saints, however, are definitively human in origin. Although the term is most strongly associated with Roman Catholicism, concepts of sainthood exist across many cultures, including African Diaspora, Buddhist, Jewish, and Muslim traditions.

Criteria for Sainthood

Roman Catholic sainthood requires a lengthy, bureaucratic process of proving miraculous deeds through official beatification and canonization. Other traditions use different criteria. In many cultures, a person whose magickal power (known as heka in Egyptian tradition, baraka in Islamic and North African traditions) is so potent that it persists after death, remaining accessible for healing and magick, is recognized as a saint. This is typically an informal process: word of miracles spreads, shrines appear, and practitioners gather.

The behavior of these saints is not always exemplary. Some are described as devout and charitable, while others display what might be called excessive tendencies. Regardless, the saint's power remains accessible to those in need.

Marie Laveau

Marie Laveau, born in 1792 and known as the Pope of Voodoo, has attained this form of sainthood. Thousands visit her grave in New Orleans annually to petition for favors. She holds a strong reputation for resolving legal difficulties, as she did during her lifetime. The traditional method of contact involves knocking three times on the front of her tomb or drawing three x's in red brick dust or chalk on the stone. Common offerings include salt water or seven dimes.

Saints in Magickal Practice

The intersection of sainthood and magick, sometimes called "folk Catholicism," has existed for centuries. Several Vatican-approved saints are frequently invoked in magick spells, including Saint Anthony, San Cipriano, Saint Martha the Dominator, Saint George, and John the Baptist. When invoked for magick, saints are treated like any other spirit: offerings and payment are made for miracles requested and received. Each saint has favored numbers, colors, fragrances, and gifts.

A saint's magickal associations may diverge significantly from their official hagiography. Saint Anthony, for example, appears in nearly as many love spells as Aphrodite.

Unofficial Saints

Unofficial saints are those recognized for consistent post-mortem miracles without official Church approval. This phenomenon is tied specifically to the Roman Catholic tradition, which is the only system requiring a formal bureaucratic process for conferring sainthood. In traditions without official criteria, such as Judaism, all saints are effectively unofficial, with popularity and demonstrated power serving as their validation.

Unofficial saints may be accessed in magick spells in the same manner as any other spirit.

Identification and Syncretism

Syncretism is the process by which one spirit is identified or fused, to varying degrees, with another. Although most commonly associated with modern African Diaspora faiths, the practice extends back thousands of years.

Ancient Origins

When the ancient Greeks began to travel and encountered other cultures with their own pantheons, they developed a system of identification. Rather than accept entirely foreign spirits, they looked for correspondences with their own deities. Hathor, the Egyptian Spirit of love with an affinity for perfume and music, was declared to be Aphrodite. The Persian Anahita, a deity of romance and fertility who also possessed a martial aspect, was identified as both Aphrodite and Athena.

This process attempts to locate one spirit within another based on shared domains and attributes. Sometimes the identification is clean and intuitive. Other times it creates confusion, as spirits rarely map onto each other in a one-to-one fashion.

Syncretism Under Persecution

Syncretism goes a step further than identification. One spirit wears the mask of another. When a pantheon is outlawed, devotees continue their practice by presenting their spirits in the guise of acceptable ones.

This is precisely what occurred among enslaved Africans in the Western Hemisphere. Forbidden from practicing their own religions but permitted Roman Catholic chromolithographs of saints, they searched the images for coded references to the orisha and lwa. Identifications were often made quickly and visually.

Some of these pairings work remarkably well. Ogun, Spirit of Iron, was syncretized with the Archangel Michael because Michael's most famous image shows him wielding a sword. The two share genuine common ground: both are tireless warriors on behalf of human safety, and Michael carries his own associations with iron.

Other pairings are more unexpected. Chango, the intensely virile Spirit of Fire and Lightning, was syncretized with the virgin martyr Saint Barbara because her chromolithograph depicts a lightning bolt.

Elegba, the road-opening trickster, was syncretized with Saint Peter on account of Peter's keys.

The Fluidity of Spirits

Spirits are fluid in nature, and shape-shifting comes naturally to them even without deliberate syncretism. India's Durga is an aspect of Parvati. In a moment of extreme crisis, Durga unleashed Kali, her own alter ego. All three are aspects of a single being, yet all three are also distinct entities.

The first generation to engage in syncretism is fully conscious of the process. After that, the lines blur. At what point do the spirits genuinely fuse? Saint Peter, syncretized with Elegba because of his keys, may have effectively become Elegba, or the reverse. After several generations, the original spirit behind the mask may no longer be remembered, yet the magickal behavior persists.

Syncretism From Above

Syncretism did not always originate with the oppressed. Religious authorities sometimes deliberately presented it to make a new faith more palatable. The Celtic Spirit Brigid, known as the Druid's daughter, became identified with Saint Brigid. The two merged so thoroughly that distinguishing where one ends and the other begins is nearly impossible. The Irish solar spirit Aine was demoted to a Queen of Fairy, remaining accessible to old devotees in a diminished role. Maeve underwent the same transformation.

Sometimes this process backfired. Missionaries in Guatemala attempted to syncretize the spirit Maam with Saint Simon. The resulting figure, Maximon (also known as Brother Simon), a spirit of primal male energy, defied all boundaries and took on a life of his own. When the Church then attempted to associate him with Judas Iscariot or the devil, the outlaw image only deepened his devotees' loyalty. Rather than merging with an official saint, Maximon emerged as a powerful unofficial saint in his own right.

Hidden Spirits

Sometimes syncretism occurred so long ago that the original spirit underneath has been entirely forgotten. The only indication that syncretism may have taken place is when a saint behaves in notably un-saintly ways within magickal practice. This applies especially to the three saints most prominent in magickal Catholicism: Saint Anthony, Saint George, and John the Baptist. Many practitioners believe that beneath their respective masks lie Hermes, Baal, and Adonis.

Santeria itself takes its name, "religion of the saints," from syncretism. Some practitioners emphasize the Roman Catholic side, others the African roots, and for many, the saint and the orisha have fused so completely that separation is no longer possible.
