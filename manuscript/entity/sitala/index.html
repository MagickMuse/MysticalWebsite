Overview

Sitala, the Cool One, is the great goddess of smallpox and pustular disease in northern India. She is at once the bringer of these afflictions and the power that protects against and heals them, and for this reason she is approached with both fear and devotion. Though she is most famous through smallpox, that is only one of her faces and not the whole of her nature.

A Village Goddess

Sitala belongs to the family of village goddesses, the local mothers who are rooted in a particular place and concerned above all with the welfare of their own people. Like her sisters across the land, she is tied to the existential concerns of ordinary life: health, fertility, the safety of children, and the survival of the community. She is approached not as a remote cosmic power but as a present, immediate, and intensely personal deity.

Grace and the Eruption of Disease

Outside of an outbreak Sitala is quiet, withdrawn, almost beyond reach. When she erupts into a community through an epidemic, that visitation is spoken of as her grace or her play. The disease is understood as her touch, her literal presence within the body of the one she has chosen, a state close to possession. To be marked by her is to be inhabited by her.

A Wider Vision of Reality

The deeper teaching attributed to Sitala is that her visitations strip away the comfortable illusion that suffering and death merely come and go and can be forgotten in good times. To attend to her is to hold past, present, and future together and to see that disease, death, and disaster are always present beneath the surface of ordered life. By accepting her constant presence, the devotee gains a steadier, less fragile view of existence, one that the sudden arrival of tragedy cannot shatter. Her grace is precisely this hard knowledge of one's true place in the universe.

Keeping Her Cool

Because she grows dangerous when she heats up, as a body does with fever, the central concern of her worship is to keep her cool. Cooling foods and cold water are offered, heating foods and activities are set aside during her rites, and the same cooling that soothes a fever in the sick is understood to soothe the goddess herself. In tending one, her devotees tend the other.

Manifestation

Sitala's most famous manifestation is as smallpox itself, though this is only one manifestation and not her essential nature. She also appears as a woman dressed in red, riding a donkey in search of victims.

Origin

Sitala is said to be the youngest of many spirit sisters, born considerably later than the others and initially receiving little human attention. Frustrated, she determined to force humans to create rituals in her honor, and so she invented smallpox, a tactic that worked: she became one of India's most popular spirits. A modern legend suggests that as smallpox incidence declined and veneration waned, she raised the stakes and produced AIDS. She is now closely identified with AIDS as well as smallpox, credited with both causing and healing each.

Sacred Sites

Several shrines and temples are dedicated to her throughout northern India, traditionally served by a female priesthood. During an outbreak, the true home of the goddess is considered to be the body of the patient for the duration of the disease.

Additional Notes

Vaccinations do not offend her; her priests reportedly performed primitive forms of inoculation prior to the development of the modern vaccine. Her annual festival occurs around March. Position her altar toward the northeast.

In Magick

Sitala is petitioned for protection from and recovery through epidemic and pustular disease, for the safety of children, for fertility, and for the steadiness of spirit that comes from facing mortality without flinching. Workings in her honor center on cooling, calming, and the acceptance of what cannot be controlled, turning the heat of fear and affliction into clear sight.
