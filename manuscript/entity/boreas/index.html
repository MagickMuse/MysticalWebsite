Magickal Properties

Boreas, the North Wind, is the spirit of winter, son of Eos (Dawn) and Astraios. He is an aggressive spirit with a violent temper and a history of sexual violence, notably abduction. He possesses the power to sink ships and command the northern winds, and by tradition he sank four hundred Persian ships in defense of Athens. He is associated with the reproductive health of horses, and among his consorts are mares.

Ritual Uses

Boreas is invoked when the North Wind is needed for sailing or weather control. In Roman practice recorded by Pliny, horses' hindquarters were turned to face the north wind to secure conception. He is appealed to for protection against enemy fleets and naval threats. The Greeks identified him as Thracian on account of his northern origin, and this Thracian association shaped the direction in which his cult was oriented.

Pitys and the Pine

Among the women Boreas pursued was Pitys, a dryad. In some tellings he chased her off a cliff, and Gaia took pity on her lifeless body and transformed it into a Pine. In other tellings Pan, who loved her, changed her into the tree to shelter her from Boreas's pursuit.

Additional Notes

Boreas abducted an Athenian princess who resisted his advances, sweeping her away to Thrace. This abduction made the Athenians perceive him as a kind of in-law obliged to be their ally, and with the princess he fathered two sons, called the Boreads, and two daughters. When Athens was threatened by the Persian fleet, successful appeals were made to Boreas for protection, and the storm that followed became the defining act of his relationship with the city. The Roman author Pliny describes the belief that mares would conceive if positioned facing the north wind. Across all of these traditions Boreas represents the harsh, masculine power of winter and of northern storms.

Encyclopedia Source Notes

Boreas is a god of the north wind honored in both Greek and Roman traditions, and in Rome he was also called Aquilo. His character was identified with the winter frosts and with the bitter storms that come from the north. He is remembered as the divine hand behind the storm that destroyed the Persian fleet as it sailed against Athens, and this deliverance secured him a place in Athenian devotion as a friendly power turned against the city's enemies.

Hesiod, in the great early Greek cosmogonic poetry, gives Boreas as the son of Eos, goddess of the dawn, and Astraios, lord of the starry sky, and names his origin as Thracian. The poetic tradition preserves the epithet of Thracian Boreas who huddles the thick clouds, a phrase that fixes him as the northern wind whose approach gathers the storm.

Boreas belongs to the class of the four principal Anemoi, the directional wind gods, alongside Zephyros of the west, Notos of the south, and Euros of the east. His Roman counterpart Aquilo carries the same office in the Latin tradition, and his sister-figure Aurora, the Roman dawn goddess derived from Eos, shares his cosmogonic descent.
