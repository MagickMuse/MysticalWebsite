Also known as

Centeotl, Centeocihuatl (goddess of the maize), Cinteotl, Chicomecoatl (in some readings)

Overview

Centeocihuatl is a corn goddess of the classical Aztec religion of the central Mexican plateau, and her name means the corn woman or the maize goddess, from centeotl meaning corn or maize deity and cihuatl meaning woman. She is represented at various sites across the Aztec religious world, including the great Toltec-Aztec center at Tula in what is now the state of Hidalgo, and according to the surviving codices Borgia, Cospi, and Fejervary-Mayer she stands as one of a group of four temple deities honored together within the sacred calendar. In some readings she is understood as a form of the maize-and-fertility goddess Chicomecoatl, whose seven-serpent name gathers the fullness of the growing corn into a single divine figure.

Classification

Corn goddess of the classical Aztec religion. One of a group of four temple deities in the sacred codices. Related to and sometimes identified with the maize-fertility goddess Chicomecoatl.

Domains

The maize crop and its ripening, the sacred corn on which Mesoamerican life depends, the fourfold temple-guardian office within the sacred calendar, the transformation of the earth's fertility into the food that sustains the community, the female counterpart of the corn deity Centeotl.

Iconography

Depicted in the manner of the Aztec maize deities, with the corn ears held in her hands or worn as an adornment, wearing the headdress and clothing appropriate to the corn goddesses of the codices. Her image sometimes shows her as a young corn maiden, other times as a mature mother of corn, in accord with the different stages of the crop from planting to harvest.

Attributes

Corn ears and cobs, corn husks worn as ornament, a woven basket of maize, the digging stick of the Mesoamerican farmer, the sacred calendar glyph of her office.

Sacred Sites

Honored at Tula in the state of Hidalgo among other classical Mesoamerican centers, and represented in the surviving sacred codices Borgia, Cospi, and Fejervary-Mayer, which preserve some of the fullest surviving evidence of the classical Aztec religion. Her cult belongs to the wider religious system of the central Mexican plateau in the pre-Conquest period.

Ritual

Honored in the ceremonies of the maize year: at the planting of the corn, at the ripening of the ears, at the harvest, and at the storage of the crop for the coming year. Offerings appropriate to her cult include fresh corn, cornmeal, tortillas, cacao, and the flowers of the field. In her fourfold temple-guardian office she was honored together with the other three deities of that group according to the ceremonies preserved in the surviving codices.

Lore

Maize is the central sacred food of the peoples of Mesoamerica, and the corn deities of the Aztec religion hold a place of particular importance within the wider pantheon, since the whole civilization is understood in the Aztec creation stories to rest upon the maize as its founding food. Centeocihuatl belongs to that central complex of maize deities alongside Centeotl in his male form, Chicomecoatl the seven-serpent goddess of the ripe corn, and the wider family of corn goddesses whose worship holds the maize year together. Her name gathers the corn and the woman into a single word, and her office is that gathering carried into the ordinary life of the community.
