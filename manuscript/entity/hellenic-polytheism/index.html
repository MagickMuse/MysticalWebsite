Hellenic Polytheism

Contemporary Hellenic polytheism places its center of gravity in the home. Of all the shapes the religion takes, the rites of the household are the oldest, the most direct, and the most enduring. They are how a practitioner builds and sustains a working bond with the Gods, the heroes, and the ancestors, and they remain the soil from which civic ceremony, mystery cult, and philosophical contemplation later grew.

On Kharis

Kharis (Greek: χάρις; also transliterated as charis) names the reciprocal favour that binds mortals and Gods within Hellenic tradition. It covers outward grace or favour, the grace felt by giver and receiver alike, and the homage, worship, and majesty owed to the Gods themselves.

At its heart, kharis is the practice of offering the Gods something pleasing. What passes between mortal and divine is not a contract of strict debt but a relationship that thickens with time, in which the God remembers the gift and feels well disposed to the giver in return. Every act of household worship, every libation, offering, hymn, and prayer, is a moment in this ongoing exchange.

For the fuller treatment, see the Kharis guide.

Names for the Tradition

The religion goes under several names today. The most common are Hellenic paganism, Hellenic polytheism, Hellenic Reconstructionism (a narrower term), and Hellenismos. Each practitioner is free to choose what to call the path, and what to call themselves.

Hellenismos is the Greek word used in late antiquity by the Roman Emperor Julian to describe the polytheistic religion of the late Hellenic world. Julian advocated a return to that religion after the official establishment of Christianity within the empire, much as modern practitioners advocate its return now. The term has gained popularity in recent years.

At its simplest, Hellenismos names the traditional polytheistic religion of ancient Greece, understood as a living tradition running from antiquity to the present.

On Ritual Language and Attire

Ancient Greek in Ritual

Daily prayer in the ancient world was extemporaneous and spoken in the practitioner's own dialect of Greek, since Greek was the vernacular. Set forms, the hymns and formal invocations, were composed in metred verse in the literary Greek of their era.

Hymns as Offerings

Hymns such as the Orphic Hymns and the Homeric Hymns were traditionally considered offerings in themselves, not simply elaborate prayers. Delivered in the original Greek, they carry a sound and a rhythm that a translation cannot reproduce, and that sonic offering is itself part of the gift.

Pronunciation of the Gods' Names

The correct pronunciation of a God's name was understood within Hellenic practice as a mark of due respect, since these are the sounds by which the Gods were addressed for millennia. The full pronunciation reference sits under Notes on Language below.

Ritual Attire

The ancient Greeks did not wear costume for their rites. For festivals they dressed in their finest ordinary clothing. The khiton and the peplos were the standard garments of the classical era, worn in daily life as well as in ceremonial contexts. A common festival ornament was the stephanos, a crown of leaves and flowers worn upon the head. These crowns were favoured for festivals and larger rites and were most often woven from foliage and flowers gathered close to the sanctuary itself.

On Hard and Soft Polytheism

A large share of Hellenic pagans hold that the Gods are real, distinct, individual beings with their own histories and personalities. This view is called hard polytheism.

Soft polytheism covers a range of positions. It may include the belief that the Gods of different cultures are interchangeable (syncretism), or that they are all facets of one divine being (monism), or of a small number of greater beings, as in the common Wiccan tenet that all Gods are the God and all Goddesses are the Goddess. It may also mean treating the Gods as archetypes or symbols.

Based on a hard polytheist standpoint, this Grimoire treats the Gods as distinct beings, and its practices and philosophy proceed from that core.

Which view is closer to the ancient Greek understanding is a matter of debate. On the balance of the evidence, most ancient people appear to have believed in the Gods as distinct beings, though as the culture matured some voices, most notably among the philosophers, began to form other ideas about the divine. It is also clear that at least some ancients had no trouble equating the Gods of other pantheons with their own when the two shared characteristics. An ancient Greek learning of the Norse God Thor, for instance, might infer that Thor was simply another name for Zeus, since both were understood to command lightning. A strict hard polytheist disagrees and treats the Gods of every pantheon as separate beings. The decision belongs to each practitioner.

On Community

Community and group rites formed a substantial part of ancient Hellenic religion. Families practised together within the Oikos, while wider observances took place at the level of the phratria, the deme, and the polis. A working vocabulary developed across the tradition for the smaller religious associations that sat between family and city, and several of these terms remain in use.

  • Demos is drawn from the ancient word for a district. It names a group of Hellenic polytheists who perform ritual together regularly, and continues in this sense within the organisation Hellenion.
  • Eranos carries connotations of a shared meal, a kindness or favour, and a club. It remains in use within the group Neokoroi.
  • Thiasos names a group devoted to one particular God. The word originally applied to Dionysian gatherings, and its use has since broadened to associations devoted to other Gods.

Understanding the Ancient Mindset

The ancient Hellenic mindset toward the divine reached beyond the surface facts of what was done in each circumstance. It was not only a matter of ritual form or of mythic variant.

A telling example lies in the libation. In ancient Greek practice, a libation was always poured out for the Gods, even when the liquid was also shared among the worshippers afterward. The underlying belief was that the liquid must actually be surrendered for the act to constitute an offering. Neighbouring traditions handled the same devotional impulse differently. The Norse toasted to their Gods and drank the entire liquid, in the belief that the Gods still benefit from the act. Small distinctions of this kind, held across many points, are what set each cultural tradition apart and are what made Hellenismos what it was.

The pattern extended past ritual into theology. In the Greek view, the spirits of ponds, streams, trees, and similar places were all female, the nymphs, whereas other traditions held that such spirits could be either female or male.

Innovation Within the Tradition

Innovation itself was not foreign to the ancient tradition. The Athenian state turned to the oracles of Zeus at Dodona and of Apollo at Delphi on religious questions, seeking approval for the founding of a new cult, for the introduction or changing of sacrifices, and for the alteration of statues on sacred land or of other sacred property. Additions and adjustments to the tradition were regularly made under such oracular sanction across every era of the ancient world.

Sources of the Tradition

No body of scripture from antiquity defines this religion. There is no canonical text and no prophetic founder. What survives instead is a layered record built from many overlapping kinds of evidence.

  • Drama, poetry, and historical narrative composed across the classical and post-classical eras.
  • Excavated sanctuaries, statues, altars, inscriptions, votive tokens, and pottery recovered through archaeology.
  • Folk customs preserved across the regions of Greece, together with international scholarship that documents them.

These streams reinforce one another, and together they form the working library from which contemporary practice is reconstructed.

The Experiential Approach

Hellenic religion is a living relationship, not a fixed procedure manual. Historical study clarifies what counts as correct form by ancestral standards, but the test of any practice is whether the worshipper actually reaches and keeps contact with the divine.

Modern life differs from the conditions of earlier Hellenic eras. Devotion adapts to present circumstances without smuggling in outside frameworks of any kind that would dilute the tradition. Personal circumstance, environment, and lived experience also shape practice in the home, so two devotional rhythms may look quite different and both be valid. There is no strict gospel to enforce. Imposing one would itself be foreign to the character of the tradition.

Telos, Arête, and Eudaimonia

The fixed points of Hellenic life are the Universe, Nature, and the human being. Lifestyles change and ideologies turn over, yet these three reference points stay constant, and the human aspirations that orient toward them stay constant with them.

Blessed is Physis, who places what is necessary within easy reach and renders the difficult unnecessary.

The trajectory of a Hellenic life points toward Telos, the perfected end. Arête (Virtue) and Eudaimonia (Well-being) describe the texture of that perfected life. In such a life, what is genuinely necessary is freely at hand, and what is unnecessary never accumulates value out of proportion to its actual worth.

Defining Hellenic Religious Tradition

Hellenic Religion is the indigenous, polytheistic religious life of the Hellenic peoples, traced from their earliest historical presence to the present. Through long centuries of shifting practice, its center has stayed the same, a living link to the divine sphere. It has also never stood apart from the rest of Hellenic culture. Philosophy, politics, science, and the arts grew from the same root, and together they make up what is recognized as the Hellenic worldview.

Two Basic Forms

Two forms of worship anchor the tradition and have done so since antiquity.

  • Household Worship, the rites carried out within the home and personal space.
  • Civic Worship, also called Communal Worship, the public worship of the community or polity.

Mystery cults appear at the periphery of these two main forms. The mantic art, the practice of formal divination, may also be restored to its ancient shape when conditions allow.

Why Household Worship Is Central

Among all the forms, household rites carry the heart of the religion. They allow a worshipper to step into awareness of the divine without first wading through theological argument or sectarian debate. Philosophy is one valid expression of the tradition, yet it is not the entry point. The entry point is the household altar.

These rites are also the oldest stratum. Every other expression, civic, mystic, and philosophical, descends from them. They mark a practitioner today as a follower of the ancestral path. The link between worshipper and god is built and kept through these specific acts of devotion.

The Oikos

For a Hellene, the Oikos (Household) sits at the center of religious life. The term covers more than the modern sense of a single building. It takes in the people of the family living there, the entire estate with its buildings and possessions, and the inherited customs of worship that the family carries forward. Until at least the Hellenistic era, individuals were never named in isolation. A person was placed within nested groups, the household first of all, then the phratria or clan, the tribe, and the city.

A Common Misconception

A widespread assumption treats the great civic festivals and the mystery cults as the primary shape of Hellenic religion, with home practice either absent or peripheral. The mistake is understandable. Surviving texts, pottery, utensils, and architectural remains preserve far more about public ceremony than about what happened inside an Oikos. Earlier scholarship therefore concentrated almost entirely on state and communal forms.

Even with rising attention to home worship in recent decades, some practitioners still treat the public feasts and ceremonies of the city, recorded mostly from the Classical Era, as the centerpiece of the religion. That view flattens daily Hellenic life and tilts the balance too far toward the civic side.

The actual relationship runs the other way. From a religious standpoint, civic and communal observance is the household form scaled up to the level of a city or a broader community. Ancient writers themselves often passed over home practice with shorthand phrases such as "according to the common" or "as per usual," because the daily ceremonies were so familiar that few felt the need to record them in detail.

Why Household Worship Matters Today

For practitioners who want to follow ancestral religion now, the household rites are indispensable. They develop a more grounded religious consciousness and serve as the route by which the tradition can return to broader society. The Oikos also offers shelter from the religious discrimination still felt in much of the world, and felt with particular weight in Greece, where Hellenic Religion holds no official state recognition.

The Principle Gods of the Oikos

Five Gods govern the religious life of the home.

  • Hestia
  • Zeus
  • Hermes
  • Hekate
  • Apollo

Within the domestic sphere every God except Hestia carries qualities distinct from those They hold on Olympus. As Household Gods their shared purpose is the cohesion and welfare of the Oikos.

On the Domestic Aspects of Hestia and Zeus

Hestia and Zeus both stand at the centre of household worship, and each takes on domestic aspects that reach beyond Their better-known Olympian offices. What follows is the specific texture of these household cults preserved from antiquity.

Hestia and the Hearth

Hestia is less anthropomorphised than the other Gods of the Hellenic pantheon. She is not represented in mythic narrative to the same degree, and in cult She is the hearth itself. In the ancient home the hearth held the central place, and virtually every private sacrifice and offering took its bearing from that fire. Meals were eaten around the hearth, and at the opening of every meal a portion of the food was cast into the fire for Hestia. She was the first to receive a portion of any sacrifice performed within the home, and this priority appears to have carried outward into public sacrifice as well, where the first portion often went to Hestia before any other God.

Any new member of the household was first brought to the hearth to be recognised there. Oaths were sworn upon the hearth, and the flame stood as witness to the binding word.

Zeus Ktesios of the Storeroom

Zeus in His aspect as Ktesios, meaning the Acquirer, holds the pantry and storeroom of the household under His protection. Traditionally a jar or amphora was set aside for Zeus Ktesios and placed within the storeroom. The vessel was filled with fresh water, olive oil, and a mixture of various fruits, a panspermia offering, and its handles were bound with wool fillets. Under this domestic aspect Zeus takes the form of a snake, as does the Agathos Daimon within the same setting. The snake as household spirit is recorded across Hellenic practice from antiquity, and the form persisted in rural areas of Greece into the last century.

Zeus Herkeios of the Courtyard

Zeus Herkeios, meaning of the fence, is the God of the enclosing courtyard. An altar to Zeus Herkeios stood in the interior courtyard of a Hellenic house and received sacrifices and libations. This altar marks the domestic space as under Zeus's protection at its inner threshold.

Zeus Kataibates against the Lightning

Some houses raised an additional altar in front of the dwelling to Zeus Kataibates, meaning He who descends, the God under His aspect as the striker of lightning. The altar served to turn aside lightning strikes upon the house.

The Sacred Rites

Devotional rites predate written history. Material remains, surviving literature, and ongoing scholarship all converge on this conclusion. The oldest of them are credited to the Gods and to the heroes of the ancestral line, and from those origins each generation has built ceremonies to keep contact with the Divine continuous. That work is still ongoing.

The known ceremonies of Household Worship are not the limit of the tradition. When circumstance calls for it, a new rite may be composed, always in deference to ancestral form. Such ceremonies meet the actual situations a practitioner faces today and let the worshipper experience the elevation that comes with each approach to the Divine.

Existing observances can also be expanded by adding new invocations, prayers, blessings, and ritual gestures, provided the roots of any addition stay anchored in tradition. Three things justify any new composition: that it is offered honestly, that the practitioner stays consistent with it over time, and that the act itself puts the worshipper in living contact with the Gods.

The aim is not to freeze the tradition or to replay a past century. Each generation lives its own moment, and the call is simply to do so as a Hellenic practitioner, with the piety, the respect, and the seriousness the ancestors brought to their own ceremonies, in a Hellenic Way.

The Boundary Gods of the Oikos

Beyond the main Gods of the household, three further divinities watch over the Oikos from its outer edges. Their charge is to ward off disease, hostile entry, and other troubles arriving from beyond the property. Devotions to Hermes, Hekate, and Apollo under the epithet Agyieus, a name translated as the One who walks the road, are made at the threshold of the dwelling and just outside its perimeter fence.

Hermes and Hekate stand here in the same office They hold along the roads. Hermes is present as the herm, the upright pillar that fixes the limits of a place. Hekate keeps watch as sovereign of the meeting of ways, since each doorway opens onto a junction where several paths lead outward in different directions.

Archaeological remains and a small body of surviving literature indicate that one or more of these Gods received an altar or shrine at every domicile. As part of the devotion owed to Them, the images had to be carefully tended and kept clean, because constant exposure to the open street wore on them quickly. Hermes was rendered as His herm, statues served for both Him and Hekate, and Apollon Agyieus took the form of a plain square stone block. A niche was customarily set into the fence beside the doorway to hold these images along with the smaller working tools of the rite, the incense burner and the offering bowl among them. In an apartment building today, the equivalent is a shelf or ledge that serves the same function. Many older houses in Greece still preserve such niches, though within them the ancient images have most often given way to Christian icons.

Within Hellenic Religion, two-dimensional pictures may decorate the home freely, but devotional images in active worship should be three-dimensional: statues, carvings, or other objects with depth.

The act of worship for these three Gods is straightforward. Outside of specific sacred days it consists of a simple offering and the burning of incense whenever the practitioner enters or leaves the home.

Family and Ancestral Worship

The Oikos by definition takes in the ancestors and the honours owed to them, so domestic religion separates into two principal lines. One line covers the family Gods of the home. The other covers the dead. Many of the rites carried out by the wider community are also rounded off by, or depend upon, celebrations held inside the Oikos itself, which means the practice of the home undergirds the practice of the polis.

Each person living under the roof has a part to perform, fitted to the particular festival or rite at hand. Formal authority for the religious life of the household tends to rest with the man at its head, while in most observances the daily round of devotion is kept up by the women.

Household Worship is by nature an intimate matter belonging to the family alone. Outsiders, whether close friends or relatives at a distance, may on the right occasion be welcomed in to share specific festivities.

The Main Household Rites

A wide variety of ceremonies take place within the Oikos, yet three central rites stand out from the rest. These three concern the three stages in the existence, or life, of a household.

The Founding of an Oikos: Marriage

The formal beginning of an Oikos comes with a marriage, binding two persons together into a single household. A solitary, childless person living on their own does not by themselves constitute a full Oikos. Lacking the standing to establish complete Household Worship, such a person continues the devotional life of the household they came from, that of parents, forefathers, or the wider phratria (clan) to which they still belong.

Athenian custom expected the wife to leave her parents' home and the worship practiced there in order to take up the form kept by her husband's household. By present standards this is no longer a fixed rule.

The Continuation of the Oikos: Birth

The arrival of a child counts as an expansion of the household, though the newborn becomes a true member of the Oikos only after the formal ceremony that introduces the child to the Gods of that home. The child then becomes the bearer of continuity, carrying the worship of the home forward as the parents pass it along through generational succession.

A birth, like a death, brings a temporary state of miasma (defilement) to the Oikos. The household remains in that condition until the rites restore equilibrium.

Loss in the Oikos: Death

Death alters the Oikos and its rites along two lines. It first brings a temporary miasma that the deceased carries into the home. Once a set period of time has passed and the traditional rite has been performed, the deceased then joins the ancestral pool, becoming one of the 'many' and from that point on receiving honours alongside the rest of the Ancestors.

The cry is not yours.
It is not you who speaks; numberless ancestors speak through your lips.
It is not you who longs; generations past counting long with your heart.
Your first charge, while you serve your kind, is to feel every ancestor alive within you.
The second is to kindle their drive and carry their work forward.

Membership Through Ceremonial Introduction

A consistent thread runs across the rites of the household. No person enters the sacred life of an Oikos without a formal ceremonial introduction to its Gods and ancestors. Historically this applied to a new bride entering her husband's household and, in antiquity, to a newly acquired slave joining the same home. Earlier scholarship has sometimes read these rites as treating the entrant as an 'item' added to the property of the house, and has further extended that reading to suggest the bride was likewise objectified. That interpretation mistakes the purpose. The rite is the threshold through which a new person is recognised by the household's Gods and ancestors and so gains the standing to take part in its observances.

On the Household Altar

An altar at the centre is heaped with flowers on every side, while song and festivity fill the dwelling.

The Altar

The altar is the foundational element of Hellenic Worship. Without it the religion can scarcely be said to exist. It provides the meeting ground between human beings and the Divine and stands at the centre of every act of worship.

As noted in connection with Zeus Êrkeios, a household with a garden or yard may keep a built altar outdoors, in keeping with classical custom. Even where this is possible, an interior sacred space is still required to meet the daily devotional needs of the family.

A typical home keeps two altars indoors. One large altar stands in a prominent position within the central room, while a second is set up in a private area reserved for the devotional life of the household and open only to those who belong to it.

The Household Shrine

The ancients left a workable solution for finding sacred space inside an ordinary dwelling. From the Hellenistic and Roman period onward, homes were fitted with a built-in shrine where the family's prayers could be conducted. Excavated examples have survived from Italian cities buried under the volcanic ash of Vesuvius, and the Romans came to call such shrines a lararium.

This wall recess functions as the household's own 'temple', and every sacred object of the Oikos is gathered within it: statues of the Gods, the altars themselves, and tripods that hold incense and the sacred flame. The space is best located in a room of the home that visitors do not access. Where the shrine cannot be set fully apart from a common room, curtains or some equivalent covering may be drawn to shield it.

When a built-in recess is not available, a wooden cabinet of similar form serves the same role. Simpler arrangements are also valid: a small table or any other furniture set aside to hold the household's ritual objects.

A clay altar of small size is often set among these objects to meet the working needs of household worship. This little altar, sometimes called a fetish, is handed down across generations and stands as the physical token of the unbroken continuity of household worship across the family line.

It is striking how much of Household Worship has carried through the centuries unchanged, persisting even after the imposition of other religions. Many of the outward forms of domestic sacred space were taken up by Christianity, and continuous polytheistic traditions such as Hinduism, with their own household customs, give a clear sense of how a home can still be organised around devotional life. The depiction of snakes within these shrines, noted earlier in connection with Zeus Ktesios, is common across these contexts as well.

The Hearth and the Fire

Contemporary floor plans differ from those of ancient Hellenic homes. The hearth's working role has been taken over by electrical appliances, so certain pieces of household practice need to be adjusted without losing their core meaning.

A fireplace, in its own dedicated space, can be assigned the role of the hearth. Where no fireplace is available, the underlying point holds: the sacredness of the hearth rests in the flame itself. Even in antiquity, the Hearth was not always a built feature of the house. There were periods when it took the form of a movable implement designed to hold a flame.

What matters today, as it did then, is that the household keeps a sacred fire on the hearth for ceremonial purposes such as the kindling of incense. This can be carried out in several ways: a slow-burning candle, a vigil lamp in the traditional form, a small oil lamp, or any comparable vessel. By such means the unbroken hearth-light, together with the proper observances for its renewal, is kept up in line with ancestral form.

Ritual Items

Household worship calls for a small set of dedicated ritual objects. Most of these things are already on hand in any home, and anything missing can be acquired without difficulty. What matters is that each piece is set apart and reserved only for worship. Setting an object aside in this way trains the mind to enter the proper state when the rite begins.

The Hearth Fire

Where no built hearth exists, the substitutes named earlier carry the role: a long-burning candle, an oil lamp, or any comparable vessel that can hold a sustained flame. Reasonable safety precautions belong to every choice. Oil lamps should be fed with natural fuel, olive oil being the traditional option, rather than petroleum or chemical lamp oil. Beeswax and other natural materials are preferred for candles, rather than synthetics of unknown origin.

The Khernips

The cleansing vessel is a wide bowl or basin able to hold enough water for the ritual washing of hands and face that opens every rite. The name Khernips comes from the Greek for 'hand wash'. Standing water is not left in the basin between rites, and the vessel is rinsed clean after every ceremony. In the Classical period, a washbasin was a standard fixture of any home and served this same purpose for all household rites.

The Incense Burner

Incense burns inside a small tripod, or in any comparable burner that can hold a charcoal disc or an incense stick.

The Libation Vessel

Any clean container that holds liquid safely will serve. A replica clay pot reserved only for ritual is the most fitting option, provided it is food-safe and was never acid-washed to mimic age. Libations may consist of just a few drops poured on the altar, or, for fuller portions, caught in a separate vessel set there to receive them.

Storage

A simple container kept beside the altar holds smaller ceremonial supplies such as incense and charcoal.

Offering Baskets

Small baskets work well for holding the gifts presented during the rite.

On Libations and Khoês

The Sallustian view holds that the Gods are perfect beings without need of Their own, which raises the question of why a religion oriented toward Them should be marked by sacrifice, libation, offering, and votive token. Sallustius answers by pointing to the principles of imitation and reciprocity. The practitioner attunes themselves to the sphere of a particular God or Goddess by offering the libations and tokens proper to that sphere, and the gift does its work through resemblance, not through any want on the divine side.

In modern practice the live sacrifice of animals has largely been replaced by cakes shaped in the form of the sacred animal. This substitution is not a recent innovation. The same arrangement was used on many occasions by ancestral practitioners.

Divine providence reaches everywhere, and only a small measure of insight on the human side is required for it to be welcomed in. The ground of that insight, and of communion, is the principle of imitation and reciprocity. On that principle the temples were raised in the image of the heavens, the altar in the image of the earth, the statues in the image of life, which is why the Gods are figured in living form. Prayer carries the principle of Nous. The sacred characters carry the ineffable higher powers. Herb and stone carry physical matter, and the sacrificed animal carries the irrational portion of the human being. None of this is a gain on the divine side. Could anything further follow from being a god? It is the human side that gains, through entering communion.

Drawn from Sallustius, On the Gods and the World, XIII.

Libations

The libation is a sacred act and a foundational element of Hellenic Worship. It is the ritual pour of a liquid offering. The vessel may be emptied across the altar surface, into the sacred fire, onto bare earth, at the roots of a tree, or into the sea, a river, or a lake. The fluid varies with the occasion. Water, oil, wine, honey, milk, fruit juice, and perfumes all serve.

Few acts in the tradition rank above the libation. The practitioner gives the finest portion of their own self and lets that respect flow into communion with the Divine. The act validates speech as well, both blessing and undertaking. Once the liquid leaves the vessel it cannot return, and that physical finality mirrors the finality of the spoken word. What is spoken cannot be unspoken. The libation thus binds Gods to mortals in a sacred contract, since neither pour nor utterance can be recalled.

Homer notes libations poured before sleep. Hesiod records them before the morning meal and again at sunset, and adds a sharp caution: no sparkling-wine libation should reach Zeus, or any other deathless God, after sunrise from hands that remain unwashed, since prayers made in that condition are not received but cast straight back.

Wine and Sober Libations

Red wine predominates in libation because it is said to mirror the divine nectar. It carries the symbolism of sacrifice, of youth, of eternal life, and of sacred inebriation, the bacchic ecstasy.

Wineless, or sober, libations are also poured, drawn from honey, water, oil, and similar fluids. Each takes its name from the liquid involved: Melisponda (ΜΕΛΙΣΠΟΝΔΑ) for the honey libation, Hydrosponda (ΥΔΡΟΣΠΟΝΔΑ) for the water libation, and so on through the rest.

Sober libations are typically offered to the Heroes, to Mnemosyne, to the Muses, to the Eumenides, to Aphrodite Urania (the heavenly), to the Nymphs, to Auge (dawn), and to Selene and Helios.

The Libation Vessels

Several traditional vessels carry the pour. The oinokhoë is the wine jug. The skyphos is a drinking cup. The depas is a goblet. The krater stands for the whole, the unifying bond of a household, of a community, of a city, of an entire country.

These vessels are reserved only for the sacred act and are kept scrupulously clean. During the pour, the celebrant grips the vessel with one hand while the free hand stays in the prayer gesture. The liquid leaves the vessel in a steady stream, and those present do not drink any of the wine until the libation has been completed.

Khoês

Khoês are libations directed toward the Khthonic Gods, the Ancestors, or the recently deceased. The act differs from a standard libation in its motion. The vessel is tipped abruptly so the entire contents fall at once, onto the soil, into a grave, or into a pit dug in the ground. The same care for vessel material and cleanliness applies. The fluid in a khoê, however, is never drunk afterward, which sets it apart from the handling of an ordinary libation.

A funerary khoê is depicted in the Odyssey, where Odysseus, as a supplicant approaching the dead, empties one into a pit.

The three customary khoês given to the Ancestors draw on water, honey, and milk. Wine is rarely poured in this setting. The Odyssey passage that does name a wine offering belongs to a magickal rite for calling forth the shades of the departed, not to a standard commemorative ceremony.

Libations for Oaths

A libation also accompanies the taking of an oath. The wine in this rite is never drunk. Once the entire vessel has been emptied, the vessel itself is shattered, and that act seals the unbreakable vow.

On Offerings and Tokens

A required act of Hellenic Worship is the giving of incense, fruit, seeds, flowers, bread, cakes, sweets, and similar gifts. These items are placed on the altar during a household ceremony. A portion is set aside for the Gods and the remainder is shared among those gathered.

Guidance on which offerings and tokens belong to which God is gathered from many strands at once: surviving texts, painted pottery, ritual vessels, sculpture, inscriptions, and other archaeological finds. The mix of offerings shifts with the type of household ceremony being performed.

On Prayers and Hymns

I speak to those who lawfully may hear: depart all ye profane, and close the doors.
The thoughts of a wise theology, in which men indicated God and the powers of God through images akin to sense and sketched the unseen in seen forms, I will show to those who have learned to read the statues as one reads a book, gathering from them the lettered teaching concerning the Gods. It is no wonder that the utterly unlearned take the carved images for wood and stone, just as the illiterate look upon inscribed monuments as mere stone, on writing-tablets as bits of wood, and on books as nothing more than woven papyrus.

Drawn from Porphyry, On Images.

ΘΥΕΙΝ ΤΕ ΚΑΙ ΕΥΧΕΣΘΑΙ, Sacrifice and Pray. Addressing prayers and hymns to the Gods is the central act of worship in Hellenic religion. Prayer and hymn differ in both shape and delivery. The hymn is composed in metered verse or in measured prose and joins an invocation to a main body of praise, eulogy, and thanksgiving directed at the Gods and at the Heroes. The prayer is simpler in form and less poetic in character, holding only the invocation and the petitions that follow it.

Prayer

Prayer is the act of worship by which the worshipper calls on the divine to hear the petition and to grant it. It belongs to every household rite, every communal rite, and every personal rite carried out under traditional custom.

The one directing the libations, offerings, and sacrifices ordinarily speaks the prayers in free verse, in a clear and well-modulated voice. Any devout worshipper may compose such a verse. A prayer opens with an invocation that includes the proper epithets and identifying marks of the God or Gods being addressed. The request or petition follows.

A personal example survives in Plato's Personal Prayer to Pan, drawn from the Phaedros.

The worshipper may raise a prayer at any moment moved to it, always with due devotion. The most pious prayers are those that entreat without demanding and never attempt to command the Gods. The one prayed for may be the speaker, a third person, or the wider community.

In its general effect, prayer gives strength to the soul and steady support to mind and heart, for individual and collective alike. It stands as one of the integral channels by which contact with the Gods is kept open.

Socratic teaching narrows the proper content of petition to its simplest form. The wise worshipper asks only for what is good, trusting that the Gods discern the good more clearly than mortals can. To pray for gold, silver, sovereignty, or any specific outcome whose worth is uncertain is closer to wagering on a throw of dice than to true devotion, since the petitioner cannot know whether the thing requested will, in the end, prove a blessing or a harm.

But I may and must pray to the Gods that my departure hence be a fortunate one; so I offer this prayer, and may it be granted.

Drawn from Plato, Phaedo.

Devotional Hymns

A hymn is a devotional tribute to the divine, given in lyrical form, a poem set to music, performed during a festival or a ceremony. The first section is the invocation to a particular God or Gods. The main and often lengthy part follows, listing epithets and dominions, raising praise of powers, and exalting the greatness of the divine attributes. Hymns frequently bring in mythological references to the God or to a related festival.

The hymn parts company with prayer on three points. It is lyrical in form. It carries a long exaltation of the greatness of the God. It contains no petition.

The Hymn to Telesphoros, known to Athenians as For Telesphoros, stands as one example of the form, sung in remembrance of the Daemon's part in lifting feverish disease from the land and bringing joy to those weighed down by trouble.

The Orphic Hymns and the philosophical hymns, such as those of Proclus, hold both elements together. They open with the typical body of a hymn but close with a petition to the divine. Whether either set was originally set to music is no longer known. The Orphic Hymn to Artemis illustrates this hybrid shape, with the petition arriving in the closing four lines.

Alongside hymns, the tradition keeps several other forms of devotional song: the paian, the dithyramb, the parthenion, the prôoimio, and others. The Paian to Hygeia, composed by Ariphron, is a surviving example of the paian form. The orator Aristeides advised that composers of dithyrambs and paians close with a prayer.

In today's household rites, the use of hymns that carry an element of prayer, such as the Orphic Hymn to Artemis, is customary. The crafting of a personal hymn or prayer in line with the prescribed traditional standards is equally acceptable.

The Posture of Adoration

The standing position, with hands outstretched toward the heavens, remains the proper posture for both hymn and prayer when the Heavenly Gods are addressed. With the Khthonian Gods of the earth and the underworld, the hands and arms point downward toward the earth. With other Gods, the arms and hands point in the direction of the related domain.

Examples of kneeling in prayer survive from antiquity, but the posture was uncommon. The Hellenic gesture of veneration was lighter than the full prostration practiced by Persian and Egyptian custom: the worshipper bent the knee and body slightly, kissed the hand, and then touched with that same hand the object of their devotion.

The Traditional Prayer Formula

The traditional structure of a Hellenic prayer follows a recognisable form. Each element of the formula has its ancient function, and the sequence is repeated across the surviving prayers of the tradition.

  • The call to attention. The prayer opens with an address inviting the God to attend. Common openings are hear me and come.
  • The naming of the God. The God is named in the vocative case, the case reserved in Greek for direct address.
  • The epithets. One or more cult titles of the God are added, sometimes at length, and may replace the name entirely. In some prayers the formula or by whatever name it pleases you to be called is appended to cover the case in which the correct epithet is unknown.
  • The places of dwelling. The God is invoked in relation to the places He or She is known to inhabit, whether birthplace, famous temple site, or particular sanctuary. The worshipper often asks the God to come from wherever the God presently is to the place of the prayer.
  • The record of past honour. Where the worshipper has previously sacrificed to this God, or the God has previously assisted the worshipper, these instances are named and recalled to the God's attention.
  • The petition and the vow. The request is made, and it is accompanied by a vow to sacrifice to the God should the prayer be answered. Where a sacrifice has already been given as encouragement of the desired outcome, the petition is made in that light instead.

One of the fullest ancient examples of the formula is the prayer of Chryses, priest of Apollo, at the opening of the Iliad. Chryses names Apollo through His epithets and cult sites, recalls his own past honours given to the God, and then makes his petition.

Hear me, God of the silver bow, You who protect Chryse and holy Killa and rule Tenedos with Your might, hear me Sminthian God. If ever I have decked Your temple with garlands, or burned for You thigh-bones in fat of bulls or goats, grant my prayer, and let Your arrows avenge these my tears upon the Danaans.

Drawn from Homer, Iliad.

Silent Prayer

Prayer is ordinarily spoken aloud. The Gods are held to hear the softest of entreaties, and in the cult of certain subterranean Gods silent prayer is prescribed rather than voiced.

On Purification

Having traversed the virtuous path toward Olympus, enter, stranger, enter without fear if you are arriving pure. But if you are carrying blame, leave the blameless temple, go wherever you wish, but stay away from the temenos of Pallas.

From a temple inscription at Lindos.

One who goes inside the sweet-smelling temple must be pure. Pure, not only by bathing, but also in mind.

From a Rhodian temple inscription, first century AD.

Ritual purity stands among the foundational elements of Hellenic religion. Because an ontic separation, in the philosophical sense, divides the world of the Gods from the world of the living and the world of the dead, any approach toward either of these realms must be made through the proper rites. For a living person to draw near the Gods demands purity of body, mind, and spirit, and this is most often attained through the simple ritual cleansing that opens every ceremony. Certain states of contamination, however, run deeper than ordinary uncleanness, and these forbid approach to the Gods altogether until they are lifted through dedicated rites.

The full household form of the cleansing is laid out in the Hellenic Purification Ceremony, and the mythic model of the rite survives in Oedipus' Purification Rite to the Eumenides.

Miasma

Miasma is the spiritual residue left when the natural flow of life is broken or diverted. It clings to every transitional event in human existence and to any act that disturbs the proper order of things. On the personal and familial level, infection, disease, death, sacrilege, violence, the spilling of human blood, murder, and crime in general all leave miasma upon those touched by them. On the level of the wider community, the polluting of water, the spread of pestilence, and the destruction of a city or its altars and sanctuaries carry the same kind of disturbance outward into the shared life of the people. Such acts and events leave their mark not only on the body but also on the soul. Death itself is miasma, and cleansing is imperative for any who have been near the deceased. A murderer is held to be impure and abominable; the affliction is understood to spread from the killer to other people and to the surrounding environment, and one in such a state may not take part in any holy ceremony until the stain has been lifted.

Purification

Purification is the means by which miasma is answered and removed. Through cleansing rites, body, mind, soul, and environment are restored to a state fit for sacred contact. The scope of a purification rite shifts according to need, reaching from a single person to a household, from a group of worshippers to an entire city, with the chosen methods drawn to suit the situation at hand. A cleansing may stand on its own as the whole purpose of a ceremony, or it may serve as the opening movement of another rite, preparing the worshipper to approach the divine. The mythic record preserves the seriousness of these requirements. After slaying the suitors, Odysseus undertook cleansing actions before resuming the ordinary life of his household. Heracles, having killed the Centaurs, sought initiation into the Eleusinian Mysteries so that he might descend into Hades, but the priests refused to receive him until the miasma of murder had first been cleansed from him.

Purificatory Gods

The foremost Gods of purification and cleansing are Zeus, Apollo, and Asklepios. In ancient Athens, purification rites were carried out in honour of Zeus Melikhios during the festival of the Diasia, and warriors returning from battle made offerings to Him in this same form so that they might be cleansed of the miasma of bloodshed and war. Apollo's own myth founds the practice. After the slaying of the Python, He withdrew into a long phase of purification lasting nine years, returning at last with laurel branches in His hands as the sign of fresh life and with songs upon His lips, His quiver emptied of arrows. The Athenian Thargelia took up this current of cleansing on behalf of the city and its citizens, with rites offered jointly to Apollo and to Artemis. The priests at every temple of Apollo across Hellas also performed cleansing rites for any worshipper seeking to lift miasma. Apollo together with His son Asklepios excels in the techniques of cleansing the body and tending wounds.

Physical Cleansing Elements

The principal elements of purification are Fire and Water, with Air joining them in some rites. Fire works ritually to cleanse, to promote healing, and to drive out impurity, and its force is held to reach where ordinary remedies fail.

Those sicknesses which cannot be treated with drugs are treated with iron. Those which cannot be treated with iron can only be treated with fire.

Drawn from words attributed to Hippocrates.

Sea water is the cleansing liquid above all others. Where the sea is not at hand, bubbling or flowing water from rivers and springs, the living water of the land, takes its place.

Do not write in ignorance of the depictions of fire and water, because with these two elements all cleansings are accomplished.

Drawn from Horapollo, Hieroglyphica.

Personal Preparation

Purification combines cleansing with atonement. The body is cleansed through a ritual bath and through fasting from foods that fill it with toxins. The soul is quietened from the cares of daily life and the mind is brought to stillness. The Arête that carries the worshipper into purification is ΑΝΔΡΕΙΑ, Courage.

Purificatory Tools and Practices

Sage stands among the cleansing herbs. Incense disperses negative energy, as does salt, which is also widely used in purification. Sulphur, with its strong odour, is suited to cleansing the place where an injury from assault or murder has occurred.

Lead the way now with blazing torches, and purify with sulphur, according to the sacred law, the innermost corners of the air, so that I may receive the pure breath of heaven. And you, in turn, if anyone has harmed the flow of life by treading it with profane foot, submit it to the cleansing fire, and light the pine before me, so that I may pass through.

Drawn from Euripides, Helen.

Water is the most common cleansing medium of all. Fire dipped into water yields holy water for ritual use. On the first day of the Eleusinian procession, with the help of the priest known as the Ydranos, the initiates purified themselves in the waters of the river Ilissos. On the second day they purified themselves in the sea with the famous cry ΑΛΑΔΑΙ ΜΥΣΤΑΙ, Into the sea, initiates, from which the day took its name. The cleansing basins recovered from Knossos and Zakros bear a clear relation to the periranteria, the special vessels set outside the entrances of temples and sanctuaries where worshippers washed their hands before entering. In present rites, hand-washing is carried out in dedicated water bowls.

After a funeral, the home is cleansed with water and with the burning of frankincense and dried laurel leaves. The residues left behind by purification rites are called katharmata, the dregs, and are gathered into containers and buried in open ground. The great cleansing festivals are the Thargelia, the Katharsia, the Êkatesia, the Oxythymia, and the Noumenia. During the great civic festivals, the responsibility for the purification rites fell to officials of the city known as Kletores and Dokheis. The Roman tradition preserves the same current under the name February, the month of purification, drawn from the Latin februa, the Greek katharsios, cleansing.

Another significant means of cleansing places, things, and people is the Zeus Kodeon, a fresh fleece taken from a thick-haired ram dedicated to Zeus Melikhios. At the close of the cleansing ceremony the fleece is carried to a remote place and disposed of there. The Kerykion, the staff of Hermes known to the Romans as the caduceus, also carries an apotropaic function, turning aside and dispelling malevolent forces. Watching performances of Greek tragedy is itself counted among the means by which the human soul is purified.

Historical and Mythical Examples

After the slaying of the suitors, Odysseus cleansed the palace with sulphur and fire. Heracles, having killed the Centaurs, requested initiation into the Eleusinian Mysteries so that he might descend into Hades; the priests refused him until he was first cleansed of the miasma. A surviving relief depicts the rite, with Heracles seated on a fleece, his head covered, two lit torches at hand, while a priestess holding a sieve performs the cleansing. This was held to be the necessary preparation for the Hero's ascent to higher planes. Euripides records in Alcestis that three days of cleansing and prayer were needed before the heroine could find her lost conscience after her ascension.

Orestes was cleansed with water from the Ipocrine by nine priests of Troizen. Pausanias notes that on the spot where these priests buried the katharmata a laurel bush grew up, and that the bush was still living in his own day. Warriors returning from battle had to be purified before taking part in any act of worship. After the events known as the ΚΥΛΩΝΕΙΟ ΑΓΟΣ, the Kylonian Affair, the entire city of Athens stood in need of cleansing. The Oracle at Delphi instructed that a complete ceremony be performed by the priest Epimenidis from Phaestos, who cleansed and purified the city for atonement, purification, and the raising of statues, calling upon Zeus and using water drawn from a river. The Pythia herself cleansed her body in the waters of the Kastalia within the precinct of Delphi and drank from the sacred spring of Kassotida before pronouncing an oracle.

Beginning Household Worship

The weight Hellenic Polytheistic Tradition places on home practice has already been emphasised. Within the home there is no outside authority constraining religious freedom, and nothing stands between the worshipper and the rites of the ancestral path. The principles have been laid out. What remains is to start.

The benefit of consistent practice gradually becomes plain in the calm that settles over the home. Through that same continuity, the tradition is passed to the children of the house, and the generations that follow inherit the knowledge that lets them form a steady character and live by the values and principles of the path. A natural first observance is the monthly Noumenia, which opens each lunar month and anchors the household round.

If any element of the procedure feels daunting, or difficulty arises in carrying it out as laid out, the right course is to begin with the simplest actions and master those first. Once they have become second nature, the next set is taken up, and so on until the full practice is comfortable. Someone taking their first steps on the ancestral path would, for example, do better not to turn their attention toward the Sacred Days until the right inner attitude for a ceremony has already been established.

The most important factor in any rite is the attitude and the will of the worshipper toward the Gods and the Ancestors. Strict adherence to procedure matters less than this orientation, though procedures still serve as necessary guides, since constructing a successful devotional ceremony from one's own information without prior ritual experience is difficult. When in doubt, the practitioner trusts the traditions that have been handed down.

On Ceremonies

For the purposes of practical instruction, the more common household ceremonies are kept in the simplest form, while still holding to a workable and comprehensible structure. The basic order is:

  • Preparation and opening of the ceremony, which may include a full ritual cleansing such as the Hellenic Purification Ceremony or a brief period of internal mental preparation, according to personal needs and the circumstances at hand.
  • Lighting of the hearth fire. If no eternal flame is kept in the home, one is lit, usually accompanied by the Orphic Hymn to Hestia or another such hymn or prayer.
  • Placement of offerings and the lighting of incense.
  • Opening of the ceremony with the first invocation.
  • Hymns and prayers, accompanied by libations, drawing on examples such as Plato's Personal Prayer to Pan, the Hymn to Telesphoros, the Orphic Hymn to Artemis, or the Paian to Hygeia.
  • Closing of the ceremony and the giving of thanks.

The Act of Worship

With the underlying theory in place, the next step is the practical sequence in which the basic structure of a rite is carried out. The space prepared in the home for ritual work is set up as follows:

  • Gather every item to be used in the ceremony. Prepare the incense and fill the libation vessels with the appropriate liquids.
  • Fill the cleansing basin with water. Set the offerings in their dishes.
  • Each participant washes their hands in the cleansing basin.
  • Light the incense and place it in the burner, such as the household tripod.
  • At the proper points within the prayers and hymns, the libations are poured, the incense or perfumes are kindled, and the offerings are placed on the altar.
  • After the ceremony has closed, the wine left from the libations and the offerings on the altar are shared among those present.

Any by-products of the rite, ashes, spent offerings, libated wine, and the like, are placed in clean ground so they may return to the earth. A garden bed or a flowerpot serves this purpose. They may also be gathered on the altar and disposed of periodically in a natural setting. Under no circumstances are they treated as refuse or waste.

Selecting Hymns and Prayers

The hymns and prayers used in household ceremony function as guidelines, not mandates. Household Worship is by nature a personal and familial matter, and a measure of flexibility around appropriate texts is part of that character.

Adaptation and improvisation are best held back until the worshipper has reached working familiarity with the act of worship as it stands. Without that grounding, well-intended experiments can throw a ceremony off course.

A wide range of texts may be drawn on: original ancient compositions, modern Greek versions, adaptations, and entirely new pieces. The variety is deliberate. No magicko-religious weight attaches to any particular linguistic form, and the focus belongs on the essence of what is being said rather than on the surface of the words. The Hellenic language has moved through many stages across the centuries, and at every stage practitioners used whatever form of the language was common to their own time to praise and petition the Gods. The same principle applies today. Older hymns and prayers remain usable when their meaning and essence are clearly understood and prove genuinely helpful.

Two elements drawn from civic worship are kept within the household rites observed by the Labrys Polytheistic Community.

Plethon's Blessing (Twenty-second Hymn)

George Gemistos Plethon (1355-1452) lived in the 14th Century CE under Byzantine rule. He developed a polytheistic theology and reopened the question of Hellenic identity, and stands today as an ideal of Hellenic virtue for Hellenes and Hellenists alike. His general hymn praises all of the Gods for the good things they bring to humanity and serves as an excellent opening prayer for any rite, guiding the worshipper along the righteous paths of virtue and into the proper attitude for ceremony. For these reasons it is used as the opening hymn of every ceremony in the household round.

Hymn to a Local or Household Deity

In Hellenic Worship every altar and sacred space is dedicated to a particular God, Goddess, or group of Gods, and every town or area may itself belong to a divine patron. When a ceremony takes place within the precinct of State, or Communal, worship in such a place, a hymn or song of praise to the local deity opens the rite. In household ceremonies such as Noumenia, the equivalent act of devotion is offered to Hestia, to the Êphêstioi, the particular God Protectors of the family, or to the Household Gods. Each household chooses which local Gods or Heroes serve as patrons of its own Oikos.

Statues and images of the Gods are established, yet they hold a cherished place in the home.

Drawn from Plato, Laws 11.932a.

Myth and Practice

The Greek myths have long served as an entry point into Hellenic religion in the wider culture, and many practitioners first arrived at the tradition through love of the stories. The myths themselves are rich and layered, offering a glimpse into the lives of the Gods and the heroes. It is a common misunderstanding, though, that the religion of ancient Greece was built upon these narratives. In practice, the myths carried little weight in day-to-day belief and ritual.

The tension between story and cult is visible in specific cases. Stories of Pan pursuing the nymphs, sometimes violently, could suggest that Pan and the nymphs stood at odds and would not be honoured together. The reverse held true. Many sites across ancient Greece paired them in worship, the famous Korykian cave above Delphi among them, a shrine that belonged jointly to Pan and to the nymphs. To the worshippers, what mattered was that these beings haunted the same places and shared the same nature-spirit character. What they did within the myths was a separate matter.

The myths also arrive in many variants. A single story might survive in half a dozen versions, some of them diverging sharply from one another. In some cases the earliest form can be traced. In most cases it is unclear which telling came first, or whether several forms grew up together in different regions. The lesser known variant in which Ariadne is killed at the end of the Theseus story by Artemis, at the command of Dionysus, is one such alternate that reshapes the meaning of the whole.

None of this settles the question of what the ancients themselves believed. Perhaps each person held to the versions learned in childhood. In later centuries, educated commentators declared the myths to be allegories, and many contemporary practitioners treat them as metaphors for the powers of the Gods or the forces of nature. Others take them as reflecting actual encounters, moments in which the Gods let their actions be known more directly, or acted through mortals for a time, or appeared in dreams and visions. Some accounts may simply be the product of human invention. Any of these readings can coexist within the tradition, since the tradition itself does not require a single interpretation.

Whatever the ancients believed, cult practice and everyday worship rested far more on local custom and personal experience than on the commonly told myths. The word cult here is used in its older, neutral sense, meaning a group of worshippers gathered around one or several Gods. This is why ancient Greek religion is often described as an orthopraxy rather than an orthodoxy. The emphasis falls on doing certain things rather than on believing certain things, the inverse of the pattern found in monotheistic religions. The piety of the ancients found its expression chiefly through acts.

Local Custom and Diversity

Even ritual practice varied from city to city. Ancient Greece was less a unified country than a collection of self-governing towns bound by shared cultural and religious threads. Each deme formed its own religious community, kept its own festivals, and performed its own sacrifices. This diversity carries an important lesson for present practice. Practitioners today are not required to converge on a single correct method or a single festival calendar. Regional variation is native to the tradition.

A pronounced difference also ran between urban and rural religious life. Athens hosted large state-sponsored festivals, numerous temples, and theatrical competitions that drew audiences in the thousands. Rural areas, populated by farmers and shepherds, kept a simpler devotional life: smaller shrines, local festivals, and a focus on the Gods whose favour touched the practical work of the land. Both remain valid models. A modern practitioner may lean toward either, or draw from both, according to environment and circumstance.

A further distinction sits at the centre of Hellenic religious life: the difference between polis religion, the worship of the city or town to which one belonged, and domestic religion, practised privately within every household. The great civic festivals and temples were impressive, but the heart of the ancient religion lay in the everyday devotions carried out by ordinary people in their homes and their fields. Records of household rites are thinner than the records of civic ceremony, yet enough survives to reconstruct the working shape of home practice.

Temples and Sanctuaries in Antiquity

An ancient naos, or temple, was understood as the house of the God or Goddess to whom it was dedicated. Temples were almost always sacred to one deity, or occasionally to two, rather than serving as general religious buildings. The God resided within, embodied in the cult statue. Out of respect for that presence, the actual rituals were performed in front of the temple rather than inside it. A worshipper entered the interior only for a more private and intimate act, such as the placing of a small votive offering.

The temple typically faced east. Before its doors stood the bomos, or altar, most often built as a stone block or a pile of stones. In some cases the altar was formed entirely from the ashes of previous sacrifices, an accumulated record of the rites carried out at that site. For sacrifices directed at Khthonic Gods or at the dead, a bothros was dug instead, a pit into which the offerings were deposited. Worshippers gathered around the bomos during a sacrifice, and the leader of the rite faced east. As a rule, only one major sacrificial festival was held at each temple in a given year.

No large communal temple buildings exist today for Hellenic practice, though some practitioners have felt called to set up small public shrines and welcome visiting worshippers. Until such buildings exist more widely, the home altar or household shrine takes on the role of the temple. The practitioner stands before it to perform the rites, treating it with the same care that the ancient naos received.

On Sacred Places and the Temenos

Within Hellenic tradition, sanctity is understood as inherent in the place itself. The temple did not confer holiness upon the ground on which it stood. Rather, the temple was built on a particular place because that place was already holy. Prominent natural features were held to be the most likely holy places: the tallest mountain of a region, its largest river or spring, or any point in the landscape that struck the observer with a singular beauty or strength.

A holy place set apart and dedicated to a God is called a temenos, from the verb temno, to cut off. The temenos may be marked by a wall, by a natural boundary, or by nothing more than the shared knowledge of the community that the place is dedicated to the God and is therefore not for common use.

Gods and Their Localities

Certain Gods are bound in tradition to particular localities. Aphrodite holds Kupros (Cyprus), Apollo holds Delos, and Athena holds Athens. Sacred cities and islands drew pilgrims from across the Greek world, and the great pan-Hellenic sanctuaries were built in these places rather than being scattered evenly across the geography.

Religious Roles

In ancient practice, the head of the household led the worship and rites of the family. Any member of the household or the community, however, could take part in a sacrifice, present a votive offering, or visit a temple to pray to the God or Goddess residing there. No intermediary stood between a person and a divinity. The role of the priest, as it is understood in the major monotheistic religions, did not exist in ancient Greece.

Ancient priests were citizens who, alongside their ordinary civic life, took on the task of overseeing the cult of a particular God and caring for the temple. Only rarely did a priest's or priestess's entire life revolve around the God they served. The office could be attained by lot, by election, by heredity, or even by sale, and was sometimes held for a lifetime, sometimes only for a set term. A priest or priestess was tied to one God and typically to one specific temple or shrine. A priest of Delian Apollo, for instance, would not be expected to officiate at rites for Poseidon.

Alongside the priests, called Hiereis in Greek, several other religious roles served the community.

  • Exegetai were interpreters of religious law, self-appointed and unattached to any single shrine. They advised individuals and families on the proper conduct of rituals. Their counsel was sought voluntarily and was not binding.
  • Khresmologoi were itinerants who carried collections of famous oracles, including those attributed to Bacis and to the Sibyls, and would read from them for those seeking answers. They were also self-appointed, and their livelihood depended entirely on reputation. They were viewed with mixed regard, though popular enough to sustain a living.
  • Manteis were prophets, capable of foreseeing the future or of interpreting the voice of a God. Some were dismissed as charlatans. Others were respected and widely renowned, among them the Pythia at Delphi.

On the Festival Calendar

Ancient Greek religion included many state-sponsored festivals, and each region kept its own calendar. The most fully preserved is that of Athens. Only fragments survive from most other regions.

The Lunar Month

The ancient calendar was lunar in structure. Each month opened at the first sighting of the new lunar crescent and was counted forward from that point through to the dark moon. Certain days of each lunar month were also set aside for particular Gods. The third of each lunar month was sacred to Athena, the fourth to Hermes, and the seventh to Apollo. The Noumenia, held at the sighting of the new crescent, opened the month itself.

Categories of Festivals

The Athenian festivals fall into several recurring categories.

  • Festivals of the agricultural cycle.
  • Festivals of the human life cycle.
  • Festivals of purification.
  • Festivals dedicated to a particular God, or to a particular aspect, statue, or cult image within a single temple.
  • Festivals arising in commemoration of a past event, such as the deliverance of a city from plague or defeat.

On the Agon

Alongside the ritual core, most large ancient festivals included an agon, a formal contest held in competition for a prize. Some concluded in theatrical contests, at which several plays were presented in succession. Others held musical contests, and others still athletic contests. The original Olympic games began as such a festival, and its athletic events in time overtook every other element in prominence. The contest was itself a devotional form, an act of arête offered to the God of the festival.

On Thusia and the Sacrificial Rite

The most widespread form of ancient Greek ritual was thusia, the public sacrifice, most commonly rendered simply as "sacrifice" in English. The rite was performed within a religious context: the animal was killed swiftly, and the meat was cooked and consumed by the participants. For poorer citizens in antiquity, receiving a portion of sacrificial meat at a public festival was often the only occasion on which they ate meat at all, and the arrangement functioned as a form of civic welfare, since the animals themselves were provided by wealthier citizens or by the state.

The Prometheus Myth

Only the bones wrapped in fat were burned for the Gods; the meat was retained by the worshippers. The explanation for this apportionment lies in an ancient myth. The Titan Prometheus, who often sided with mortals against the Gods, attended a communal feast between men and Gods and divided the sacrificial animal into two piles, one of bones and fat, the other of savoury meat. He arranged the inedible portion to appear the more attractive of the two, and Zeus was thereby led to choose it as the divine share. Angered at the deception, Zeus decreed that mortals and Gods would no longer share a table, and from that point onward humans sacrificed alone and offered the Gods the same portion Zeus had chosen. The Gods were held to receive the fragrant smoke of the burning flesh rather than the flesh itself, since they had no need of food.

Holocaust Sacrifice

In certain rites, most often those directed to the Khthonic Gods, the entire animal was burned upon the fire rather than shared with the participants. This form is called a holocaust sacrifice. It appears in these cases because the Khthonic Gods were held to have different requirements than the Ouranic Gods, and because the total loss of the animal, itself an object of considerable value in antiquity, made the offering a sacrifice in the strictest sense of the word.

The Sacrificial Animals

The animals most frequently offered were cattle, sheep, and goats. Pigs were used only rarely, chiefly in the cult of Demeter and Persephone. Birds and fowl were likewise rare.

The Structure of the Rite

The participants moved in a pompe, or procession, to the temple, and then gathered in a circle around the bomos, the altar. Each worshipper washed their hands in the khernips, the vessel of pure water set at the entrance to the sacred precinct. At the opening of the rite, the officiant called for sacred silence with the formula κοίμησον στόμα, koimēson stoma, meaning let the mouth be still, quieting every participant before the address to the Gods began. The presiding priest or officiant then spoke a prayer aloud, addressing the Gods generally or the God to whom the rite was dedicated. Every participant then cast a handful of barley onto the altar as a first offering, and incense was placed in the altar fire so that fragrant smoke might rise to the Gods. The animal was led forward, and a tuft of hair was clipped from its head and fed to the flames. A trained slaughterer then killed the animal quickly. The splankhna, the inner organs, were roasted, and a few pieces were burned for the Gods. The remaining meat was cooked and shared among those present, sometimes with cakes, broth, and wine as further offerings. The skin of the animal was received by the priest as payment for the service. A sudden burst of flame from the altar during the rite was taken as a sign of divine presence, and the appearance of that sign was among the aims of the ceremony.

It is not the abundance of wine, nor the roasting of meat, that makes the joy of sharing a table in a temple, but the good hope and the belief that the God is present in kindness and graciously accepts what is offered.

On Categories of Offering

Beyond the sacrificial meat, ancient Hellenic worship recognised several further categories of offering, each with its own conventions and its own place within the ritual life of the tradition.

First Fruits

The offering of first fruits consisted traditionally of the first harvest of a crop, presented to the God associated with the increase. The category was also broadened to include other foods, flowers, wreaths, money, locks of hair, and models of perishable items rendered in a durable material so that the offering could remain permanently within the sanctuary. Incense, in particular the granules of frankincense strewn upon the altar flame, was among the most common offerings across every era.

Votive Offerings

A votive offering is one made in fulfilment of a vow, though the term also extends to any devotional gift. Surviving examples include statuettes of the Gods and of animals, tripods, vases, rings, clothes, armour, masks, oil flasks, cups, and reliefs. Almost any object of value or personal significance could serve as a votive, and unusual objects were themselves considered fitting: a striking stone or shell recovered from the shore, a snakeskin, a piece of wood shaped by nature into a suggestive form.

Offerings Tied to Particular Rites

Certain offerings belonged to a single God or to a single festival. The thargelos, a stew of harvested vegetables, was offered to Apollo at the Thargelia. Similar mixed preparations of grains, legumes, and pulses were widely used and were collectively called panspermia, meaning all-grains. A comparable dish was offered to Hermes at the close of the Anthesteria. The Greek Orthodox tradition preserves a direct descendant of the panspermia in the kollyva, prepared in commemoration of the dead and the ancestors.

The eiresione, a branch of olive, or in some regions of laurel, hung with fillets of wool, ribbons, and small objects, originated as the mark of a supplicant. It later took a formal place within the Puanepsia festival, where it was carried in procession by children.

The Measure of Devotion

An anecdote preserved from antiquity illustrates the ancient understanding of what makes an offering valued. A wealthy worshipper who had made many costly and lavish offerings to Apollo enquired of the Pythia at Delphi as to who among mortals was the truest worshipper of the Gods. The Pythia answered that this honour belonged to Klearchos of Methydrion in Arcadia, a modest man who garlanded his household statues each month and made simple offerings at every festival. Consistency of devotion and sincerity of intent were held to weigh more than expense.

On the Disposal of Offerings

In ancient practice, perishable offerings that were not consumed within the rite itself were either burned upon the altar or buried in the earth of the sanctuary. Permanent offerings remained in place, gathered around the shrine. Over time, the humbler of these accumulated in such number that they were collected together and buried in ground close to the sanctuary. Broken metallic offerings, and those of gold in particular, were melted down and reshaped into new votives dedicated afresh to the same God, so that the material of the original offering remained continuously in devotional service.

The Phrase Hekas Hekas Este Bebeloi

The Greek phrase ἑκάς, ἑκάς, ἔστε βέβηλοι, transliterated hekas, hekas, este bebeloi, meaning depart, depart, all ye profane, appears in some contemporary Hellenic rites at the opening of ceremony. The phrase originated within the Eleusinian Mysteries, where it functioned as a directive to any person carrying miasma to withdraw from the ceremony before it began. The address was directed to living persons in a state of impurity, not to spirits or other beings, and does not derive from general Hellenic sacrificial practice.

On the Rites of Passage

The transitional rituals of the ancient household, birth, marriage, and death, were family affairs rather than priestly ones, carried out in and around the home. The wider framing of these three stages sits under The Main Household Rites above. What follows is the procedural detail preserved from ancient practice.

Amphidromia

The primary rite associated with a new baby was called the amphidromia. The child was officially welcomed into the family by being carried around the central hearth. It usually took place on the fifth or seventh day after birth. Gifts were sometimes sent by relatives, though generally only those present at the birth attended the ceremony itself. The doorway of the house might be decorated to mark the occasion: an olive wreath for a boy, and wool for a girl.

Marriage in Three Parts

An ancient marriage consisted of three parts.

  • The engue, or betrothal, a verbal contract between the fathers of the bride and groom.
  • The ekdosis, which began what is now called the wedding itself. Sacrifices and offerings were made, followed by a nuptial bath for both bride and groom. The bride then adorned herself and attended a feast at her family's home. After the feast, she was carried by chariot in a torchlit procession to her new home, accompanied by music and by relatives bearing gifts.
  • The gamos, the physical consummation of the marriage within the new household.

Funeral Rites

Funerals were often elaborate affairs, lasting many days and held at considerable expense.

On the day after death, the body was washed, anointed, wrapped in a shroud, and crowned with garlands. It was laid out on a bier in the house for a whole day. This was called the prothesis, the laying out.

On the third day after death, the body was brought in procession to the cemetery. This was called the ekphora, the carrying out. The procession included women who cried, sang dirges, and gave open displays of grief. At the cemetery the body was interred, libations poured, and offerings placed in the grave.

The family then returned home, purified themselves, and shared a funeral feast called the perideipnon, the supper beside, so named because in earlier times it had been held at the graveside itself. On the third day after the funeral, food offerings were left at the grave, and again on the ninth day, which commonly marked the end of the mourning period.

Because of the miasma associated with death, no priest could take part in any funeral rite. Even the Gods themselves were said to withdraw from the presence of the dying and the dead. Apollo and the other Olympians are described in the poetic sources as pulling back at the approach of death to avoid its pollution.

On Continued Rites for the Ancestors

After the funerary rites laid out under On the Rites of Passage above, the deceased relative is honoured in the same manner as other chthonic beings. Food offerings and libations are left at the gravesite. Beyond the day of the funeral itself, the tradition preserves several specific dates on which such offerings are given.

  • The third day after the funeral.
  • The ninth day after the funeral, which commonly marked the end of the mourning period.
  • The thirtieth day after the funeral in some regions.
  • The birthday of the deceased.
  • The anniversary of the death.
  • Community festivals honouring the dead, chief among them the ancient Athenian Genesia.

In addition to food and drink poured or placed on the ground, ribbons and flowers may be left at the tomb.

The Ancestral Office

In ancient practice, the oldest son of the family carried the primary responsibility for the offerings owed to the ancestors, though every member of the household could take part in the rites.

The Ancestors and Fertility

The ancestors were held to bring fertility of every kind. Offerings and prayers were made to them for good crops, for the increase of flocks, and for fertile wombs. At the same time the dead were feared, and it was customary to pass by graves in silence so as not to attract the notice of the soul within. Both the caution of the silent grave-passing and the regularity of rites performed at the tombs themselves indicate that at least some portion of the psyche was believed to remain at the burial site.

The Cenotaph

When a person died away from home and the body could not be recovered for proper burial, the soul had to be summoned back to an empty tomb, called a cenotaph. The absent person was represented at the site by a stone. Cenotaphs were tended by relatives in the same manner as ordinary graves.

On Miasma in Sanctuaries

Alongside the ordinary cleansing rites laid out under On Purification, a body of ancient custom governed what could and could not be brought into a sanctuary or holy place.

Death stood chief among the forbidden states. Childbirth was likewise excluded, and in most cases sexual activity as well. At the entrance to a sanctuary, a vessel filled with pure water was set so that worshippers could wash their hands and clear themselves of any incidental miasma before crossing the threshold. This ancient sanctuary vessel is the direct ancestor of the periranteria used outside temple entrances and, at the household scale, of the khernips.

Modern practitioners rarely observe these sanctuary bans to their ancient extent. Most still perform some form of ritual cleansing before formal worship, whether the simple hand-wash of the khernips or the fuller ceremony described in the Hellenic Purification Ceremony.

On Oaths

Oaths were far more common in ancient life than they are today, and in the ancient understanding they were a religious matter through and through. Modern equivalents, such as the custom of swearing on a sacred text in a courtroom, preserve a distant echo of the same practice.

To take an oath in the ancient manner was to swear by the Gods, to make libations, and often to sacrifice an animal. This was not a small act. The full weight of the divine was invoked as witness, and the ritual pour that sealed the oath was never drunk. Once the entire vessel had been emptied, the vessel itself was shattered, and that act sealed the unbreakable vow. See On Libations and Khoếs above for the ritual mechanics of the oath libation.

On the Mixing of Wine

Ancient Greek custom held that wine should be diluted with water, both in ritual and in ordinary drinking. The mixture was typically one part wine to two parts water, combined in a large krater, the mixing bowl, before being portioned out into cups. Drinking wine unmixed was considered barbaric.

Various theories have been offered as to why the Greeks did this. Some suppose that ancient wine was much stronger than modern wine, or that it was routinely laced with other drugs such as belladonna. Neither is likely. Wine cannot naturally reach an alcohol level near the dangerous range, and no direct textual evidence supports the idea of routine drug-lacing. The more grounded explanation is a matter of decorum. Rituals and shared gatherings, especially the symposia that ran for many hours of steady drinking, called for a measured pace, and the dilution slowed the progress of inebriation.

On Music, Dance, and Theatre

Music and dance held an important place in many ancient rites, though they appear less often in modern household practice, largely for want of numerous available musicians and dancers.

A sacrificial procession in ancient Greece typically included several musicians playing the aulos, the double pipe, and the lyre. Dances often accompanied the songs, ranging from simple rhythmical steps to more structured pieces set to particular hymns. Certain songs belonged to particular Gods: the dithyramb for Dionysus, and the paian for Apollo. Songs and dances also marked the harvest and the making of wine. Some ecstatic cults were especially known for their bands of frenzied dancers and musicians, among them the followers of Dionysus and of Kybele.

A modern practitioner celebrating alone can still play music and perform simple dances during a rite. Doing so is one of the more direct bridges between ancient and modern devotional practice.

Theatre itself was a religious matter. Legend traces its invention to a man named Thespis, who stepped forward from the usual chorus of boys singing and dancing and added dialogue to the performance. Because Dionysus was the God of theatre, the dramatic form remained a devotional act. Most of the plays that survive to the present, including the Theban cycle of Sophokles, were originally entries in the dramatic contests at the Greater Dionysia in Athens and at other large festivals. Attending a live performance of an ancient Greek play, when a local troupe stages one, can serve as part of celebrating the festivals of Dionysus.

On Oracles

Oracles were a central institution of ancient Greek religion. The most famous, and the only truly pan-Hellenic oracle, was that of Apollon at Delphi, where the aged priestess called the Pythia entered a trance and delivered the God's word to those who had first completed the proper preliminary rites. Major decisions by individuals and by entire cities were made only after consultation with the Pythia.

Other oracles were scattered across the Greek world. In each, a different God spoke to worshippers through a priest or priestess, or, in some cases, through a natural sign. At Dodona, Zeus was heard in the rustling of oak leaves. No established oracle exists today for Hellenic practitioners to consult, though members of the wider Hellenic community are working to revive the practice of prophecy, and in time new oracles may take shape.

On the Modes of Divination

Divination stood among the primary forms by which the ancient Greeks addressed the Gods and received their reply. It counted, in the mythic ordering, as one of the technai, the arts and skills given to humanity by Prometheus, and was placed second only to medicine among them. In the ancient conception, divination was not chiefly fortune-telling. It served as insight into the present shape of a situation, into the movement of fate, and into what the Gods wished the practitioner to know. It was to be approached with offerings, prayers, and an open mind, in the same devotional posture as any other rite.

Haruspicy

The oldest form of divination was tied directly to sacrifice. When the sacrificial animal was cut open, certain of its organs, most often the liver, were examined for divinatory markings. The reading of these organs is called haruspicy, and the practice carried its own detailed method within antiquity.

Ornithomancy

Ornithomancy is divination by the movement and calling of birds. The basic principles that survive are as follows: a bird approaching from the west, or the left, is read as a negative or unfavourable sign, and a bird from the east, or the right, is read as positive or favourable. Birds of prey carry greater significance than other birds. Anything unusual or noticeable is a sign, including the speed of flight, the calls the bird makes, and sudden shifts in direction. Ornithomancy admits a wide field of interpretation and rests strongly on the reader's attention.

Kleromancy

Kleromancy, divination by lots, was among the simplest and most widespread forms of ancient Hellenic divination. Small objects, most often knucklebones or stones, were cast upon the ground, and the resulting pattern was interpreted for its answer. The method admits many variants, and any consistent system of meanings applied to the fall of the lots may work, so long as the system is held to steadily.

The Homeric Oracle

The Homeric Oracle survives within the Greek Magical Papyri, a body of magical and religious documents produced chiefly in Hellenistic Egypt where Greek and Egyptian traditions blended. Three dice are cast, and the three-number combination that results is looked up against a table that assigns a particular line from either the Iliad or the Odyssey. The named line is the oracle. In its underlying form, this is a variety of bibliomancy, divination by opening a book at random, and may be performed with either epic by opening the text at random and reading the line touched.

The Limyran Oracle

The Limyran Oracle consists of twenty-four sentences, each tied to a letter of the Greek alphabet. A set of stones or potsherds is inscribed with the letters, and one is drawn from among them. The sentence corresponding to the drawn letter is the oracle.

Oneiromancy and Dream Incubation

Oneiromancy is divination through dreams. Ancient practice recognised both universal symbols and personal ones, and the interpretation of a dream drew on the dreamer's own life as much as on any general lore. A particular form of oneiromancy called dream incubation was widely practised for the seeking of healing. The suppliant travelled to a sanctuary, performed the rite and made the appropriate offerings, and then slept in a designated place within the precinct while awaiting a dream in which the God would either heal them directly or give guidance toward a cure. The most famous such site was the sanctuary of Asklepios at Epidauros. A cured suppliant left a thanks-offering, often in the form of a small plaque or votive depicting the body part that had been healed.

Direct Inspiration

A further class of divination stands apart from all the deductive forms. This is direct inspiration, in which a mantis, a prophet, receives the word of a God without the mediation of tools. Such divination rests on the direct contact between mortal and divine, and it depends on a working relationship developed over time between the mantis and the God who speaks through them. The tradition of the Pythia at Delphi belongs to this category. Direct inspiration is not undertaken lightly, nor is it common.

Confirmatory Omens

The practitioner performing a divination is enjoined to guard against bias in the reading. A safeguard practised in antiquity is the watching for confirmatory omens in the days that follow a divination. Rather than repeating the divination on the same question, the practitioner watches for further signs that either confirm or unsettle the first reading. A divination advising a fresh course of offerings to Poseidon, for example, meets its confirming omen when the practitioner encounters, the next day, a sailor met by chance for the first time.

On Omens

Ancient Hellenic religion held the everyday world open to divine communication. Alongside the formal modes of divination laid out above, omens formed a broader body of signs by which the Gods were understood to make Their presence known or to reply to a worshipper's petition. Recognition of a sign as a sign belonged to the interpretive act itself. A chance occurrence became an omen when the circumstances of the worshipper called for one, and the meaning of the sign came clear once the recognition had taken place.

Events with an ordinary physical cause could still carry divine intent. A lightning storm arriving after a petition to Zeus was taken as an omen without implying that the storm existed only for the petitioner. Divine attention was held to arrange the timing or the nearness of an event, and might even move the petitioner to ask for a sign in advance of the very event that would supply it.

Body Signs

The involuntary motions of the body, sneezes, twitches, and small tinglings, were counted among the potential omens of ancient practice. Interpretation followed the natural associations of the part affected. A tingling of the hand was read as money coming, and something involving the ear portended news to be heard. Where no specific association applied, such motions were understood simply as a call to attention or as confirmation of something just spoken.

The Kledon

The kledon is an omen taken from a snatch of overheard speech. A single word or a stray phrase that answered a question the hearer had been carrying became a sign in that moment. Ancient practice formalised the kledon into a mode of divination sacred to Hermes. The querent went to a busy marketplace, lit a lamp at a statue of Hermes, left a coin at the base, presented the question, and then walked into the crowd with the ears covered. Once in the middle of the crowd, the querent uncovered the ears and took the first words heard as the answer of the God.

Enodioi Sumboloi

The enodioi sumboloi, meaning signs upon the road, were chance meetings encountered while travelling. Travel by foot in the ancient world was strenuous and often dangerous, and portents were watched for along the way. An encounter with another person could become meaningful through appearance, occupation, chance word, or bearing. These road-signs belonged to Hermes in His office as the God of travellers and boundaries.

The Oikoskopikon

The oikoskopikon is an omen received within the home, an incident of domestic life carrying import for the person involved. The category is preserved by name in the ancient sources with little further procedural detail.

On Values and Eusebeia

Among the enduring legacies of the oracle at Delphi is a body of principles called the Delphic Maxims. These were inscribed at the temple for all visitors to see, and they served as a guide to a proper way of life. A partial selection:

  • Know thyself.
  • Nothing too much.
  • Worship the Gods.
  • Obey the law.
  • Respect your parents.
  • Control yourself.
  • Help your friends.

The list runs on for many more entries. Alongside such particular counsels, ancient culture held a set of larger values, and the central one was eusebeia, piety. Piety in this sense was defined as knowledge of sacrificing and praying, and once again the emphasis fell on what is done rather than on what is believed. Modern practitioners still hold eusebeia among the highest characteristics to cultivate. This aligns with the broader orientation toward Arête and Eudaimonia laid out above.

On Non-Mainstream and Foreign Cults

Alongside the conventional forms of the religion, ancient Greek culture also held space for non-mainstream cults that diverged from the norm in belief and practice. These often began as small groups and grew, over time, into full movements.

Orphism was one such cult. The Orphics kept their own cosmology and took Dionysus as their foremost God, though their conception of him differed significantly from the standard form. They were also vegetarians, which set them outside the mainstream religion at once, since they could not take part in the customary animal sacrifice. The Pythagoreans, also vegetarians, followed the philosophy of Pythagoras and held to a doctrine of reincarnation not shared by most Greeks. Both examples show that a range of cults with varying beliefs and practices can legitimately sit under the umbrella of Hellenismos.

The inclusion of foreign Gods within Hellenic worship also has ancient precedent. On numerous occasions a deity from another culture was brought into Greek religion and made part of it. Kybele originated in Asia Minor and gathered a substantial following within Greece. In the Hellenistic period, many Gods from Egypt found Greek adherents, foremost among them the great Goddess Isis, whose worship eventually spread across the Roman Empire and drew devotees from many peoples.

This is not a licence for the loose eclecticism sometimes seen in the wider neo-pagan community, where pantheons and traditions are mixed and matched according to preference. There are, however, proper ways to include one or two Gods from other pantheons within Hellenic polytheist practice without compromising its integrity. The condition is that the newcomer's original system be studied seriously, so the practitioner understands what that God carries and how the God may fit alongside the Hellenic order.

The Roman Question

The foreign pantheon most closely tied to Hellenic religion is the Roman one. When Rome conquered Greece, it took back much of Greek culture, including the religion, and adjusted its own Gods to fit the Greek pattern. Jupiter became another Zeus, Minerva another Athena. Older pre-Roman Gods were shaped to the Greek mould, so that Faunus became Pan. As a result, many now assume that the Greek and Roman religions are interchangeable, but the two differ in practice, outlook, and priorities. Religio Romana, the revival of ancient Roman polytheism, is today its own independent religion, with its own beliefs and rites. Some practitioners walk both paths, though most keep to one or the other. Where Greek and Roman Gods appear in the same breath in the wider community, keep in mind that each is understood differently within its own tradition.

On Patriarchy and The Twelve

Two related misconceptions about Greek religion are worth addressing head-on.

On Patriarchy

The first is that Greek religion, and by extension the Greek pantheon, was patriarchal. It is true that ancient Greek culture, like nearly every culture of that era, was patriarchal, and that women were not treated as equals to men. The revealing fact is that the one realm in which women could hold recognised standing and play an important role was religion. Priestesses were as common as priests, just as Goddesses were as common as Gods. Even in daily life, women took part in sacrifices, visited temples, and left offerings at their hearths. Within cult practice and myth alike, the Goddesses and female divinities were respected, feared, and loved every bit as fully as their male counterparts.

The image of the Greek pantheon as a family ruled by the father figure of Zeus is largely a nineteenth-century invention, popularised by mythographers such as Thomas Bulfinch. The Gods were indeed all related and formed something like a family, but the arrangement was never as tidy as later retellings suggest, especially given the many mythic variants that record different genealogies for the same Gods.

On The Twelve Olympians

The second is that the religion revolved around the twelve Olympians. Modern Hellenismos still often refers to the twelve, and there is some historical basis for the grouping. An altar to The Twelve Gods stood in the Agora at Athens, for instance. This does not mean, however, that there were only twelve Gods, or only twelve major Gods, or that every ancient Greek would have named the same twelve Olympians if asked. Which Gods counted as the Olympians varied by region, and the question does not appear to have mattered much to the ancients in daily practice. A worshipper honoured the Gods they were drawn to or had need of, whether or not those Gods belonged to the twelve. Many extremely important and well-loved Gods were never counted as Olympians, Persephone among them.

In some versions, Dionysus is listed as one of the Olympians, and he even brings his dead mother to Olympos and deifies her there. In other versions he is left out, and Hestia is counted among the twelve. The popular story that Hestia stepped aside from Mount Olympos to make room for Dionysos appears to have been invented by the writer Robert Graves. It appears in his work, but in none of the sources he cites, and in no ancient text yet known.

The Gods as Persons and Their Epithets

The Hellenic Gods are persons, not abstractions, ideas, or functions. To render a God as "the goddess of X" flattens what the tradition holds to be a many-sided being with a proper history, distinct personality, and full presence in the world. The ancient formulation is that the God acts, rather than that the God embodies a category. Zeus does not represent the storm; Zeus thunders. Aphrodite does not represent desire; Aphrodite bestows Her gifts.

Divine Epithets

Each God carries many epithets, cult titles that name a particular aspect, trait, history, or seat of the God. The epithet does not exhaust the God, and it may reveal a face that the popular functional summary would not lead the worshipper to expect. Aphrodite, commonly summarised as the Goddess of love, carries an epithet meaning bearing weapons. Zeus appears as Horkios, of oaths, as Lukaios, of the wolf, and as Meilikhios, the gentle.

Ancient practitioners themselves were not always in agreement over whether these epithets designate different aspects of a single God or distinct beings sharing a name. Both readings survived across the period. The tradition permits the question to remain open.

The Limits of Divine Attribute

The Hellenic Gods are not held to be omnipotent, omnipresent, or omniscient in the sense monotheistic theology assigns to a single God. The mythic record confirms this at every turn. Distinct beings hold conflicting opinions and wishes, take opposing sides in wars, and vie for the same object, as when three Goddesses contest the golden apple in the Judgement of Paris. Prayers and hymns customarily open with a call for the God to come to the worshipper from wherever the God presently is, a form incompatible with strict omnipresence. Even Apollo at Delphi is said to convey through the Pythia the wisdom of His father Zeus, and Zeus in turn is bound by the weaving of the Fates.

Beyond the Olympians

The divine world of the ancient Greeks extended far beyond the twelve Olympians. It was populated by theoi (Gods), daimones (spirits), heroes, ancestors, nymphs, and other beings, each with their own place in the tradition and their own forms of cult.

Gods Beyond the Olympians

Among the most widely worshipped Gods beyond the standard Twelve are Hekate, Asklepios, Helios, Selene, Pan, and Eros.

Groupings of Gods

Hellenic religion is rich in divine groupings, most often of three or a multiple of three.

  • The three Moirai, or Fates: Klotho, Lakhesis, and Atropos.
  • The three Horai, or Seasons.
  • The three, sometimes four, Eumenides, or Kindly Ones, also known as the Furies. These were more strictly avoided than actively worshipped.
  • The nine Mousai, or Muses: Kalliope, Kleio, Euterpe, Terpsikhora, Erato, Melpomene, Thaleia, Poluhumnia, and Ourania, each with their own artistic domain.
  • The Kabeiroi and the Kouretes, groupings of male Gods with their own regional cults.

The full list is far longer. Only a portion of these groups received active cult in any given period, and this was true in antiquity as much as in later practice.

On the Daimones and the Agathos Daimon

The daimones form a broad category of spiritual beings within Hellenic religion, standing between the Gods and mortals. A daimon is ordinarily less powerful and less far-reaching than a God, though the terms daimon and theos are at times used interchangeably in the ancient sources. The word was originally neutral and could name a beneficent or a malevolent spirit alike. Only across later centuries did daimon acquire an entirely negative sense, from which the modern English word demon descends.

The daimones were understood as intermediaries between the divine and the human. Plato in the Symposium describes them as interpreters and ferrymen, carrying communication between the two orders. A relationship with a daimon could therefore be more intimate and more accessible than a direct relationship with a God, and it could also serve as a bridge toward one.

The Agathos Daimon

Among the daimones, one of the most important within Hellenic religion is the Agathos Daimon, also written agathodaimon, meaning the Good Spirit. The Agathos Daimon is the spirit of fortune and good luck. It may belong to and protect an entire household, and in that capacity received libations of wine poured out after meals. It could be seen about the house in the form of a snake.

Originally an androgynous being, in the Hellenistic period the Agathos Daimon became decidedly male in depiction, and was even named as the consort of Agathe Tukhe, the Goddess of Fortune. He was portrayed at that time as a young man holding a cornucopia. The serpentine aspect was nonetheless preserved: when a great snake appeared to Alexander at the future site of Alexandria and was killed, Alexander raised a hero shrine for it as the Agathos Daimon.

Public and Private Cult

The cult of the Agathos Daimon is more individual in shape than the cult of the Gods or the heroes. No large festivals or surviving hymns are known for this spirit. In Boiotia the opening of new wine jars was dedicated to the Agathos Daimon, which stands as the sole public festival reference. In the ancient Athenian lunar calendar, the second day of every month was set aside for the Agathos Daimon, and appears to have been kept as a private household or personal observance.

The Personal Agathos Daimon

The Agathos Daimon also belongs to the individual, attached to a person at birth to protect and guide them across the length of their life. It bears on the person's fortune. Zeus Himself was said, in Pindar, to steer the daimon of those He loved. The philosopher Sokrates spoke of his own daimon as the voice that told him when to stop or to keep silent. The personal Agathos Daimon expected regular attention, and the wise practitioner cultivated the relationship in proportion to their means.

On the Chthonic Gods and Their Rites

The khthonioi, meaning of the underworld, form an important body of divinities within Hellenic religion. They include Hades, Persephone, and several further chthonic divinities, along with the souls of the dead. Under certain aspects, other Gods take on chthonic character as well: Hekate in one of Her principal offices, and Hermes under the epithet Psykhopompos, the guide of souls.

Hades Himself received little cult in either ancient or later practice. He was dreaded, feared, and respected more than He was courted with offerings. Persephone stood at the centre of the largest Mystery rites of the ancient world, those at Eleusis, and though She is Queen of the Underworld only for a portion of the year, that aspect appears to have drawn the greatest devotion.

Ritual Differences

Rites offered to the chthonic Gods differ from those offered to the Ouranic, or heavenly, Gods along several distinct lines.

  • Black animals were offered rather than white.
  • Rites were held primarily at night rather than by day.
  • Sacrifices were made into a bothros, a pit dug into the earth, rather than upon a raised bomos.
  • Libations took the form of the khoê, poured out in entirety, rather than the shared sponde.
  • Prayer was offered with the hands and arms lowered toward the earth rather than raised to the sky.
  • The holy places of the chthonic Gods were caves and other underground settings rather than temples above ground.

On the Nymphs

The nymphs, the divinities of the natural features of the landscape, held a large and continuous place in ancient Hellenic religion. In the rural areas their cult often took precedence over the cult of the Olympians, since a shepherd or a farmer met the nymphs in daily life among the trees, the pastures, the springs, and the caves. Greek pastoral poetry records shepherds encountering nymphs and at times falling in love with them across the long hours spent with their flocks on the mountains.

Names and Types

The word nymph, numphe in Greek, means bride, though the nymphs are rarely portrayed as married. They are always female. Their male counterparts are the satyroi, the silens, and the kentaurs. The nymphs are long-lived but not immortal.

  • Dryads are nymphs of the trees, most closely of oaks, and are so bound to their tree that they are born and die with it.
  • Oreads are nymphs of the mountains.
  • Naiads belong to springs and to bodies of fresh water.
  • Nereids are nymphs of the ocean.
  • Limnades dwell in lakes, marshes, and swamps.
  • Epimeliades protect the flocks of sheep.
  • Leimoniades inhabit flowering meadows.

Many further types are named in the sources.

Named Nymphs in Myth

Individual nymphs appear across the mythic record. Thetis, a Nereid, is the mother of Akhilles. Echo flees from Pan. Daphne is pursued by Apollo and becomes His sacred laurel. Maia is the mother of Hermes.

Divine Companions

In myth, the nymphs often appear in the company of, or pursued by, the rural and pastoral Gods: Pan, Hermes, Apollo, and Dionysus. Although the mythic frame casts these encounters as hostile or unwelcome, in cult the same Gods were honoured beside the nymphs without any note of animosity. The Korykian Cave on Mount Parnassos above Delphi belonged jointly to the nymphs, to Pan, to Hermes, and possibly to Dionysus. The nymphs there appear in the Homeric Hymn to Hermes in the form of bees, and the association of the nymphs with bees, and with honey as a proper gift for them, is recorded across many sites.

Healing and Prophecy

The nymphs were widely credited with powers of healing, often carried in the waters they guarded, and with prophetic gift. Rites for the nymphs frequently included simple divination. Thousands of astragaloi, sheep knucklebones used for casting lots, have been recovered from the Korykian Cave and from many other nymph caves across Greece.

Nympholepsy

A mortal touched by the prophetic gift of the nymphs was called a nympholept. The term carries several senses. It names a heightened awareness and increase in verbal skill through which a person becomes a poet. It also names a possession understood as illness, and in some sources a physical rapture in which the person is taken away by the nymphs. In its most enduring sense it describes a mortal exceptionally devoted to the nymphs in the religious sense, one who kept a sanctuary for them and delivered prophecy under their inspiration. Nympholepts lived at the edges of ordinary society yet often founded and tended sanctuaries visited by pilgrims. Some passed entire lives within the caves sacred to their nymphs, dwelling there as living guardians of the shrine. A nympholept sometimes held a particularly close bond with one nymph, understood at times as romantic or sexual. The inscriptions left by these caretakers preserve the depth of the bond: in third-century BCE Cyprus, one Onesagoras filled a cave with dedications to a single nymph, addressing her across the inscriptions as sister, as daughter, and, in some, as lover.

Continuous Cult

The cult of the nymphs survived the conversion of Greece to Christianity and continued in rural areas long afterward. In modern rural Greek folk practice all nymphs are called nereids, though the tales told of them and the offerings made to them have remained substantially unchanged. The worship of local nature spirits is often described as the most persistent of all forms of Greek religion.

Offerings

Offerings suited to the nymphs include libations, astragaloi, honey, jewellery, shells, and small votive female figurines. Some sources hold that libations to the nymphs should be wineless, while others record wine offered to nymphs in particular regions. Small shrines set within the natural landscape belong equally to their cult.

Theoxenia

A theoxenia is a feast held in honour of a God or group of Gods, to which the divine guest is formally invited and served. Tables are laid out generously, food and drink are set out with a place kept for the God, and the God's presence is understood to attend the meal. A theoxenia held for the nymphs is attested in ancient practice: at Mytilene, the Theodaisia was celebrated for Dionysus together with the nymphs. Banquets more generally were counted among the offerings suited to their cult.

On River Gods and the Anemoi

Alongside the nymphs, a further body of divinities is tied directly to the natural features of the landscape and to the movements of the sky. The river Gods and the Anemoi belong to this stratum.

The River Gods

Each river was held to have its own God, distinct from the nymphs of its waters. River Gods were depicted as bulls with human heads, or as men bearing bull's horns, and they were held to be the fathers of the river nymphs, sometimes called potamiads or okeanids. The river God governed the flow of the river itself, its rising and falling and its bearing on the land through which it passed.

The Anemoi

The winds were understood as divine beings called the Anemoi. The most widely named are Boreas, the north wind, Notos, the south wind, Apheliotes, the east wind, and Zephuros, the west wind. Some evidence indicates that the Anemoi received regular cult in antiquity, though little detail of their rites has survived.

Other Male Nature Spirits

The satyrs, silens, and kentaurs, named earlier as the male counterparts of the nymphs, appear across the mythic record but received little cult of their own. Where the mythic and cult attention among the male rural divinities gathers, it gathers upon Pan, worshipped widely in His homeland of Arkadia and across the wild places of the Greek world.

On Hero Cult

The heroes were figures, mortal or believed to have been mortal, who received cult after death as semi-divine beings. Hero cult belonged to the khthonioi, the chthonic side of Hellenic religion, and its rites resembled those given to the dead rather than those given to the Olympian Gods. Heroes received the same categories of food and libation as the ancestral dead. Their shrines were passed in silence.

A hero did not need to be of divine parentage. Extraordinary quality or deed sufficed. Cult was directly tied to the tomb of the hero, though the same hero was sometimes claimed by several places, each keeping its own tomb-shrine.

Heroines

Heroines were honoured alongside heroes, though the balance of the surviving record favours the male figures. Where heroines appear, they are often drawn from mythology and poetry, as with Helen and Ariadne, rather than from remembered history. In certain cases the distinction between heroine and Goddess is unclear. Ino Leukothea, for instance, was treated in some places as a heroine and in others as a Goddess outright.

Local and Pan-Hellenic Hero Cult

Hero cult was mostly a local affair. Each city and region kept its own heroes, and pan-Hellenic heroes were rare. Heracles stands as the primary example of a hero worshipped across the whole Greek world.

New Hero Cult

The tradition allows the recognition of new heroes. Where a family, cult association, or city passed the appropriate resolution, the honours of hero cult could be extended to the recently dead, and the new hero could receive the same forms of offering and shrine as the older heroes. Great Gods are no longer born, but new heroes may still be raised from the ranks of the dead through such formal recognition.

On Patron Gods

Alongside general devotion to the Gods, ancient Hellenic practice recognised the case of a worshipper drawn especially to one God, or to a small group of Gods, and forming a lasting personal bond with that deity. The relationship did not entail neglect of the other Gods, but it did concentrate the worshipper's attention, offerings, and prayers upon the chosen God to a greater degree than the general round of practice required.

Several ancient examples illustrate the shape of the bond.

  • The playwright Aiskhulos received a dream of Dionysus as a boy while asleep in a vineyard, in which the God commanded him to write tragedies. Aiskhulos spent the remainder of his life in service to the God of theatre, and the account is preserved by Pausanias.
  • In the Odyssey, Odysseus stands in an intimate bond with Athena. She counsels him, favours him above others, and receives his devotion in return.
  • The poet Sappho spoke of herself as a servant of Aphrodite, and the Goddess appears personally in her surviving verse.
  • Certain priests in antiquity volunteered for their office out of love for the God of the temple they served, and votive inscriptions surviving from that period record affection, gratitude, and continuing relationship between worshipper and God.

Personal and Professional Patronage

The tradition distinguishes between personal devotion of this kind and the broader category of professional or civic patronage, under which particular Gods presided over given trades, roles, or communities. A blacksmith might name Hephaestus as patron of the craft without holding the God as a personal patron in the fuller sense. Cities, tribes, and family lines likewise carried their own patronal Gods without the arrangement implying an intimate individual bond.

Kharis in the Patronal Bond

The kharis flowing between worshipper and patron God is personal and often intimate in character. For the fuller reference on this dimension, see Kharis in Patronal Devotion within the Kharis guide.

On Devotional Acts

Where a worshipper stands in a close bond with one or several Gods, particular devotional acts serve to deepen the relationship. Ancient practice preserves a wide range of such acts, and the tradition remains open to the composition of further acts along the same pattern.

Study and Composition

Thorough acquaintance with a God is itself a form of devotion. The variants of the myths, the surviving festivals and rites, the epithets and cult associations, and the recorded hymns and inscriptions are returned to across the years of practice, and each fresh reading tends to yield further insight.

Hymn, Image, and Shrine

A dedicated shrine, permanent or temporary, gives the working ground for personal devotion. Composition of a hymn, or the memorisation of an existing hymn for regular recitation, is a traditional gift. Artwork, image, and craft made in honour of a God carry the same devotional force. The greeting of a statue with the word khaire, meaning rejoice or hail, was customary among ancient practitioners when passing the image of a beloved God.

Dedicated Activities

Skills, crafts, and pursuits may be taken up as offerings to the God whose sphere they belong to. Devotion to Poseidon could find its expression in the labour of the sea, devotion to Artemis in archery or in the wild-country hunt. The Gods' own work in the world was also carried forward on Their behalf. Food was left at herms set up at crossroads, and hungry travellers passing through would take and eat it in the name of Hermes.

Theoxenia

The theoxenia, meaning the hosting of the God, is a feast held in honour of a God to which the deity is formally invited and served alongside the mortal guests. Food and drink are laid out with a place reserved for the God, and the God's presence is understood to attend the meal.

Deity-Specific Devotions

Certain devotions belong to particular Gods.

  • For Hekate, the deipnon, a supper laid out at Her statue or at a crossroads on the night of the dark moon at the close of each lunar month.
  • For Hermes, coins scattered at a crossroads and gifts of money offered to those in need, both understood as continuations of the herm custom.
  • For Dionysus, the pannukhia, an all-night revel.
  • For Apollo, the burning of laurel.
  • For Aphrodite, the presentation of roses.

Sustained Dedication

Deeper dedication may take the form of a devotional object worn continually, such as an owl pendant for Athena. A period of concentrated devotion, a week, a month, or a year given over to the God, allows the worshipper to observe every festival and holy day of that God with full attention. The taking of a religious name related to the God stands within ancient precedent, as does formal dedication as a devotee or priest of a particular temple.

Ritual Marriage to a God

On one night of the Athenian festival called the Anthesteria, the queen was ritually married to Dionysus. The consummation of the marriage was held privately and was kept secret from the wider community. No full text of the rite has come down from antiquity.

On Daily Practice

Alongside the household ceremonies and the great festivals, the tradition preserves a body of small daily acts by which contact with the Gods is kept continuous. These practices are personal in scale and undemanding in form, and they sustain the working relationship between worshipper and God across the ordinary round of the day.

Waking and Retiring

Extemporaneous prayer at the opening and closing of the day, addressed to the Gods held closest by the worshipper, keeps an ongoing exchange in place across the days of practice. Homer records libations poured before sleep. Hesiod records prayers made before the morning meal and again at sunset.

Silent Presence at the Shrine

A daily pause before the household shrine, without spoken words, sustains the sense of divine attention. The practice takes only a few moments and requires no ritual preparation.

The Small Libation

A few drops poured from every cup before the worshipper drinks extends the domestic libation practice across the ordinary meals of the day.

The Monthly Sacred Days

The lunar month carries its own devotional rhythm, with certain days belonging to particular Gods. On these days, brief observances keep the household in contact with the God whose day it is. The fuller listing sits under On the Festival Calendar above.

On Recognising the Gods in the World

The ancient philosopher Thales declared that all things are full of Gods (Greek: πάντα πλήρη θεῶν, panta plērē theōn). The saying, preserved by Aristotle, has stood across the centuries as one summary of Hellenic religious perception. Divine presence pervades the natural world and the ordinary scenes of human life, and a worshipper trained in attention can meet the Gods within the surroundings.

Drawn from Aristotle, De Anima I.5.

Cultivation of this awareness proceeds by concentrated observation. In any given place, the practitioner identifies the features associated with particular Gods. An oak is sacred to Zeus. A gate or boundary marker is sacred to Hermes. A dove is sacred to Aphrodite. A spring is sacred to the nymphs. The bow and the wooded margin are sacred to Artemis. Held over time, the practice moves from an exercise of knowledge to a sustained sense of the Gods as continuously present within the world.

On Divine Appearance and Recognition

Ancient Hellenic tradition preserves many accounts of Gods appearing directly to mortals, and the recorded appearances follow a recognisable set of patterns. The Gods take on the semblance of a familiar mortal, cloak themselves in mist or cloud, or assume the form of an animal.

Well-known appearances of this kind survive in the poetic and mythic record. Zeus comes to Danae in a shower of gold. Aphrodite approaches Anchises in the form of a mortal woman, revealing her true nature only afterwards. Apollo leads the Cretan priests to Delphi in the form of a dolphin. The Homeric epics describe the Olympians in disguise moving among the heroes throughout the action of the poems.

Recognition of the God within the encountered form was itself a mark of insight, and the Odyssey records Odysseus addressing Athena on the point.

It is difficult for a mortal encountering you to recognise you, goddess, for you are able to liken yourself to everything.

Drawn from Homer, Odyssey.

On Ekstasis

The Greek word ekstasis (ἔκστασις), meaning standing outside oneself, names the altered state of consciousness in which the ordinary boundaries between the mortal and the divine loosen. Within this state the presence of the Gods is felt more directly than in ordinary awareness, and with practice the worshipper may receive images, hear voices, speak prophecy, or be possessed for a time by the God addressed. Possession is not held to be bound by gender, and a female worshipper may be taken by a male God and a male worshipper by a female Goddess.

The Maenadic Cult

The most fully attested ancient example of ekstasis is the maenadic cult of Dionysus. The maenads, His female devotees, entered into trance through rhythmic music, sustained dance, and the consumption of wine, and their state carried them for a time out of the ordinary conditions of civic life.

The mythic and cultic record together credit the maenads with a body of extraordinary attributes: heightened bodily strength beyond ordinary human capacity, imperviousness to weapons and to fire, and the charming and wearing of snakes within the hair. The rite culminated at points in sparagmos, the tearing apart of a live animal, and omophagia, the consumption of its flesh raw, understood as a direct communion with the God through His sacred animal. Ancient authors sometimes divided the mythic maenads from historical practice, though the boundary between the two is not sharp in the surviving evidence.

Techniques

The ancient sources, taken together with the wider cross-cultural record, describe several routes by which ekstasis is attained.

  • Rhythmic chanting and drumming.
  • Sustained dance.
  • Fasting and other bodily disciplines.
  • Sensory adjustment, including darkening of the room, veiling of the face, and the burning of heavy incense to fill the air.
  • Intense prayer and concentrated focus.
  • Controlled use of wine or of sacred plants.

Entheogens

The Greek term entheogen, from en-theos-genes, meaning that which generates the God within, names a sacred plant taken to open the practitioner to divine encounter. Such plants were consumed within a ritual context, with respect for the plant and awareness of its effects, rather than casually or for recreation. Wine within the maenadic rite of Dionysus is the clearest Hellenic instance of the category.

The Character of the State

Aelius Aristides, who spent long periods within the healing sanctuary of Asklepios at Pergamon, described the quality of the encountered state.

It was a sort of sense of contact and a clear realisation that the God himself had come, an intermediate state between sleeping and waking, a desire to look and at the same time a fear that He would go away first, a listening and hearing half in dream and half awake, the hair on end and tears of joy and an inward swelling with delight. Those who are initiated will understand and recognise it.

Drawn from Aelius Aristides, Sacred Tales.

On Prophetic Trance

The prophetic mantis, laid out under Religious Roles above, moved between two distinct modes of the office. Some worked chiefly by interpreting omens and signs, and others received the message of the God through direct inspiration in a state of trance. The Pythia at Delphi, seated over the adyton and speaking on behalf of Apollo, is the best known figure of the latter form.

The Setting

The setting for a prophetic trance is prepared with the ordinary observances of ritual: fasting, purification by khernips, and prayer or hymn addressed to the God from whom the message is sought. Where the God addressed is Apollo, the burning of bay (laurel) leaves is customary, and the heavy smoke of the burning leaves fills the space of the rite. Darkening of the room, veiling of the face, and instrumental music without words are all used to loosen the ordinary current of thought.

The State Proper to Prophecy

The state proper to prophetic trance is neither sleep nor ordinary waking. Images, words, and impressions arise unbidden. The practitioner attends to them without gripping any one of them, and with time and practice the arising material coalesces into an intelligible answer. Discernment of a true divine response from the practitioner's own mental noise is the labour of years.

Relation to Mechanical Divination

The mantic art also includes the mechanical modes laid out under On the Modes of Divination above. Prophetic trance stands as the direct-inspiration mode of the same art, and the two are not held in opposition. Confirmatory omens are watched for after a prophetic reading in the same manner as after a mechanical one.

On the Wandering Mystics

Alongside the mystic cult figures rooted in place, ancient Hellenic tradition preserves a small body of individuals credited with extraordinary spiritual attainments and often associated with wandering, distant travel, and long solitary retreats. Their reported abilities include separation of the soul from the body during life, prophecy, memory of past incarnations, healing of sickness, appearance in two places at once, and extension of the ordinary human lifespan. Modern scholarship sometimes calls them the Greek shamans by comparison with mystic figures of other traditions, though the label itself is not ancient.

Aristeas of Prokonnesos

Aristeas of Prokonnesos, celebrated in an epic poem composed under his name, was said to have travelled to the fabled land of the Hyperboreans, to have taken the form of a raven, and to have spread the cult of Apollo across the places he passed through. Herodotus records the tradition surrounding him.

Epimenides of Knossos

Epimenides of Crete, who cleansed the city of Athens after the Kylonian Affair (laid out under On Purification above), was said to have slept for many years within a cave and to have subsisted upon food given to him by the nymphs. He served as a purificatory priest of great renown and reached, in some accounts, a lifespan far beyond the ordinary.

On Magic in Hellenic Religion

Magic held a widely acknowledged place in ancient Hellenic life, practised across every social class from the countryside to the city and threaded through the same fabric as the more formally recognised religious observances. Philosophical writers developed rarefied views of religion that sometimes stood apart from popular magical practice, but those views were not typical of the general population, and the surviving evidence, textual and archaeological alike, shows magic as ordinary within the broader Hellenic religious sphere rather than as a separate compartment.

Certain kinds of magical practice were open to any competent worshipper under the right conditions. Divination, laid out under On the Modes of Divination above, belongs to this category. Healing through dream incubation at the sanctuaries of Asklepios and other healing Gods likewise falls within it. Folk healing traditions used specific herbs, prepared amulets, and simple ritual acts to work on illness and misfortune. Further categories of magical practice, such as necromancy, are laid out below.

On Necromancy

Necromancy, from the Greek nekromanteia, is the ritual raising of the spirit of a deceased person in order to obtain information from that spirit or to secure its aid. The rite is not the literal restoration of the dead to bodily life; it is a temporary summoning of the shade to the place of the ritual.

Structure of the Rite

The structure of a necromantic rite draws upon the ordinary Hellenic ritual pattern of purification, libation, sacrifice, and prayer, with several adjustments proper to work with the khthonioi and the dead.

  • The rite takes place between sunset and dawn.
  • Offerings are made into a bothros, a pit dug into the earth, within which a fire is kindled.
  • Prayers are addressed to the khthonioi and to the shades of the dead themselves, and follow the downward-facing gestures of chthonic worship laid out under On Prayers and Hymns above.
  • A blood offering is often included. Blood was held to feed the shades of the dead and to grant them, for a brief interval, the memory and awareness needed to answer the practitioner. The Nekyia of the Odyssey preserves the pattern: the shades regain speech and recollection only after they have drunk from the blood Odysseus pours out.

The Kolossoi

Alongside the summoning of shades, the necromantic tradition also made use of kolossoi, small human-shaped figures fashioned in the likeness of a targeted person. Threats, curses, and physical manipulation of the kolossos were used to work upon the person represented. This coercive branch of the practice sits at a distance from the ordinary devotional rites and belongs, historically, to the harsher edge of magical activity within the tradition.

On the Mystery Cults

Alongside the household and civic forms of worship, ancient Hellenic religion preserved a distinct sphere of initiatory rites known collectively as the Mysteries. Initiates numbered in the thousands across the ancient world, yet the observances themselves were held under strict secrecy, and comparatively few procedural details have come down.

The Major Mysteries

Several Mystery cults are attested by name.

  • The Eleusinian Mysteries, held at Eleusis, dedicated to Demeter and Persephone, and the most famous and long-lasting of the ancient Mystery cults.
  • The Mysteries of Dionysus, held under several regional forms and closely tied to the maenadic cult laid out above.
  • The Mysteries of the Kabeiroi, celebrated at Samothrace and at Thebes.
  • Further Mystery cults are named in the ancient sources with little procedural detail preserved.

Aporrheton and Arrheton

Two Greek terms describe the secrecy proper to the Mysteries.

  • Aporrheton, meaning forbidden to be spoken of to the uninitiated.
  • Arrheton, meaning unspeakable, in the sense that the content cannot be adequately put into words at all.

The ancient sources apply both terms to the culminating revelation of the rite. The double naming reflects the twofold character of the silence: a discipline enforced upon the initiates, and a limit inherent in the material itself.

The Purpose of Initiation

The stated aim of initiation into most of the Mystery cults was the assurance of a blessed afterlife. The ordinary Hellenic understanding of the underworld held it to be a dismal place, and the souls within it to be mere shades of their living selves, without memory or continuing consciousness. Initiation was held to secure the initiate a passage beyond that fate, into the Elysian Fields, with memory and awareness preserved.

The Eleusinian Epiphany

The Eleusinian rite culminated in an epiphany. The nature of this epiphany was itself among the elements held under aporrheton and arrheton, and the surviving hints in the ancient sources vary: an appearance of Persephone Herself, the display of an ear of corn, and other elements are named across different accounts.

On the Character of the Rites

Aristotle preserved a summary of the peculiar character of the Mysteries as distinct from ordinary teaching.

The candidate did not have to learn, but to experience something, and to come to be in a certain state of mind.

Drawn from Aristotle, On Philosophy.

On Historical Facts

The information now available about ancient Greek religion is not perfect. Facts get garbled in transmission, and they are sometimes misinterpreted by parties with biases of their own. Secondary sources written more than half a century ago, or by authors carrying particular theoretical commitments, or by poets writing as scholars, should be double-checked before use. This does not mean discarding them. Many hold solid information and useful insights alongside material that is outdated, disproven, or better read as metaphor than as fact.

Archaeological discoveries can shift the picture too. A new find can overturn ideas held for a generation, and anyone attempting to reconstruct the ancient religion has to hold their conclusions with flexibility, an open mind, and a willingness to change practice when a good reason arises.

The Snake Goddesses of Crete

A vivid example is the controversy over the so-called Snake Goddesses of Crete. A great deal has been made of these few small statuettes, particularly in Goddess-worshipping circles. They are often cited as evidence of a matriarchal culture in ancient Crete, and they have become the basis for some modern religious practice. Evidence that has come to light in recent decades tells a more complicated story. Many of the statuettes were fabricated outright by nineteenth-century excavators. The genuine pieces were often altered in some way, or the circumstances and locations of their discovery were misreported.

Being Prepared to Revise

Everyone is free to believe as they choose. For practitioners committed to continuing the religions of the past, however, it matters to know the facts of those religions rather than only one's own ideas about them. Some of what feels settled today may yet be disproved, or the picture may shift as new evidence arrives. Every religion grows and evolves, and revision belongs to that natural process. What is required is the readiness to meet it.

Notes on Language

The ancient Greek language is an important element of Hellenic practice. Correct spelling and pronunciation of the Gods' names is understood as a mark of respect, just as any person prefers their own name spoken correctly.

Transliteration Conventions

This grimoire uses a transliteration of Greek letters into English that reflects a more accurate pronunciation than the older convention.

  • Υ / υ is rendered as u in place of the common y.
  • Χ / χ is rendered as kh in place of ch.
  • Κ / κ is rendered as k in place of c.
  • The digraphs ae and oe are replaced with ai and oi.
  • Latin us endings are replaced with the correct os.

By these conventions, Oedipus becomes Oidipos and Aeschylus becomes Aiskhulos. A few names retain their familiar English forms where the strict transliteration would cause confusion, such as Dionysos rather than Dionusos.

Greek Versus Roman Names

Strict Hellenic reconstruction uses the Greek forms of the Gods' names, not the Roman equivalents. Common pairs, Roman followed by Greek:

  • Apollo becomes Apollon
  • Hecate becomes Hekate
  • Asclepius becomes Asklepios
  • Hercules becomes Herakles

Pronunciation Notes

Ancient Greek pronunciation is a matter of ongoing scholarly discussion, and regional dialects produced many variations. The following rules describe the most commonly taught working pronunciation, closest to Attic Greek.

  • Iota (Ι / ι) is an ee sound.
  • Omicron (Ο / ο) is a short o, and Omega (Ω / ω) is a long o.
  • Epsilon (Ε / ε) is eh, and Eta (Η / η) is ay.
  • Gamma (Γ / γ) is always a hard g, as in the word Greek.
  • Ancient Greek has no h consonant. A leading h in transliteration marks an aspirated initial vowel.
  • Kh (Χ / χ) comes from the back of the throat, close to the ch in the Scottish word loch or the Hebrew Channukah.
  • Vowel diphthongs are voiced quickly together so the two sounds meld into one. Alpha-iota (ai), for instance, is a short a followed by ee, producing something like the word eye.
  • Ancient Greek has no silent letters. Every letter is voiced in some way. The English word psyche opens with an s sound, but in ancient Greek the same word opens with a p, producing psoo-khay.

Accent placement in Greek follows no single simple rule and is best consulted case by case.

Hellenic and Greek

This grimoire uses Hellenic and Greek interchangeably. Greek and Greece come from the ancient name of a single tribe, later applied to all Greeks. In the Greek language itself, the country is Hellas, from which the adjective Hellenic derives. Hellenistic is a distinct term. It refers specifically to the historical period from the death of Alexander the Great in 323 BCE to the Roman annexation of Egypt in 30 BCE, and it is not properly used as a general adjective.
