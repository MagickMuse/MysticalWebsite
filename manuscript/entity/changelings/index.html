Magickal Properties

Changeling technically names the fairy replacement left when fairies steal humans, but the word has come to include the entire phenomenon as well as the abducted human. This is among the most feared accusations hurled against fairies across many traditions as far afield as North Africa and the Middle East.

Trolls, nixies, Korrigans, and Sidhe are all accused of stealing children and leaving changelings, though Irish legend has a particularly extensive catalog of changeling lore.

Common Victims

Not everyone is equally vulnerable to being stolen. Common victims include:

Midwives and wet-nurses: Ostensibly stolen to serve fairy mothers. Once upon a time, midwives did more than deliver babies; they performed blessing rituals believed necessary for the baby's good health and fortune.

Handsome young men: Stolen to become lovers and/or prisoners of fairy queens, as in the folk ballad Tam Lin.

Babies and young children: Two types are at risk, particularly beautiful, vigorous children and absolutely ordinary, run-of-the-mill healthy children. Frail children are not at risk; however the changeling left in exchange is often frail, sickly, and wizened.

Fairy Motivations

One common explanation suggests that fairies, a dwindling, scarce race, believe humans are sturdier, healthier, and more prolific, and thus seek to incorporate human bloodlines into their communities to strengthen them. Small children and infants are easiest to integrate. The added implication is that fairies secretly observe human communities and individuals very closely.

The Hidden Story

It's possible that intensive attention to changelings masks Pagan spiritual, magickal, and healing traditions. "Changeling" is also the name given to people who voluntarily went to live with fairies, often eventually returning to their communities in a changed state. Their personalities are described as "changed" or "different," often distant. They traditionally return as masters of herbal and magickal knowledge.

Changelings serve as conduits between people and the fairy community. They also initiate and train fairy doctors, teaching them fairy healing techniques.

Methods of Reversing the Switch

Three traditional methods exist for getting the old person back and returning the changeling to the fairies:

1. Direct Exchange During Fairy Processions: Trooping fairies leave their fairy mounds and strongholds several times a year. A direct exchange may be made during this time, although specific magickal spells and rituals are required for success.

2. Nurturing the Changeling: The fairy changeling, often weak and frail, must be nurtured so that he or she transforms into a happy, healthy, vigorous child. Supposedly when this occurs, the fairies will prefer having their own child back and will affect the change themselves.

In general, one must be kind to the changeling if one ever wants to see one's own child again. The unspoken threat is that if the changeling is misused or abandoned, the fairies will inflict similar treatment (or worse) on the human child.

3. Fairy Exorcism: If fairies are perceived as demons then the "stolen victim" hasn't been kidnapped; instead they are possessed. Fairies are exorcized like demons. Exorcism rites often involve beating or torturing the possessed victim in the belief that when life becomes sufficiently unpleasant, the resident demon will voluntarily withdraw.

The danger is that these intensive exorcism rites may end up doing more damage (sometimes fatal) than the possession itself. Because sidhe, unlike zar, are allegedly afraid of fire, victims of fairy possession have been burned in efforts to make the fairies depart.

Fate of Unreturned Changelings

The fairy child abandoned to live with humans often grows up to be a sniveling, dullwitted person. No longer a child, he was no longer classified as a changeling but as an ouphe, the original "oaf."

The stolen human's fate varies: some reportedly yearn for their human life and lost friends and family. Others are reportedly happy among the fairies, living a life of joy, music, and dance.

Thomas the Rhymer

The most famous changeling was Thomas the Rhymer, a thirteenth-century Scottish prophet, a historically documented individual also known as Thomas of Erceldoune, subject of a famous ballad. Depending on the version, Thomas either kissed or made love to a Fairy Queen and was transported to Fairyland. After seven years, she either transported him back or he grew homesick and requested leave. The Fairy Queen offered him a choice of gifts: he could become a harper or a seer. Thomas chose the latter and returned as a master prophet.

Ritual Uses

Changelings who return from fairy realms serve as teachers and initiators of fairy doctors, passing on herbal and magickal knowledge learned in the fairy realm. They act as bridges between the human and fairy worlds.

Offerings

Kindness and nurturing to changeling children to encourage fairies to return human children, respect for those who have lived among fairies and returned with knowledge.

Warnings

Treat any changeling with kindness, the fate of the stolen human depends on how the changeling is treated. Never harm a changeling through exorcism rites, as this may doom the human child. Fairies observe human communities closely and will retaliate for mistreatment.

Modern Interpretations

Some modern readers interpret changeling narratives as folklore's attempt to explain developmental differences first noticed around the same age that changelings traditionally appear, including conditions resembling autism, or as an early name for the still poorly understood pattern of failure to thrive.
