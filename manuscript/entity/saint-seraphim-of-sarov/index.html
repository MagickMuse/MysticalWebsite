Orthodox Christian saint, perhaps the most beloved figure in the Russian Church and all Orthodox branches, renowned for miraculous healings and powers of exorcism.

Early Life and Miracles

Born Prokhor Moshnin on 19 July 1759, the future saint was the son of a builder who died when he was an infant. His mother took over the family business, but young Prokhor already displayed spiritual inclinations and miraculous abilities:

  • As a child, he saw angels and experienced his first vision of Mary at age ten
  • A serious childhood illness that defied medical treatment was cured when his mother appealed to a miraculous Marian icon
  • A fall from a high scaffold that should have killed or seriously injured him left not even a scratch. His mother said invisible angels had cushioned his fall. The local holy fool proclaimed the boy obviously blessed by God.

Monastic Life

At nineteen, Seraphim (the name he adopted upon taking monastic vows) left home to wander, study, and worship at various monasteries. He began his lifelong practice of the Jesus Prayer, constantly chanting: "Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, have mercy on me, a sinner." He was ordained a priest in 1793.

The Jesus Prayer, also known as the Prayer of the Heart, is believed to have originated among the early Christian desert hermits. The repetitive chant is synchronized to the practitioner's heartbeat, functioning as something like a Christian mantra.

The Brides of Christ Controversy

Seraphim served as religious advisor to female spiritual seekers and was surrounded by an entourage called the Brides of Christ. Rumors circulated about unconventional spiritual rites. He became the subject of a police investigation, though these allegations were never verified. His official hagiographies now downplay this period.

The Forest Hermit

Following the investigation, Seraphim abandoned civilization to become a forest hermit. He lived alone, praying, studying, and tending a small garden. Wild animals became his sole companions; he befriended them with gifts of food. A bear who sometimes lay at his feet was a constant visitor and appears frequently in his iconography.

Robbers nearly beat Seraphim to death, believing he had treasure hidden in his cabin. He walked bent over for the rest of his life from his injuries, yet he pleaded for mercy on behalf of his attackers in court. After recovering, he spent a thousand days and nights kneeling in prayer on a large rock in the forest with his arms outstretched.

In 1815, on the advice of Mary, Mother of God, he began admitting visitors to his hermitage. They came by the hundreds daily. Seraphim demonstrated clairvoyance, often answering questions before they were asked. He was acclaimed for his asceticism and gentleness.

The Exorcist

Seraphim gained special renown as an exorcist, a reputation that continues after death. He cast out demons and expelled evil spirits. The archives of the Sarovskala Uspenskala Hermitage contain testimony regarding posthumous exorcisms attributed to him. However, in nineteenth-century Russia, the days of demon-expelling holy men were officially considered over. The crowds loved Seraphim; the Church was embarrassed.

Prophecy and Canonization

Seraphim died in his cell on 2 January 1833, kneeling before an icon of Mary. Though considered a holy man (staretz) during his lifetime, the Church resisted calls for his official canonization, viewing exorcisms, clairvoyance, and miracles as backward superstition.

Seraphim had the last word. In 1879, he had foretold the names of the future Tsar Nicholas and his wife Alexandra, and predicted his canonization would occur during their reign. An admirer recorded these predictions when he made them, providing verification. (He simultaneously prophesied that "rivers of Russian blood" would flow during their reign.)

When Alexandra desperately desired a son after bearing four daughters, she learned of Seraphim's reputation for fertility miracles and his prophecy about her husband. The imperial family declared Seraphim their spiritual patron. Nicholas pushed for canonization, and in July 1902 the Holy Synod finally relented. Alexandra petitioned Seraphim for a son; Tsarevitch Alexis was born 30 July 1904.

Manifestation

Seraphim always wore white robes. He walks bent over from his injuries.

Iconography

Seraphim permitted devotees to make several portraits during his lifetime. The most famous, the Novo Diveyevo Icon, now resides in the Russian Orthodox Convent Novo Diveyevo in Nanuet, New York. He may appear in Russian peasant garb and is frequently depicted as a hermit feeding a bear.

Colors

White.

Sacred Site

Saint Seraphim-Diveyevo Monastery near Sarov, where his relics rest. A spring at his shrine is associated with miraculous healings.
