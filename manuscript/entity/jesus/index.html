The Magician Controversy

A controversial and long-suppressed tradition holds that Jesus was a magician. Count Cagliostro described him as "the first and greatest magician who ever lived," though the Church never took this as a compliment. During Jesus' lifetime, the Mediterranean world teemed with traveling magicians specializing in healing and exorcisms, and Jesus first gained fame as a miracle healer whose cures made him renowned.

The central tension of the Gospels addresses whether Jesus was divine or simply another magician. This question becomes difficult to answer because nearly all surviving information about Jesus comes from his devotees.

Lost Writings and Accusations

In 396 CE, Emperor Constantine ordered the destruction of "heretical" books, followed by successive decrees eliminating writings that contradicted official Christian teachings. What survives are fragments, lists of lost books, and official refutations that reveal early accusations of Jesus practicing magick.

Justin Martyr complained between 150 and 165 CE that Jews described Jesus as "a Galilean magician." Galilee held a reputation for retaining Pagan and unconventional Jewish traditions, lending credence to these characterizations.

The Platonist Celsus wrote a treatise attacking Christianity as conjury near the end of the second century. Though all copies were destroyed after Christianity's political triumph, the Christian Origen quoted extensively from it in his 247 CE refutation Against Celsus. Celsus claimed Jesus grew up in Galilee, traveled to Egypt as a laborer, and returned as a magickal practitioner.

Egyptian and Babylonian Training

The most common legend suggests Jesus learned magick in Egypt, though alternative theories place his training with master magicians in Babylonia. According to these traditions, Jesus performed miracles and resurrections through the magickal use of the Ineffable Name, the greatest of all spells. Some legends claim he bore magickal tattoos or symbols called "the Egyptian marks."

This theory proposes that Jesus' miracles resulted from controlling spirits, making him a forerunner of High Ceremonial Magick and Commanding and Compelling traditions. King Solomon similarly commanded spirits, and a powerful tradition of angelic magick already existed in Jewish practice. Jews were renowned throughout the Roman world for exorcism skills and spirit-summoning magick.

Simon Magus and Mandaean Traditions

Simon Magus was openly acknowledged as a great magician yet worshipped by some as a god, suggesting that perceiving Jesus as a magician did not necessarily deny his divinity for all early worshippers. Pope Gelasius (492 to 496 CE) listed works by Simon Magus among heretical writings to be purged.

The Mandaeans of southern Iraq, claiming descent from devotees of John the Baptist, maintain that Jesus was a magician in contact with Samaritan practitioners similar to Simon Magus.

Gospel Terminology and Crucifixion Charges

In Matthew 27:62, some translations have Chief Priests calling Jesus a "magician" rather than "deceiver." In John 18:30, the term "malefactor" used to describe Jesus carried powerful magickal implications during certain historical periods, particularly the Burning Times. This suggests Jesus may have been charged with practicing magick before Pilate.

Crucifixion punished political sedition, rebellion, rabble rousing, and the practice of magick and witchcraft, among other crimes.

Early Christian Magickal Practice

Early Christians, particularly Gnostic devotees, may have venerated Jesus as a sacred magician rather than viewing magick with the vehement opposition that later characterized Christianity. After Christianity gained official status, previous magickal aspects of the religion appear to have been suppressed.

Early Christians used the fish or ChiRo as emblems rather than the cross. Historian Morton Smith documented that two of the three oldest crucifixion representations appear on magickal gems, with the third likely referring to Christian magickal beliefs. A fourth-century gold glass plate in the Vatican Library depicts Jesus with a wand raising Lazarus from the dead, presented not as mockery but as legitimate magickal practice.

Name of Power

Within Jesus' lifetime, magicians began using his name in spells as a Name of Power. Acts 19:13 describes Jewish magicians and exorcists invoking Christ's name. In the Magickal Papyri and Greek curse tablets of the first and second centuries, Jesus' name appears among those used to conjure and control spirits and perform exorcisms in both Pagan and Christian spells, though whether these practitioners considered themselves Christians remains unknown.
