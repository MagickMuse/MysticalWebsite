Magickal Properties

Fairies are a diverse category of nature spirits found across many cultures, particularly in Celtic and European traditions. The generic English term "fairy" encompasses many distinct cultural entities that deserve recognition of their unique identities and traditions.

Nature-Spirit Categories

Folklorists divide fairies into two main categories:

Trooping Fairies live in sophisticated societies similar to those of humans and often accumulate wealth. "Trooping" indicates that at least once a year, fairies leave their home and travel in processional.

Solitary Fairies are not all literally solitary, although some are. Many live in packs. These fairies do not troop: they are wild or feral spirits and are described as solitary because they live a stark, simple existence in the forest or underwater away from civilization.

Etymology and Linguistic Connections

The word "fairy" derives from Old French fée, which comes from Latin fatua (female seer) and fatum (fate/destiny). In Italian, fata means both "fairy" and is the root of "The Fates" – revealing the deep connection between fairies and fate-determination across European cultures.

Symbolism and Folklore

The word fairy is a catch-all for every kind of little person, elves, pixies, banshees and the rest, each an aspect of a nature spirit generally held to be invisible to human sight. Fairies have been said to represent the paranormal powers of the spirit and the extraordinary reach of the imagination, and so they stand for an escapist faculty of the mind that slips past the bounds of the everyday and even the laws of physics. One old idea holds that fairies began as the spirits of dead ancestors, who before the coming of Christianity were thought to take a different form in order to guard and protect the generations to come.

Their typical powers are supernatural: invisibility, shape-shifting, spell-casting, and flight. Fairies tend to appear as female, though there are exceptions. Like witches they often come in threes, another tie to the Fates, and the kinship runs deep: Morgan le Fay, the Fata Morgana of the Arthurian tales, and Shakespeare's Queen Mab share a leaning toward malice quite unlike the tiny flower-fairies of the nursery. In the tale of Sleeping Beauty it is the fairy left off the guest list who curses the child, a reminder that these beings do not share human morals and must be propitiated. Their hour is twilight, the threshold between day and night, and they may carry off the soul of one who dies at that time, bearing it away to Fairyland. The hawthorn is a fairy tree, and anyone minded to cut it down or take from it would do well to ask the fairies' leave first.

Ritual Uses

Leave fresh offerings and respect fairy boundaries at mounds and hedgerows. Remove perishables within a day. Work with fairies for agricultural blessings, animal protection, death rites, and enchantments.

Warnings

Do not steal fairy tools, insult their sites, or assume they tolerate disrespect. Fairies can be benevolent allies or dangerous adversaries depending on how they are treated.

Childbirth

The spirits most associated with childbirth in fairy tales are the fairies themselves, proverbial fairy godmothers who fatefully appear either to bestow gifts and blessings on a newborn or else doom the infant with temperamental curses, usually because of an oversight by the child's parent — as in the tale of Sleeping Beauty. This is not merely a storyteller's device but a reflection of once-common practices.

The belief that spirits presiding over childbirth can impact and determine a baby's future is ancient. In Egypt, the role of the fairies was played by The Seven Hathors, who may be seven aspects of the goddess Hathor, her daughters, or a wholly separate divinity. Vestiges of the Seven Hathors appear millennia later in Muddy Waters' blues song "Hoochie Coochie Man," with its Seven Doctors who prophesy that the singer "was born for good luck." Celtic folklore describes a party of three fairies known as The White Women, present at every birth to predict or bestow the baby's fortune and fate. This connection between fate and fairies is hidden in English but apparent in Italian: Morgan Le Fay's name in Italian is Fata Morgana.

The Birth Chamber Offering Table

Once upon a time, fairies were invited into the birth chamber to bestow their blessings and influence the infant's fate. The invitation was extended by creating an offering table — essentially an altar, but "table" is meant literally. Fairies expect a genuine place setting for a meal on an actual table, not the top of a dresser or a counter; improvised or folding card-tables will do if necessary, but it must look like a meal will truly be served. These traditions were particularly strong throughout France, from whence Sleeping Beauty originally derives. According to Breton tradition, three guardian fairies attend the birth of every child to endow it with gifts and make predictions for its future, and the custom of preparing a festive table for these fairy sisters survived in Northern Brittany into the nineteenth century, on the reasoning that if the fairies have to choose whom among many births to visit, a fabulous offering table ensures they will choose you.

Following the conversion to Christianity, these practices were discouraged and forbidden — in France, consorting with fairies was historically a more common witch-hunter's charge than consorting with Satan. Yet anyone possessing the power to bless always retains the power to curse, and childbirth rituals to appease the fairies' anger and invoke their blessings continued regardless, since assuming the fairies will overlook a missing table risks a Sleeping-Beauty-style curse.

Changelings

Fairies steal humans, especially vigorous babies, midwives, and handsome young men, leaving fairy replacements called changelings. The practice may strengthen fairy bloodlines or serve fairy mothers. Some "changelings" voluntarily lived with fairies and returned as master healers and fairy doctors, like Thomas the Rhymer, who spent seven years in Fairyland and returned as a prophet. Methods to reverse the switch include direct exchange during fairy processions, nurturing the changeling until fairies want their own child back, or dangerous fairy exorcism. Abandoned fairy children often become dull-witted "ouphes" (the origin of "oaf").

Fairy Roads

Fairy roads are invisible paths that fairies travel and fiercely protect. Accidentally building on one or stumbling onto it risks disaster from fairy wrath. In 1921, Alfred Watkins identified "ley lines" connecting ancient sites (barrows, standing stones, stone circles, old churches on Pagan shrines) and published The Old Straight Track (1925). Though archaeologically controversial, metaphysicians understand these as energy paths rather than physical roads, similar to feng shui dragon paths. Rhiannon Ryall describes "Fairy Paths" or "Trods" as deeper-green straight lines in fields. Alternative theories suggest they're spirit roads linking burial grounds (Corpse Roads in Britain, German Geisterwegen connecting cemeteries).

Fairy Doctors

Fairy doctors heal illnesses caused by fairies: fairy dart (joint inflammation), fairy blast/stroke (tumors and paralysis), and in Hungary, muteness and "shrinking." Irish fairy doctors acquired healing gifts directly from fairies or as returned changelings who spent seven years in Fairyland. They may descend from female Druids (drui-ban) who evolved into independent practitioners post-Christianity. Balkan fairy doctors serve four-year fairy apprenticeships learning herbalism. They treated all maladies, served as bonesetters and midwives, and charged silver for herbal remedies. Famous practitioners include Biddy Early (Wise Woman of Clare with her magickal blue bottle), Maurice Griffin (who gained powers from fairy-touched cow's milk), and Murough O'Lee (who opened his fairy gift-book of cures three years too early).

The Fairy Faith

The Fairy Faith theory suggests fairy tales encoded sacred Pagan spiritual instruction during witch-hunt persecution. Stories of changelings, fairy encounters, and offerings (resembling African-Diaspora spirit offerings) may preserve vestigial Druidic practices that were illegal and heretical. Rural Ireland maintained lax Church control until the 19th century when standardization attempts and Age of Rationalism dismissed the Faith as primitive superstition. Irish Nationalist Party feared fairy belief would prevent political independence. Neo-Paganism has revived the tradition. Classic text: The Fairy-Faith in Celtic Countries by W.Y. Evans-Wentz (1911).

Fairy Illnesses

Various ailments are attributed to fairy agency:

Fairy Dart: In Ireland, painful inflammation of the joints, usually in the hands or feet. Fairy doctors specialized in removal via herbal ointment made with unsalted butter. An actual physical dart was often removed, which was frequently kept and displayed. Darts are also associated with elves. Some folklorists believe references to these darts and the illnesses they cause may actually describe the use of poisoned arrows by aboriginal peoples who are known as fairies.

Fairy Blast/Fairy Stroke: Tumors that arise suddenly and paralysis.

In Hungary, typical fairy illnesses include muteness, paralysis, and "shrinking" (perhaps describing stroke).

The Four-Leaf Clover and Fairy Sight

A four-leaf clover laid against the skin opens the eyes to the fair folk. A Cornish tale tells of a young milkmaid who stooped to grab grass and clover for a balancing pad for her milk pail. The instant she touched the clover, hundreds of little people swarmed the bucket, dipping clover blooms into the milk and gathering beneath the cow with foxglove and convolvulus flowers to catch the drops. Only when she returned home did her mother tell her she must have laid a four-leaf clover on her head. The four-leaved clover also guards its owner from all pishogues, the spells fairies and other supernatural beings may cast.

Foxglove, the Fairy Cap

Foxglove is the fairy cap, the witch's thimble, the lusmore of Irish telling. Each pendulous bell is mottled inside with purple, pink, white, and near-black in patterns that read almost like a secret hand. Grow it in the garden and the fairies are welcomed in, and with them comes their fierce protection. The plant is theirs to guard, which is one reason it is considered unlucky brought indoors as a cut flower. In the legend of Knockgrafton, the hunchback Lusmore wins the lifting of his hump because he meets the fairy song with patience and harmony, while greedy Jack interrupts the music and has Lusmore's discarded burden laid onto his own back. The lesson is to keep fairy time and fairy rhythm.

Fairy Cats

Cats, especially black ones, are the favored form of many species of spirits, and fairies are no exception. One Celtic tradition suggests that gazing into a cat's eye is a method of viewing fairies and magically entering Fairyland. Cats are also identified as Fairies.

The Cait Sith (pronounced "cat shee") is the fairy cat of the Scottish Highlands, described as being as large as a dog or calf and black, with a white star on its breast. This is no little cuddly kitty but an exceptionally fierce animal, spitting and growling when encountered. Many Highlanders once believed these cats were transformed witches on the prowl.

The cait sith is no forgotten legend: in recent years, Scottish police have received over a thousand reports of sightings of huge black cats. Although long considered a creature of fantasy, it is now believed to be the Kellas Cat, named after the village in Morayshire where it was first identified in the mid-1980s. Generally believed to be a cross between feral domestic cats and indigenous Scottish wild cats (Felis sylvestris grampia), some believe that Kellas cats are a unique species instead. Kellas cats are usually completely black with a white blaze on their chest, and they are large: the maximum recorded length for a male Kellas cat has been 43 inches from nose to tail, although larger ones have been reported.

The Fairy Witch: Bridget Cleary

An Irish nursery rhyme memorializes Bridget Cleary (1867–1895), "the last witch burned in Ireland." Independent and enterprising with her own income from sewing and chickens, childless after eight years of marriage, she "disturbed" neighbors by looking men in the eye, a trait of Pagan women. Both her mother and mother-in-law were fairy doctors; Bridget likely trained as one too. After her mother's 1894 death, she frequently visited fairy forts. Her husband Michael, whose own mother had disappeared with fairies for three days, feared and hated them. He ordered Bridget to stop visiting the forts; she defied him. On March 6, 1895, Bridget fell ill with fairy-sickness symptoms (aches, memory loss, temperament change). Michael believed fairies had replaced her with a changeling, she seemed two inches taller and "more refined." He consulted a fairy exorcist (not a Fairy Faith doctor). For nine days they starved her, force-fed her herbal cures and urine, then on March 15th at midnight, six men held her down while Michael threatened her with a hot poker, shoved her in the fireplace, poured paraffin oil over her, and set her on fire, saying he was "burning a witch." Bridget died of extensive burns. Eleven were arrested; Michael got 20 years for manslaughter (served 15). No priests would bury her. She and her mother lie in unmarked graves outside church walls. The scandal exposed the suppressed Fairy Faith and those who feared it as demonic. Further reading: The Burning of Bridget Cleary (Bourke, 1999).

The Sicilian Fairy Cult

Spanish Inquisition archives (1579–1651) document Sicilian witch trials revealing possible genuine fairy cult practice. Accused argued they didn't worship the devil but worked with fairy-like beings called Donna di Fuora. Members (mostly poor wise-women, farm laborers, fishermen, some nuns and prostitutes) healed "witch's touch," illnesses from offending fairies, ranging from indisposition to epilepsy (associated with Hecate). Nocturnal healing rituals involved elaborately set offering tables with bread, honey-cake, wine, and sweetmeats in perfumed rooms with red-draped beds. The fairy healer stayed awake all night playing tambourine, petitioning fairies to lift the affliction. Rituals were also held in stables for livestock and in fields for crops. Fairy cults organized into companies where human and fairy women (described with cats' paws, horses' hooves, pigs' tails, dressed in black/white) danced together to male fairy minstrels. A 1588 confession described dream-flights on goats to Benevento (belonging to the Pope, not the devil) to worship a Queen and King who promised wealth, beauty, handsome lovers, and healing remedies, followed by feasting, drinking, and sex. "The Seven Fairies" assembly involved transformation into animal forms. Testimony given under torture makes distinguishing genuine practice from coerced confession impossible.

Fairy Forts

Fairy forts (ring forts, stone forts) are roughly circular earthen banks or stone walls. 60,000 once existed in Ireland with names like cashel, forth, rath, or rusheen. Built as early medieval dwelling enclosures, these overgrown ruins became favorite fairy haunts. Like ruins attracting djinn in North Africa, ring forts attract spirits, making them wild, mysterious places where those seeking fairy contact go despite discouragement. Many contain physically dangerous underground passages.

Ash for Fairy Protection

The ash tree is one of the surest defenses against fairy magick. If you find yourself witness to a fairy procession, or feel yourself drawn to the ring where the fair folk cavort, take up an Ash branch for protection. The branches may also be wrapped around the horns of cattle or laid against a child's cradle to keep both safe from bewitchment and enchantment.

Elder and the Midsummer Procession

Standing beneath or near an Elder on Midsummer's Eve opens the eyes to trolls and fairies as they file past in procession. Elderflower water and elderberry wine carry the same revealing virtue into Midsummer rites. The elder is sacred to the fair folk, and any work with its wood, berries, or blossoms requires explicit permission from the Elder Mother who dwells within the tree.

Fairy Magicians

Central and southeastern European fairy magicians combine Irish fairy doctor healing skills with shamanic spirit possession. Female spiritual mediators maintain ritual connections with Keshalyi, Tündér, and Vila fairies, healing fairy-caused illnesses and practicing fortune-telling, necromancy, and protection magic. Initiated directly by fairies through dreams, visions, and trance-induced dance, they form fairy societies where spirits ritually possess members (similar to zar, orisha, and lwa possession). Romanian healers gather for rituals with Fairy Queen Doamna Zónelor; Balkan societies honor "Saint Helen" (possibly Tündér Ilona). The practice can stimulate illness as well as healing.
