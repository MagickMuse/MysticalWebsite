Ishtar is a Babylonian and Assyrian Goddess who appears under several names across Mesopotamian traditions, most notably as Inanna and Astarte. She shares many attributes with the later Goddess of Love, Venus and Aphrodite; the Larousse Encyclopaedia of Mythology describes her as "the divine personification of the planet Venus." Like Venus, she is called both the Goddess of the Morn and the Goddess of the Evening, because the planet appears on the horizon at both times of day. Her most recognizable image is a small, rounded statuette, naked and wide-hipped, offering her breasts in her hands.

Parentage and Domain

Ishtar's parentage is a matter of debate, and with it, her primary domain. As the daughter of Sin, the Moon God, she is a Goddess of War. As the daughter of Anu, the Supreme Sky God, she is the Goddess of Love. Despite her fearsome temper, she inspired sexual desire in all creatures and encouraged procreation. Her city, Erech, was called "the town of the sacred courtesans." The cult priestesses who carried out sacred prostitution as a profound fertility rite were deeply respected; the word "harlot" carries the same meaning as "shrine prostitute" or "holy woman."

Lovers and the Descent into the Underworld

Ishtar had numerous lovers, though none of them benefited from the relationship; she was cruel and fickle, quickly abandoning her mate once she tired of them. The Epic of Gilgamesh describes how her love brought about the death of Tammuz, the God of the Harvest. This tale echoes the later story of Aphrodite, who similarly mourns the loss of her lover Adonis; neither Goddess grieves for long.

In the descent myth, Ishtar travels to the Underworld to find her dead lover. At each of the seven gates she passes through, she is forced to surrender one item of adornment, from her crown down to her girdle and finally her dress, until she stands naked before Ereshkigal, Queen of the Infernal Regions, who locks her away. While Ishtar is imprisoned, the Earth falls into despair, mirroring the grief that overtakes the world when Demeter, the Greek Corn Goddess, abandons her duties to search for her daughter Persephone in the Underworld. Ishtar is ultimately rescued through the intervention of the God Ea; sprinkled with the waters of life, she reclaims her belongings at each gate on the return journey and arrives back on Earth fully restored. Like so many divine stories, this one speaks of death and resurrection.

Religious Associations

  • The Christian faith demonized Ishtar, vilifying her as the Whore of Babylon and the Mother of Harlots, despite the fact that she had long been worshipped in Jerusalem and honored with the title Queen of Heaven.
