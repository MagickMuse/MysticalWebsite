Britannia is a tutelary goddess of the Romano-Celtic British tradition, the genius loci of the island of Britain personified as a seated divine woman. She first appears on the coinage of the Roman emperor Antoninus Pius in the second century, and from that first minting onward she has been the presiding figure through whom the island itself is imagined as a sovereign presence.

Domains

Sovereignty of the island of Britain, the personified genius of the land, tutelary protection of the British people, and the sea-girt authority of a nation understood as a divine woman.

Iconography

Britannia is depicted seated, helmeted, and armed. She holds a spear and a large round shield in her earliest Roman-era portrayals, and in her later imperial phase she is often shown holding a trident that marks her guardianship of the seas. Her seated posture, her helmet, and her weapons all belong to the visual family of the Roman war goddess Minerva, whose iconography she inherited through provincial syncretism.

Syncretism with Minerva

In the Roman provincial period Britannia was partly syncretized with the Roman war goddess Minerva, and through that identification she gathered the martial, wise, and civic attributes of the elder Roman goddess. The pairing gave Britannia the shape she would carry through the succeeding centuries, in which she stands as both patron of the land and figure of martial and civic authority.

Historical Presence

Britannia has appeared on the coinage of Britain in an almost unbroken line from her second-century Antonine debut through the succeeding centuries of Roman rule, and thereafter into the medieval and early modern periods. She was revived as the emblematic figure of the British nation on modern coinage from the seventeenth century onward, and later served as the personified symbol of the British Empire, appearing on the coinage, statuary, and civic imagery of that empire through the nineteenth century.
