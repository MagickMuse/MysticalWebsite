The Dog-Star

Sopdet is the divine personification of the star Sirius, the brightest star in the night sky. The Greeks called her Sothis. Her heliacal rising (first visible appearance before dawn after a period of invisibility) marked the most important astronomical event in the Egyptian calendar.

The Nile Flood

Sopdet's rising coincided with the annual inundation of the Nile, typically in mid-July. This flood was essential for Egyptian agriculture, depositing fertile silt across the farmlands. The star's appearance thus heralded the renewal of life itself.

Identification with Isis

Sopdet was identified with Isis, the Great Lady of Magic (Weret Hekau). This connection linked the star's appearance to Isis weeping for Osiris, her tears causing the Nile to rise. The stellar and mythological cycles were thus intertwined.

The Hour-Priests

Egyptian hour-priests (wnwty) were specialists in observing the movements of heavenly bodies by night, often from temple rooftops. They used their observations of Sopdet and other stars to:

  • Plot monthly and annual ritual cycles
  • Judge which days would bring good or ill fortune
  • Create the calendars of good and bad days

Harkhebi's Expertise

The priest Harkhebi, known from a 2nd-century BCE statue, listed among his accomplishments the ability to "read the omens of the sun and the dog-star Sirius." This suggests Sopdet's movements were used for divination, presaging the later development of astrology in Egypt.
