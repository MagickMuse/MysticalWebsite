Magickal Properties

The Each Uisge is considered the most dangerous of Scotland's water spirits. Each Uisge literally means "Water Horse" in Gaelic, indicating its most famous manifestation. It is a shape-shifter not limited to one form. It may appear in the guise of a sea bird or a handsome young man with seaweed in his hair.

Ritual Uses

The Each Uisge is not a spirit to work with but one to avoid. The best way to survive is caution and avoidance. Should one wish to beckon them closer, the scent of roasted meat on a deserted shore in a region they are known to frequent allegedly serves as a lure.

Offerings

None. This is a dangerous predatory spirit to be avoided.

Favored People

None. The Each Uisge is a dangerous predatory spirit.

Petition For

None. Avoid this spirit entirely.

Warnings

The usual manifestation is as a strikingly beautiful, mysterious, prancing horse or pony. The Each Uisge appears to be a friendly horse, someone's pet who's just wandered off. It may approach people and beckon them to mount. It's a trap. Mount the Each Uisge and it will quickly and abruptly plunge deep into the sea. Once drowned, the victim is devoured.

Additional Notes

The Each Uisge lives in the sea and in Scotland's deep lochs. It does not necessarily target people but is happy to consume sheep or cattle too, although it must use different ruses. It is smart and insatiably hungry. Attempts made to poison an Each Uisge who lived in Loch na Beiste, Inverness, failed.

Mull and the Green Water-Herbs

A Hebridean account tells of a traveler passing a lonely loch northeast of Mull who came upon a horse grazing along the shore. He noticed green water-herbs, called liaranaich, clinging about its feet and wisely refrained from touching it. A stranger he met along the road later confirmed his caution: had the horse been a water horse and found him unfriendly, it would have carried him straight into the deep.

Skye and the Sharp-Billed Form

On Skye, the each uisge is said to have a sharp bill (gob biorach), or, by other accounts, a narrow brown slippery snout. Reports are uniform that it carries a long flowing tail and mane. In colour it is sometimes grey, sometimes black, and sometimes black with a white spot on its forehead. Some say this variation arises because the water horse may be any colour like other horses; others say it has the power of changing its colour as freely as it changes its shape. When it appears in the shape of a man, it is detected by horse-hooves still hidden in the boots and by green water-weeds or sand still tangled in the hair. In that form it is very amorous, but the end of those unfortunate enough to encounter it is the same: to be taken to the loch and devoured.

The Bridle, the Cap, and the Cow-Shackle

Local tradition holds that an each uisge may be captured and bound with a bridle, a cap, or a cow-shackle, and that the farmer who secures one in this way may at first derive great benefit from its strength. The catch is in the long view. However much profit comes at the beginning, the bargain always ends in ruinous loss for the one who tried to tame it.
