Magickal Properties

  • Beloved Norse god of light, joy, purity, and reconciliation
  • Bringer of peace, healing of grief, and restoration of innocence

Ritual Uses

  • Petition for reconciliation after conflict, clarity of purpose, and gentle guidance through endings
  • Dream petitions for reassurance, omen‑clarity, and relief from sorrow

Offerings

  • Symbols of light and kindness; poems or songs praising peace; plants associated with him (valerian, mayweed)

Favored People

Those who value peace, kindness, beauty, purity, and reconciliation

Petition For

Joy, healing of grief, reconciliation, purification, clarity, and safe transitions

Warnings

Beware hubris and blind spots. His myth teaches that small, overlooked things (mistletoe) can betray invulnerability, heed omens and details.

Midsummer Pine Fires

Pine branches are lit at Nordic Midsummer festivals to light the way for Balder's descent into darkness.

Additional Notes

  • Epithets: Baldr, Baldur, Baldaeg (Anglo-Saxon)
  • Associations in lore include sun‑bright radiance and universal belovedness; betrayed through mistletoe

Historical Context

Worshiped from around 700 CE, possibly earlier, until Christianization circa 1100 CE. Centers of cult are unknown. Art references include stone carvings.

Attributes

Balder is the spotless "good" god, the "shining one," and Othin's favored second son. His name means "lord." He lives in a hall named Breidablik. He is the father of the god Forseti.

Lore

According to Snorri's account, Balder was made invulnerable to injury or death by his mother Frigg, who had extracted a promise from "all things" not to harm him. She had omitted the mistletoe as being too small and insignificant. Loki, seizing on this oversight, used the blind god Hoder as his instrument, guiding Hoder's hand to turn a sprig of mistletoe into a lethal dart.

Saxo, in contrast, defines Balder as a warrior slain by a magic sword in a battle of jealous rivalry between him and Hoder for the hand of the goddess Nanna. There are separate suggestions that Balder traveled the road to the underworld ruled by Hel in the company of many other slain warriors, implying that he met his death in a wider combat.

Additional Context

There is no evidence of a Germanic precedent for Balder, and he is probably of purely Norse extraction. Attempts to cast him as a copy of Christ seem wholly unfounded. He is also difficult to relate to the dying-and-rising gods found in other religions such as Dumuzi, Telepinu, and Osiris, since there is no suggestion of his return from Hel's kingdom of the dead. However, there is an implication that he will be released by Hel at Ragnarok.
