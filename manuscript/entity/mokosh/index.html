The Great Mother

Mokosh (also Mokoš) is the ancient Russian goddess of spinning, weaving, earth, fertility, and women's mysteries. She is the only female deity included in Prince Vladimir's pantheon in Kiev, demonstrating her supreme importance in Slavic tradition. As a spinning goddess, she embodies the sacred connection between textile arts, earth's fertility, and women's spiritual power.

Spirit of Moisture

Mokosh is the spirit of moisture. She is manifest in dampness and liquids. Mokosh protects mother's milk and semen. Rain is considered to be her milk. Czechs invoked her aid during times of drought.

Spinning Goddess and Sacred Waters

Mokosh exemplifies the profound connection between spinning goddesses and water sources, particularly wells and springs. Like the Norse Norns at the Well of Urd, spinning goddesses are traditionally associated with water. Wells serve as liminal spaces, thresholds between worlds, where the sacred work of spinning takes on mystical dimensions. The act of spinning by a well connects the spinner to ancient fate-working traditions.

Sacred Fiber Plants

Mokosh is intimately connected to the sacred plants that can be spun into fabric: flax, hemp, and nettles. These fiber plants held sacred status in goddess-worshipping cultures, as they literally could be "spun" into thread and woven into cloth. After Christianity's spread, these plants were demonized as "witch plants" specifically because of their associations with goddess worship, textile magic, and women's spiritual traditions. The negative associations with hemp particularly are believed to derive from its connections to witchcraft, shamanism, and women's magical traditions rather than from the plant's properties alone.

Earth Mother and Weaver

As both earth goddess and spinning goddess, Mokosh represents the connection between growing the sacred fiber plants and transforming them through spinning into usable thread. Her dominion over both earth and textile arts demonstrates the complete cycle from cultivation to creation, from plant to cloth.

Demonization of Spinning Traditions

After Christianization, Mokosh's worship was suppressed and her sacred plants were condemned. The very act of spinning, especially spinning the sacred plants of flax, hemp, and nettles, became associated with witchcraft. Women who maintained the old spinning traditions and worked with these plants faced persecution as the textile arts were stripped of their sacred dimensions and the plants themselves were demonized.

Pilgrimages

As late as the sixteenth century, Christian authorities were complaining that Slavic women "went to Mokosh." Pilgrimages to Mokosh stones in the Ukraine reportedly continued through the nineteenth century as women petitioned her for good health and fertility.

Magical Properties

Mokosh embodies the sacred power of spinning and weaving as acts that connect earth's abundance with women's creative and magical abilities. She represents the transformation of raw plant fiber into thread, paralleling the spinning of fate itself.

Ritual Uses

Work with Mokosh for spinning and weaving mastery, earth magic and fertility work, protection through textile magic, and connection to women's ancient spinning mysteries. Invoke her when working with the sacred fiber plants of flax, hemp, and nettles. Honor her at wells and water sources where spinning magic is strongest.

Manifestations

Mokosh manifests as a woman and as stones, especially those that are breast-shaped.

Iconography

Mokosh is portrayed spinning beside a well.

Attributes

Mokosh carries a spindle and/or a child.

Creature

Sheep

Warnings

Respect the sacred nature of spinning and the wells where spinning magic is strongest. Honor the plants that were persecuted alongside the goddess and her devotees. Remember that the demonization of "witch plants" was actually the persecution of women's spiritual traditions and goddess worship.

Additional Notes

Mokosh's importance is demonstrated by her inclusion as the only female deity in Prince Vladimir's pantheon. Her survival in folk traditions despite Christianization shows the deep roots of spinning goddess worship in Slavic culture. The connection between spinning goddesses, sacred wells, and fiber plants forms a consistent pattern across cultures from Russia to Scotland to the Mediterranean.
