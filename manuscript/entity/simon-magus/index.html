Gnostic saint, magician and spiritual leader from Samaria, contemporary of Jesus and the Apostles.

Origins

Whether Simon was an ethnic Samaritan or a Jew from Caesarea remains uncertain. A magician named Simon lived in Caesarea around 40 CE. The theologian Justin Martyr claims Simon was born in Gitta, a Samaritan village. His title "Magus" literally means "Magician."

Encounter with the Apostles

Simon was baptized a Christian by the Apostle Philip. In the Book of Acts, he is portrayed as a wandering magician who converted to Christianity. Observing Saint Peter healing by the laying on of hands, Simon offered money to learn this skill. Peter sharply rebuked him for assuming divine powers could be purchased. This incident gives us the word "simony," meaning the practice of buying positions within the Church.

Bishop Irenaeus of Lyons described Simon as "from whom all heresies take their origin." He became the Christian symbol of arrogance and pride. Peter remained his primary competitor and nemesis throughout his career.

Simonian Gnosticism

Simon may have been a disciple of John the Baptist before achieving tremendous success as a spiritual leader in Samaria and Rome. He founded Simonian Gnosticism, teaching that the cosmos was not created directly by God but by a female emanation called Ennoia (Thought). She created angels who rebelled against her. The battle was so fierce that Ennoia became lost in her own creation, forgetting her identity and wandering through incarnations.

Meanwhile, the rebellious angels tried ruling the world but fought among themselves, filling creation with suffering. God finally came to earth in the form of Simon Magus to rescue Ennoia. He found her incarnated as Helena, a prostitute in the Phoenician city of Tyre. Through Simon, Helena regained her memory and retired from her profession.

Some perceive Simon's pairing with Helena as deliberately echoing the relationship between Jesus and Mary Magdalen. Both women were associated with prostitution, though ancient cultures distinguished between common and sacred temple prostitution. The pairings may indicate involvement in forbidden esoteric practices.

Teachings and Opposition

Simon taught that anyone recognizing him as God was saved, after which conventional morality became unnecessary since those rules were created by angels to enslave humanity. His opponents, the source of most surviving accounts, claimed he conjured spirits, concocted potions, and encouraged free sex. Some accused him of accomplishing miracles through necromantic control of a murdered boy's spirit. He was identified as the Antichrist.

Despite Christian opposition, Simon was worshipped in Rome. Devotees built temples featuring statues of Simon and Helena.

Death and Legacy

According to one legend, Simon died flying off the top of the Roman Forum during a magical duel with Saint Peter. Another account claims he buried himself for three days, promising to resurrect, but failed. He was buried in Aricia near Rome.

His disciple Menander assumed leadership of the sect. Simonian Gnosticism survived in the Roman Empire for over 150 years, though by the early third century, Origen claimed fewer than thirty Simonians remained. All writings of Simon's school were destroyed.
