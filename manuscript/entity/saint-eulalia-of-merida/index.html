Classification

Roman Catholic saint.

Life and Martyrdom

From her earliest childhood, Eulalia (c. 290-304) longed to be a martyr. Her Christian family lived in Emerita, capital of the Roman province of Lusitania (now Mérida, Spain). Her mother, aware of this intense desire for martyrdom, tried to keep her daughter safe by sending her to the countryside.

At approximately fourteen, Eulalia ran away. Arriving at the Governor of Emerita's court, she publicly professed her Christianity, insulted the deities Apollo, Isis, and Venus, insulted the Roman emperor, and challenged the governor to make her a martyr.

The Governor's Response

The governor met her challenge. Eulalia was:

  • Stripped naked
  • Tortured with hooks and torches
  • Burned at the stake

She taunted her torturers throughout. She finally died of smoke inhalation, though a French tradition suggests decapitation.

The Dove and the Snow

At the moment of her death, a white dove flew from Eulalia's mouth. Her corpse was thrown into the street, but a sudden, miraculous snowfall covered her nakedness.

Early Veneration

Rumors quickly spread that Eulalia was a saint. A shrine was erected over her tomb. Documented evidence shows she was venerated by 350 CE. The Christian poet Prudentius (c. 348-410) dedicated part of his Peristephanon (Crowns of Martyrdom) to her. A basilica was built around 560, and her relics were distributed throughout Iberia.

She was very popular among the Visigoths. Her body was transferred to Oviedo around 780, where she lies in a silver coffin.

Iconography

Depicted as a naked young woman lying in snow, as in John William Waterhouse's famous 1885 painting Saint Eulalia. Though the snow allegedly fell to cover her nudity, her portraits may be more revealing.

Spiritual Ally

Venerated alongside Saint Julia of Mérida, another young Christian martyr. They share the same feast day.
