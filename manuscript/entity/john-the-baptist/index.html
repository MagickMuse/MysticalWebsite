John the Baptist, venerated as a saint by most branches of Christianity, is renowned for healing convulsive, seizure, and spasmodic disorders, as well as banishing bad dreams and ensuring successful surgery.

Three Perspectives

Depending upon spiritual orientation, people tend to understand John the Baptist in one of three ways:

  1. Christian Saint: Jesus' cousin and forerunner who paved the way for the Messiah. His feast is an exceptionally popular Christian festival.
  2. The True Messiah: Conspiracy theorists suggest John's true identity was suppressed by the Vatican, with clues planted in Leonardo da Vinci's paintings. This premise underlies Dan Brown's The Da Vinci Code.
  3. Masked Pagan Spirit: John's associations with sacred, magickal, and healing powers of water made syncretism natural. His summer feast coinciding with the solstice allowed beloved Pagan festivals to continue undercover. This explains why so many magick spells are associated with him. Many practitioners believe that beneath the Baptist's mask lies the ancient spirit Adonis, a deity intimately connected to cycles of death and rebirth.

Life and History

John the Baptist was a Jewish prophet who inclined to asceticism. In trouble with the law, he fled to the Judean desert, where he dressed in animal skins and subsisted on wild honey, carobs, and locusts. Some believe he was affiliated with the Essenes, an ascetic, esoteric Jewish community linked with the Dead Sea Scrolls.

Some perceive John as the personality who bridges the Old and New Testaments. He followed in the footsteps of the Old Testament prophets, and some consider him to be the literal reincarnation of the prophet Elijah.

John publicly protested against the marriage of Herod, king of Judea, to Herodias, the king's former sister-in-law, a union that could be construed as incestuous under Jewish law. John thus earned the undying enmity of Queen Herodias, who plotted his demise.

Death

When King Herod promised Herodias's daughter, Salome, anything her heart desired following her performance of the Dance of the Seven Veils, Herodias encouraged Salome to request John's head on a platter. John the Baptist was beheaded in approximately 30 CE.

The whereabouts of his head remain mysterious. One theory suggests it briefly resurfaced among the treasures of the Knights Templar.

The Mandaeans, an ancient Gnostic tradition centered in Iraq, perceive John as the last and greatest prophet. They acknowledge he baptized Jesus but do not believe he was beheaded. Instead, an angel of death appeared to John, who recognized the angel's true identity and went willingly.

Powers and Domains

John the Baptist is believed able to fulfill any petition but is especially invoked for healing. He is petitioned for help with convulsive, seizure, or spasmodic disorders. He banishes bad dreams and protects against spiritual or magickal dangers.

Macabre Healing Associations:

  • A medieval remedy for headaches involved meditating on images of John's severed head. Allegedly, if you look at it long enough, your own will start to feel better.
  • He is invoked before surgery, because his beheading may be construed as the worst possible outcome of surgery. John is petitioned to ensure your surgery is successful.
Saint John's Bread is a folk name for carob seeds, a staple of John's diet. Offer them with a glass of water and some honey if you feel in exile and need his help.

Cross-Cultural Veneration

  • Mandaeans (Nazoreans): An ancient Gnostic tradition sometimes called "Christians of Saint John"
  • Druze: Venerate John the Baptist as Al Khidr, the Green Man
  • Masonic Orders: Claim him as their patron
  • Haitian Vodou: Classified among the lwa
  • Brazilian Umbanda: Head of the Oriental Line of spirits, which includes spirits from the Aztecs, ancient Egypt, China, Inca, India, Israel, and Japan
  • European Folk Tradition: Special protector of witches, midwives, and all who heal and work with herbs. In Siberia, his feast is called Ivan Travnik (John the Herbalist) or Ivan Koldovnik (John the Magician).

Iconography

John is the subject of innumerable icons. Artists have drawn inspiration from various episodes of his life, from child companion of Jesus, to ascetic desert prophet, to his death and aftermath. He is the patron saint of Florence, birthplace of the Renaissance. Titian, Caravaggio, and Leonardo da Vinci painted his portrait. Gustave Moreau and Aubrey Beardsley created many images featuring John's head on a plate.

Feast Days

Western Church:

  • 24 June (birth) - Midsummer's Day, among the oldest on the Church calendar
  • 29 August (death/Decollation)

Eastern Orthodox Church (six feasts):

  • 23 September (conception)
  • 7 January (transfer of relics)
  • 24 February (first and second finding of head)
  • 25 May (third finding of head)
  • 24 June (birthday)
  • 29 August (beheading)

The first day of the Ethiopian calendar is dedicated to Saint John.

Saint John's Eve Magick

The night preceding his feast (23 June) is considered among the most magickal nights of the year. Botanicals are believed to be at the peak of their mystical power. Plants harvested from dusk to dawn allegedly possess enhanced magickal and healing powers. The festival is traditionally celebrated with bonfires and water rituals: a night devoted to love divination and fertility rituals.

Sacred Plants

No saint is more closely associated with botanicals. The Seven Herbs of Saint John: hawkweed, mistletoe, mullein, mugwort, wormwood, orpine, and Saint John's Wort. These are tossed into midsummer bonfires; ashes are collected and carried as a protective amulet believed to attract love, romance, and fertility.

John is also closely associated with ferns, roses, plantain, yarrow, and vervain.

Botanical Spells

  • According to Russian tradition, any plant harvested before dawn on Saint John's Day is innately magickal
  • Italian folk healing teaches that the most therapeutically powerful herbs are gathered on Saint John's Eve before dawn
  • Wear a mugwort wreath around your brow on Midsummer's Eve to banish headaches for a year
  • Gather blossoming Saint John's Wort at midnight on Saint John's Eve: if blossoms remain fresh in the morning, the year will be happy; if wilted, magickal protective measures may be needed
  • Add fresh flowers to your bath at midnight on Saint John's Eve for good luck and happiness
  • Hang Saint John's Wort over your bed or beneath your pillow on Saint John's Eve to receive dreams of your true love
  • Rise at dawn on Midsummer's Day and pick a sprig of Saint John's Wort with dew still clinging to it (without speaking, eating, or drinking); tradition says you will marry within the year
  • Gather Saint John's Wort on Saint John's Eve, dry it, and place in a glass jar near a window to keep premises safe from fire

Sacred Sites

John is the patron of natural springs. He presides over Louisiana's Lake Pontchartrain (Saint John's Lake), where Voodoo Queen Marie Laveau once presided over Saint John's Eve rituals.

Major Sacred Sites:

  • Greek Church of John the Baptist in Jerusalem (fifth century, oldest church in Jerusalem)
  • Franciscan Church of Saint John the Baptist grotto in Ein Karem (possible site of John's birth)
  • Aachen Cathedral, Germany (cloth on which John's severed head was placed, displayed every seven years)
  • Munich's Residenz Museum (claims to house John's head)
  • Topkapi Museum, Istanbul (John's right arm on display)
  • Sanctuary of John the Baptist in Coptic Monastery of Saint Macarius (built over his tomb)
  • Basilica of Saint John Lateran, Rome
  • Cathedral of John the Baptist in Turin (home of the Shroud of Turin)
  • Amiens Cathedral, France (claims to possess his head)
  • Shrine of Prophet Yahia in Grand Mosque of Damascus (believed to contain John's head; shrine considered a place of intensely magnified spiritual power)
  • Cetinje Monastery, Montenegro (claims to possess John's right hand)
  • Black Sea island Sveti Ivan (bones discovered in 2010 in fifth-century monastery ruins)
