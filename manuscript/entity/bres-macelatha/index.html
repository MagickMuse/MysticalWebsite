Overview

Bres Macelatha, whose name means Bres son of Elatha, is a vegetation god of the Celtic Irish tradition. He is the son of Eriu, one of the sovereignty goddesses of Ireland and a member of the Tuatha Dé Danaan, and of Elatha, a king among the Fomorians. Through this parentage he carries the blood of both peoples, and the tension between those two inheritances shapes the whole course of his story.

In Irish literature

Bres appears above all in the Cath Maige Tuired, the account of the Second Battle of Moytura, and in the Book of Invasions. When Nuada, the first king of the Tuatha Dé Danaan in Ireland, lost his hand and was therefore compelled to give up the kingship, Bres was chosen in his place, largely on account of his beauty and his mixed lineage, which promised to secure peace with the Fomorians. His reign, however, was remembered as a time of oppression. He imposed harsh tributes on the Tuatha, showed little of the generosity expected of a king, and neglected the sacred obligations that bound a ruler to the welfare of his people. When Nuada's hand had been restored by the healers of the Tuatha, Bres was removed from the kingship.

He then turned to his father Elatha and to the Fomorians for support, and in the great Battle of Moytura he stood on the Fomorian side against the Tuatha. The Fomorians were defeated, and Bres was captured. According to the tradition, his life was spared in exchange for teachings on the proper times for plowing, sowing, and reaping, which he conveyed to the victors. In this way even his defeat delivered agricultural knowledge to Ireland.

Domains

Bres Macelatha presides over the supply of food from cultivated land, over the fertility of grain and cattle, and over the knowledge of the agricultural calendar. The cycle of his story, in which a beautiful and mixed-heritage figure fails as a king yet ultimately yields the wisdom of cultivation, marks him as a vegetation deity whose power is realized through the seasonal renewal of the harvest.

Ritual

Approach Bres Macelatha at the turning points of the agricultural year, especially at sowing and at harvest. He is a figure of ambivalent authority; petitions should center on the fruitfulness of the land itself rather than on personal dominion, since his own kingship failed on that account.
