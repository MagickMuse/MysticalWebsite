Classification

Unofficial saint. Beatification proceedings opened 13 February 2005.

Life and Legend

Therese Neumann, mystic, visionary, stigmatic, and Franciscan tertiary, was born in 1898 in the small Bavarian village of Konnersreuth. She arrived late at night, so it remains unknown whether she was born on 8 April (Good Friday) or 9 April (Holy Saturday). She was baptized on Easter Sunday.

Therese came from a large, impoverished peasant family as the first of ten children. She attended school from 1904 to 1911 but, as the eldest, was needed to care for her siblings. A sturdy, robust girl, she did much physical labor at home and also worked as a servant. Always spiritually inclined, she wished to become a missionary.

The Accident and Illness

On 10 May 1918, while helping extinguish a fire in her uncle's barn, Therese carried heavy buckets of water, suddenly felt sharp pains and cramps, and fell. She was never the same again.

She developed partial paralysis and suffered repeated falls resulting in disproportionately severe injuries. She lost most of her eyesight after another fall and by 1919 was completely blind. She also suffered from pneumonia, digestive problems, and ear abscesses causing hearing loss. Bedridden, she developed such severe bedsores that her bones were exposed.

Miraculous Healings via Thérèse of Lisieux

Therese prayed novenas requesting restoration of her health and petitioned Thérèse of Lisieux for assistance:

  • On 29 April 1923, the day Thérèse of Lisieux was beatified, Therese Neumann regained her sight.
  • On 3 May 1923, an ulcer between her toes severe enough to potentially require amputation was spontaneously healed when three rose leaves from the tomb of Thérèse of Lisieux were placed on the bandage.
  • On 17 May 1925, the day Thérèse of Lisieux was canonized, she reported hearing the new saint call to her, advising her that she was healed. Her paralysis and bedsores vanished.

The Stigmata

In 1926, Therese began manifesting signs of the stigmata, initially keeping it secret until blood began seeping visibly through her clothing. Each week, beginning Thursday at midnight and continuing until Friday at one in the afternoon, her wounds would open and bleed. Coinciding with the stigmata, every Friday she experienced profound visions of Christ's Passion, claiming to observe the entire Passion as a "helpless observer." She wept tears of blood.

By 5 November 1926, Therese displayed nine bleeding wounds, which devotees claim never healed or became infected.

Mystical Abilities

During her trances, Therese spoke Aramaic and comprehended Greek, Hebrew, and Latin. When not entranced, she could only speak colloquial German. Linguistic scholars declared her pronunciation and vocabulary authentic. She also claimed to have witnessed Mary's death, burial, and ascension, asserting that Mary's grave was in Jerusalem rather than Ephesus.

Inedia

In 1922, after praying that a young man's throat cancer be transferred to her (he was miraculously healed), Therese claimed she could no longer consume food other than consecrated communion wafers. From 1922 until her death in 1962, she allegedly consumed only one daily consecrated wafer. She also claimed not to have consumed water or any other liquid from 1926 until death, and reportedly slept no more than two hours a night.

In July 1927, she was placed under twenty-four-hour watch for two weeks by a medical physician and four Franciscan nurses who confirmed she consumed nothing beyond her daily wafer.

Nazi Resistance

Therese made no secret of her disapproval of Nazi policies. Although never directly attacked, she was under constant Gestapo observation and suffered continuous threat of arrest. The Nazis mocked and ridiculed her and harassed her family and local priest.

German journalist Fritz Michael Gerlich (1883–1934), a primary opponent to National Socialism, visited Therese intending to debunk her but instead became convinced of her authenticity. He became her biographer and converted to Catholicism under her influence. As his spiritual advisor, Therese encouraged his resistance to Hitler but strongly advised him to remain in Switzerland. He did not take this advice and was arrested and killed at Dachau after Hitler's rise to power.

During World War II, Therese refused ration cards, claiming to live on God's light.

International Fame

After the war, Therese became an international phenomenon as news spread worldwide. Paramahansa Yogananda visited her in 1935 and devoted a chapter to her in his bestselling Autobiography of a Yogi (1946). Despite her apparent lack of nourishment, Therese was never emaciated. Yogananda described her as "healthy, well-formed, rosy-cheeked, and cheerful."

Death and Signs of Sanctity

Therese died from cardiac arrest on 18 September 1962. Physicians testified that even four days after death, there was no putrefaction or cadaverous smell. Rigor mortis failed to set in; she remained so pliant that some feared she was not really dead but only entranced. Over 40,000 people have signed a petition to open her cause for sainthood.

Sacred Sites

The shrine at her gravesite in the Konnersreuth cemetery is covered with votive plaques testifying to miracles received.

Related Entities

Catherine of Siena, Saint Pio, Anna Maria Taigi, Thérèse of Lisieux.
