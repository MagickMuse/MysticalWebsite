Adad is the weather god of the Mesopotamian Babylonian-Akkadian tradition, derived from the older Sumerian model of Iskur. His name in Akkadian cuneiform means wind, and he carries the full range of the storm, from the rains that feed the fields to the violent weather that breaks them.

Origin and Cult

Adad's known period of worship runs from circa 1900 BCE or earlier to circa 200 BCE. His centers of cult include Karakara and the Syrian cities of Aleppo and Mari, with a major following at Mari. He was occasionally the subject of a sacred marriage ceremony in parts of Mesopotamia and Syria. His father is the supreme sky god Anu, and his place in the pantheon is that of a son of the sky who carries the sky's weather down into the world.

Two Faces of the Storm

Adad is described as a benevolent giver of life in the fields, the bringer of rain whose arrival turns dry ground into harvest. He is also a more violent storm god, a presence whose anger shows in destructive weather. The two faces are not separate gods. They are the same Adad seen from the field and from the wreckage.

Iconography

In human form Adad is depicted wearing a horned headdress and a tiered skirt or robe decorated with astral symbolism. He may carry a scimitar embellished with a single panther head. His symbol is the lightning fork, often fixed upon a pair of pincers, the forked bolt held in a grip that marks the visible discharge of his weather. His sacred animal is the bull, whose bellow stands in for the rolling of his thunder.

Sources

Adad appears across the surviving Mesopotamian record. Art references include reliefs, stelae, and glyptics. Literary sources include cuneiform texts such as Atra-hasis, alongside numerous inscriptions naming him in his weather-god role.

See Also

In the western Semitic and Syrian sphere, Adad is identified with Hadad. The two names mark the same storm god as he is known across overlapping cultural zones.
