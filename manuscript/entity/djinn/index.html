Magickal Properties

Djinn (also spelled Jinn, Djinni, or Genie; Moroccan plural: Jnoun) are indigenous spirits of North Africa and the Middle East. They are spirits made of smokeless fire, created by Allah before humans were made from clay. Djinn predate Judaism, Christianity, and Islam, but have traveled the world with Islam and are now found in locales far from their original habitat. They inhabit an invisible parallel realm that occasionally intersects with our world. Djinn are intelligent beings with free will who can be righteous, neutral, or evil, just like humans. They have their own societies, religions, and cultures. Djinn can shape-shift, become invisible, possess humans, and move with incredible speed. They live much longer than humans but are not immortal. Some Djinn are Muslim, Christian, Jewish, or Pagan, while others are unbelievers. The offerings they accept reflect their religious beliefs: Muslim djinn may not accept offerings of liquor; Jewish and Muslim djinn may only accept kosher or halal meat respectively; Christian djinn reputedly eat anything. Djinn may be more likely to assist (or at least not harm) humans who share their religious proclivities.

Ritual Uses

In Islamic tradition, attempting to command or conjure Djinn is generally forbidden and dangerous. However, in folk magick traditions, practitioners may seek to work with righteous Djinn for protection, knowledge, or assistance. Djinn may be bound to objects like lamps or rings in legends. They can grant wishes but often in unpredictable ways. Protective magick against malevolent Djinn involves reciting Quranic verses, especially Ayat al-Kursi, wearing amulets, and maintaining ritual purity.

Offerings

Righteous Djinn appreciate offerings that align with Islamic practice: incense (especially frankincense), perfumes, dates, honey, milk. Never offer blood or alcohol to Muslim Djinn. Offerings should be made respectfully at thresholds, crossroads, or wild places.

Repelling Djinn

All djinn despise and fear salt and iron. If you want to avoid or repel djinn, salt and iron are your primary tools.

Favored People

Djinn interact with humans unpredictably. Some favor those who show respect for their realm and observe proper boundaries. Righteous Djinn may favor pious individuals. Others favor magicians or those with whom they have made pacts.

Petition For

Protection from evil spirits and black magick, knowledge of hidden things, assistance in difficult tasks, healing from Djinn-caused illness, removal of malevolent Djinn influence.

Warnings

Do not summon Djinn without expert guidance and rigorous protective protocols. Never attempt to enslave or command Djinn. Avoid bargaining for selfish or harmful ends. Never trespass in places where Djinn are traditionally honored (ruins, bathrooms, deserts at noon). Malevolent Djinn can cause possession, illness, madness, and death. Even well-intentioned contact can be dangerous. Most Islamic scholars strongly advise against attempting to contact Djinn.

Djinn-Master Saints

Legend suggests that King Solomon coerced a work-gang of djinn into serving as his construction crew. King Solomon remains renowned as a djinn master, and certain saints share this reputation. They can command djinn to do their bidding, banish or summon them as needed, and remedy damage and illness caused by them. If you run into trouble with djinn, these are the saints to invoke.

In general, the djinn-master saints are Sufi or Jewish, perhaps because Christianity does not distinguish between djinn and demons. Thus the only acceptable Christian approach is permanent exorcism, not the long-term relationships shared by some saints and djinn.

Types of Djinn

There are different types of Djinn: Marid (most powerful), Ifrit (strong and cunning), Ghul (shape-shifters who haunt graveyards), Si'lat (female Djinn), and others. Famous individual djinn include Aisha Qandisha, Aisha Sudaniyya, and Lalla Mira.

Additional Notes

King Solomon famously commanded Djinn to build his temple. Djinn can marry humans and produce hybrid offspring. They fear iron, salt, and certain sacred texts. Djinn are also believed able to cause infertility. They are particularly active at twilight, in deserted places, and near water. Related spirits include the Peri and various regional variations across the Islamic world.

The Genie and the Soul

Though the word genie comes from the Latin genius and has a different root from Djinn, the two came to share a meaning: the superior part of the soul, a kind of familiar or psychic double, the part of the self that is enhanced by enlightenment. In the tale of Aladdin this is told as allegory. The lamp he polishes so faithfully stands for his own soul, the polishing for the work of enlightenment won by conscious effort, and the genie who rises from it for his awakened self, his possibility and potential. The three wishes stand for discipline, for lessons learned through direct experience, and for the need to curb desire.
