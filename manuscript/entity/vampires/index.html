Death Spells: Death, Ghosts, Necromancy & Vampires

Living Corpses

Vampires are revenants who rise from the grave. Traditional folkloric vampires drain life-energy through sexual energy or siphoning chi, rarely through blood-sucking. Many are compelled to count, afflicted with obsessive-compulsive syndrome. Traditional antidotes include garlic, bells, sunlight, and fishing nets.

The Saint's True Opposite

Popular culture identifies saints and sinners as opposite ends of a spectrum. According to fairly universal folk tradition, however, it is the vampire who is the saint's true opposite:

  • Saints overflow with such potent life energy that, even after death, they can use it to benefit the living.
  • Folkloric vampires drain the vital energy or aura of a living person, typically sucking the lifeblood, not actual blood.

Bram Stoker did not invent vampires. The concept dates back thousands of years and exists across world cultures, though different names for this type of harmful dead soul may be used. This folkloric vampire remains extremely real to many people and has virtually nothing in common with the fictional vampire.

The vampires of folk tradition are not suave, sexy, or glamorous. They do not sparkle. They are clearly dead, albeit restless and destructive. Their very presence is often believed to spread infectious disease. People are not doomed to become vampires simply because they are bitten. Instead, certain lifestyles, conditions, and spiritual predilections are believed to be causal factors.

Causes of Vampirism in Christian Europe

  • Illegitimate birth (born outside the sacrament of marriage)
  • Death by suicide
  • Inadequate or improper burial rites
  • Being born on Christmas Day (perceived as arrogance to share a birthday with Jesus)

Essentially, those who defied Church rules were believed likely to become vampires after death. Alternatively, those born with traits that in pre-Christian Europe would have indicated latent shamanic power, such as a caul, teeth at birth, or significant birth order like being the seventh son or daughter, were believed likely to turn vampiric after death.

Vampires & Death Magick

Vampires occupy a specific place within the broader category of Death Spells. Spells concerned with preventing vampirism — particularly those involving funerary rites and immediate post-death ritual — are grouped with Death Spells, since they address the conditions that allow vampirism to occur in the first place.

Spells that deal with vampires that have already formed — "mature" vampires already active and affecting the living — are addressed separately under vampire workings specifically.

There is considerable overlap between death spells, ghost spells, and vampire spells. The distinctions can be blurry in practice, since all three categories involve souls of the dead in some relationship with the living.

The single most powerful preventative measure across traditions is proper funerary rite. Incorrect or absent burial rites are among the most widely cited causes of vampirism across cultures — which is why magical traditions place such emphasis on how the dead are treated and prepared. The soul that cannot transition peacefully has no recourse but to remain, and a soul that remains too long with insufficient spiritual support may eventually become predatory.

Causes of Vampirism in Non-Christian Societies

Asian traditions especially believe that predatory magical practitioners are likely to become vampires. East Asian alchemy focuses intensely on cultivating life energy with the ideal goal of achieving immortality. Alchemists who possess sufficient knowledge to drain energy from the living, but not enough to defeat death, are believed likely to become vampires.

Garlic and the Wreath

Garlic is the single most iconic apotropaic against the vampire. The cinematic wreath of large cultivated cloves, Allium sativum, is a comparatively late image. The older folk practice more often turned to the wild garlic, Allium ursinum, also called ramsons. The fact that the wild relative appears so prominently in the oldest workings speaks to the antiquity of the practice. The standard household ward uses the fresh flowering tops rubbed across every opening of the threatened room, with a wreath at the throat of the sleeper. See the plant entry for Garlic and the ritual Garlic Wreath at the Sickbed. The literary figures of the Gothic tradition, Van Helsing and his foil Dracula, preserve and transmit the older method.

Blood as Life-Force

The popular image of the vampire, the deathless once-human creature that lies in its tomb by day and rises at night to drink the blood of the living, spread across Europe, Russia, and much of Asia. That it draws all its nourishment from blood recalls the old understanding of blood as the sustaining life-force, the same belief that led ancient peoples to daub their dead with red ochre in the hope that the vitality of the color alone might restore life.

Wards and the Missing Reflection

A Slavic edict of the Middle Ages laid a heavy fine on any female vampire found guilty of taking blood from a man. The classic protections have held steady for centuries: the Christian Cross, since the vampire is reckoned an agent of the Devil; the silver bullet, whose metal holds the essence of the moonlight the vampire needs and so works as a kind of homeopathic poison; garlic, a herb of protection loathed by demonic creatures; and the wooden stake through the heart. The surest sign of a vampire is the absence of a reflection, for a reflection is an emblem of the soul, and that the vampire does not possess.

Additional Lore

Another interpretation suggests that a vampire may not be dead at all, but a living sorcerer able to send out a shadow soul that others interpret as a vampire.

Additional traditional antidotes include bright light, a rooster's crow, peppermint, onions, and bullets melted from bell metal. Fishing nets placed over entrances work because a vampire is compelled to count their holes and knots; spilling poppy or millet seeds serves the same purpose, since a vampire may feel compelled to stop and count or pick them up.
