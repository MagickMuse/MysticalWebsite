Encyclopedia Source Notes

Goddess of war and patron defender of many Greek cities. Worship attested from circa 800 BC and earlier through Christianization circa 400 AD and later. Synonyms include Athene, Pallas Athenae as the maiden goddess of Athens, and Roman Minerva. Cult centers extend beyond Athens to Argos, Sparta, Gortyn, Larisa in Thessaly, Lindos, and Ilion at Homer's Troy. Major art references include the Parthenon frieze, the Athena of Phidias known as the Varvakeion, and the Olympia metope showing her assisting Herakles in supporting the sky. Literary sources include the Iliad and Odyssey of Homer and the Theogony and Hymn to Pallas Athene of Hesiod.

According to Hesiod she is the daughter of Metis, wisdom, and was born fully armed from the head of Zeus. As a goddess of battle and allegedly a snake goddess, she stands for discipline against the more unruly conduct of figures such as Hermes and Poseidon. Her most famed sanctuary is the Parthenon. The olive tree is sacred to her, particularly the one grown by tradition on the Acropolis, whose oil was given to the victors in the Panathenaia festival. She is credited with offering the olive to humankind. Her symbol is the aigis, the skin of a sacrificial goat. She is associated with shipbuilding and with domestic crafts including wool work and spinning, and Athenian women have traditionally woven the peplos at the Panathenaia festival. In legend she is the destroyer of Ajax and lures Hector to his death, while supporting heroes such as Perseus against the Gorgon and Diomedes against Ares. She also acts as a moderating influence in the conflict between Achilles and Agamemnon, the most notable instance of her characteristic ability for self-control.
