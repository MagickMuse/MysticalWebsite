Magickal Properties

Akerbeltz is a goat spirit and horned deity who lives in the mountains and caves of the Pyrenees. Generally considered a virile, male spirit, he is closely associated with Mari and may be her companion, alter ego, or even twin soul. Some perceive Akerbeltz to actually be a path of Mari. He is ancient, with Roman inscriptions referencing him, and manifests as a black goat.

Ritual Uses

Akerbeltz protects flocks from danger and disease and raises storms while leading a host of spirits. His sacred days are Monday, Wednesday, and Friday. Work with him for livestock protection and storm magick within traditional Basque spiritual frameworks.

Offerings

Bread, eggs, and coins presented with respect for his ancient role as protector of flocks and guardian of Basque spiritual traditions.

Favored People

Shepherds, those seeking protection of livestock, practitioners of traditional Basque spirituality, and people working with horned deities and protective spirits.

Petition For

Protection of flocks from danger and disease, storm raising, spiritual guidance, and connection to ancient Basque spiritual traditions.

Warnings

Was identified with Satan by the Inquisition during devastating witch hunts in Basque country in the 16th-17th centuries, leading to accusations against women. This historical persecution should be understood as political and religious oppression rather than accurate spiritual assessment.

Additional Notes

The goats presiding over sabbats in Spanish artist Francisco Goya's Witchcraft Paintings may be understood as portraits of Akerbeltz. His relationship with Mari is complex and may represent different aspects of the same spiritual force. His connection to traditional Basque spirituality predates Christian influence by centuries.

The name Inquisitors gave to Basque witches' sabbats was "Akelarre" or "Goat meadow," directly referencing Akerbeltz. Goya's famed painting of that name depicts a witches' sabbat presided over by a mammoth upright goat. While traditional Pagan offerings to Akerbeltz were modest (bread, eggs, coins), the Inquisition claimed in the 16th and 17th centuries that witches offered him more dramatic sacrifices, such as their immortal souls, claims that were part of the systematic persecution and demonization of Basque spiritual practices.
