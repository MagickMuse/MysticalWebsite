Martín de Porres (9 November 1579 to 3 November 1639) was a Roman Catholic saint born in Lima, Peru, to a Spanish knight and a freed slave of African and indigenous Panamanian descent. He was baptized by the same priest in the same basin as Saint Rose of Lima, who became his good friend.

Apprenticed to a barber (a trade that incorporated herbalism and surgery at that time), Martín entered the Dominican Third Order at fifteen. Because of his dark complexion, he was only accepted as a lay servant performing menial tasks, earning the nickname "Brother Broom." After nine years, his exceptional powers, humility, and spirituality convinced the monastery to accept him as a full member.

Martín spent hours in nightly prayer, fasted intensely, and never ate meat. He continued as a barber and healer, performing miraculous healings with herbs he grew and by laying on of hands. He ministered to everyone regardless of race or status and helped establish an orphanage and foundling hospital.

Domains

Martín is invoked for racial harmony, healing, fundraising (he could raise miraculous sums in miraculous time), pest control, taming rambunctious children, and keeping straying spouses home. Wrap a rope around his statue's feet and request his help with the latter.

Iconography

Martín is depicted with a broom, hairdresser's shears, and a dog.

Attributes

Broom, hairdresser's tools (shears), dog. His emblem shows a dog, cat, and mouse eating from the same dish.

Colors

Purple, white.

Sacred Sites

Casa San Martín de Porres (his birthplace) in Lima, Peru. His shrine within the Church and Convent of Santo Domingo in Lima. Saint Martín de Porres National Shrine in Memphis, Tennessee.

Lore

Martín adored animals and communicated with them psychically. He ran a veterinary hospital from his sister's home, treating livestock, cats, dogs, and birds. When ordered to poison monastery mice, he instead summoned them, reprimanded them, and struck a deal: he would feed them if they left. The mice accepted and lined up outside each evening for dinner.

Martín demonstrated clairvoyance, prophecy (accurately predicting his death), bilocation, and levitation. Veneration began immediately after his death; his funeral shroud was cut into squares and preserved as relics. Canonization began in 1660 but took over three centuries, completed in 1962 during the US Civil Rights movement. He is recognized as the first Black saint of the Americas.
