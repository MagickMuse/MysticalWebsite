The Mahavidyas (great revelations or manifestations) are a group of ten goddesses in the Hindu Tantric tradition. They appear in the medieval period and represent the Devi's capacity to manifest herself in fearsome, overwhelming forms. The group expresses a central Shakta theological idea: the supreme goddess contains an inexhaustible variety of forms within herself, and her most terrifying aspects carry the greatest transformative power.

The Ten Mahavidyas

The standard list of ten is: Kali, Tara, Chinnamasta, Bhuvaneshvari, Bagala (Bagalamukhi), Dhumavati, Kamala, Matangi, Sodasi, and Bhairavi. Some lists identify Kamala directly with Lakshmi and Sodasi with Tripura Sundari.

Origin: The Sati Myth

The mythological origin of the Mahavidyas is set in the story of Sati and Shiva. Sati's father Daksha plans a great sacrifice and deliberately excludes Shiva and Sati, disapproving of Shiva's uncivilized habits. Shiva is unbothered, but Sati is outraged and insists on attending the sacrifice to disrupt it. When Shiva forbids her to go, she loses her temper. She assumes a dreadful form, then multiplies herself into ten forms that surround Shiva completely on all sides. Shiva, terrified, relents and gives her permission to go.

In the Mahabhagavata-purana account, Sati then takes the form of Kali, attends the sacrifice, and kills herself. Daksha weeps at the sight of his daughter as Kali: dark, naked, and disheveled.

Descriptions of the Ten Forms

Kali has a fierce countenance, is naked, dwells in the cremation ground, holds a severed head and a bloodied cleaver, has disheveled hair, and sports a garland of decapitated heads and a girdle of severed arms.

Tara as a Mahavidya is nearly identical to Kali. She is dark, rests her left foot on a corpse, wears a tiger skin and a necklace of severed heads, laughs terribly, has her hair in a single matted braid, stands on a funeral pyre, and is pregnant.

Chinnamasta stands in a cremation ground on the copulating bodies of Kama and Rati. She has decapitated herself with a sword held in one hand; in the other she holds a platter bearing her severed head. Three jets of blood spurt from her neck into the mouths of two female attendants and into the mouth of her own severed head.

Bhuvaneshvari nourishes the three worlds. She holds a piece of fruit in one of her four hands. Her breasts are large and ooze milk. She has a bright, light complexion, smiles pleasantly, and holds a goad and a noose.

Bagala (Bagalamukhi) has the head of a crane, is seated on a throne of jewels, is yellow, holds a club with which she beats an enemy, and with another hand pulls her enemy's tongue.

Dhumavati has a pale complexion, is tall, and has a stern, unsmiling expression. Dressed as a widow in dirty clothes, her hair is disheveled, she has no teeth, her breasts are long and pendulous, she is afflicted with hunger and thirst, her nose is large and crooked, she has a quarrelsome nature, she holds a winnowing fan, and she rides a crow.

Kamala is a beautiful woman of golden complexion surrounded by elephants pouring pitchers of water over her. She is seated on a lotus and holds two lotuses in her hands. She is portrayed in most respects like Lakshmi, one of whose epithets is Kamala.

Matangi is black. Her eyes roll in intoxication and she reels about like an impassioned elephant.

Sodasi is a girl of sixteen with a red complexion. She is shown astride the prone body of Shiva, with whom she is having intercourse. They rest on a pedestal composed of the gods Brahma, Vishnu, Rudra, and Indra.

Bhairavi (Tripura Bhairavi) has a reddish complexion and wears a garland of severed heads. She holds a rosary and a book in two of her four hands and makes the signs of fearlessness and conferring boons with her other two hands. Her breasts are smeared with blood. The Kalika-purana adds that her eyes roll from intoxication and she stands on a corpse.

Theological Function

The Mahavidyas are not primarily cosmic warriors defending order. Their primary function in the myth is to terrify Shiva and demonstrate that Sati's power exceeds his. They serve as an assertion of the Devi's supremacy over the male gods. When Sati assumes these forms, the Mahavidyas overpower Shiva completely.

In both the Brhaddharma-purana and the Mahabhagavata-purana, Sati commands Shiva to give mantras and instructions for worshiping the Mahavidyas. Both texts state she appeared in these forms so that devotees may achieve not only ultimate release (moksha) but also desires and power over others. Specific ritual powers associated with the group include: marana (destruction of enemies), uccatana (causing a person to cease their actions), ksobhana (causing emotional disturbance), mohana (causing infatuation or delusion), dravana (causing people to flee), jrmbhana (causing people to become slack), and stambhana (causing paralysis). These powers arise from their fearsome collective nature and are not assigned to individual members.

Relationship to the Vaishnava Avataras

The ten Mahavidyas function as a Shakta parallel to Vishnu's ten avataras. The Guhyatiguhya-tantra identifies each Mahavidya with a Vishnu avatara and claims the avataras arose initially from the Mahavidyas: Kali became Krishna, Chinnamasta became Narasimha, and so on. However, the Mahavidyas differ significantly from the avataras in function. The avataras appear to defeat demons and restore cosmic order; the Mahavidyas overwhelm the gods and empower Tantric practitioners.

Nature as a Group

The Mahavidyas act primarily as a collective force. None of the ten is individually significant when they appear as a group; they represent the inexhaustible, terrifying variety of the Devi's self-manifestation. Three of the ten are also important as individual goddesses: Kali (who is almost always named first), Tara, and Chinnamasta. Their inclusion and their fearsome qualities set the tone for the group as a whole.
