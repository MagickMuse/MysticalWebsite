🏺Egypt❤️‍🩹Healing

Amenhotep, son of Hapu, is renowned for healing through sacred dreams at his sanctuary. Together with Imhotep, he forms a divine healing pair who offers diagnosis and cures to pilgrims seeking their aid.

The Wise Scribe

Amenhotep, son of Hapu, was a historical figure who became worshipped as a god, credited with great wisdom and medical knowledge. He lived a long, esteemed life during the late 18th Dynasty, directing building projects for King Amenhotep III (r. c. 1386-1349 BCE).

Historical Life

At Thebes, Amenhotep lived a distinguished career. In return for his service, the king allowed Amenhotep to erect statues of himself in the temple of Amun at Karnak, an extraordinary honor for a non-royal person.

The Karnak Statues

Some of these statues stood outside the temple gateways for centuries, inviting local people to pray to Amenhotep, son of Hapu, who would make sure the gods heard them. The statues show Amenhotep in the cross-legged posture of a dedicated scribe, with a roll of papyrus stretched across his lap.

The stone surface has worn away over time as thousands of people touched the 'papyrus' to make contact with these statues as they prayed or passed by.

Appearance in Art

In scenes carved on temple walls, Amenhotep was depicted as a respectable priest with a shoulder-length hairstyle or head-covering. The temples honoured him as priest, magician, and medical man.

Healing Pair with Imhotep

In the Ptolemaic Period, Imhotep and Amenhotep formed a healing pair in several temples in and around Thebes. They appear together in the temple of Hathor at Deir el-Medina and in a temple of Thoth at nearby Qasr el-Aguz.

Sanctuary at Deir el-Bahri

Imhotep and Amenhotep had their own dedicated sanctuary at Deir el-Bahri, where graffiti from the Roman period give thanks for healing and protection. The sanctuary may have offered sleeping spaces where the faithful could spend the night, hoping for a healing message from the gods in their dreams.

Funerary Imagery

The coffins of two young people who died in Roman-era Thebes, Padiamenipet and Kleopatra, depict Imhotep and Amenhotep in pride of place at the head end, either side of a boat containing the sun-god as a scarab beetle. Their presence on these coffins suggests the family may have sought healing from these divine physicians before the deaths occurred.
