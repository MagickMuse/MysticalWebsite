Books as Magickal Tools

Throughout history, the mere possession of magickal books was enough to warrant persecution. In late antiquity, charges of witchcraft frequently included owning such texts, alongside accusations of astrology, divination, and potion-making.

Books serve far more purposes in magick than simply providing spell instructions. A book can function as an amulet in its own right. Certain texts need not even be read; their physical presence in the home is believed to ward off various misfortunes. Notable examples include the Bible, the Koran, The Book of Raziel, Book of Pow-Wows: The Long Lost Friend, and the Russian Dreams of a Virgin.

Books also serve as tools of divination. Practitioners open specific texts to random pages and interpret the passages they find. The Bible, the Koran, Homeric verses, and the works of Virgil are among the most commonly used for this purpose.

Psalms in Magick

The Book of Psalms carries a parallel identity as a magickal text. Individual psalms and their verses are employed to cast spells for a wide range of intentions. While many associate this practice with Hoodoo, where psalm recitation is deeply embedded, the tradition actually stretches back centuries through European magickal practice. A medieval compilation cataloguing the magickal applications of each psalm proved so popular that it was reprinted in pocket-sized editions and translated from Latin into multiple European languages. The work was eventually placed on the Index Librorum Prohibitorum by the Roman Catholic Church.

Spells from the Islamic world similarly incorporate recitation of appropriate Koranic verses.

Sacred Texts

The Koran, the Bible, and similar works are regarded as inherently magickal because they are sacred texts. This category extends well beyond monotheistic faiths to include the works of Homer and Virgil, the Indian Vedas, and the Chinese I-Ching (Book of Changes). The key consideration for effective spell-casting is whether a given text holds sacred significance for the practitioner. Sacred texts are so deeply charged with baraka and heka that anyone may access their power, yet magick operates as a reciprocal process between transmitter and receiver. The most potent sacred texts for any individual are those personally perceived as sacred.

Methods for the magickal use of psalms and sacred texts include:

  • Whispering texts over a cup of water, then giving it to someone to drink (or drinking it oneself, depending on the spell's purpose)
  • Writing texts on paper, dissolving the paper in liquid, and drinking it (a method derived from ancient Egyptian practice)
  • Wearing written texts as amulets for empowerment and to transmit constant magickal energy
  • Tracing texts on an apple with a pin, then eating the apple or feeding it to the spell's target

Books of Shadows

Many practitioners maintain a personal record of spells cast and created. Over time, especially when magickal inks and designs are incorporated, a Book of Shadows becomes highly charged with magickal power in its own right.

Book Protection

Books themselves sometimes require protection. Mugwort, wormwood, and Saint John's Wort are believed to preserve books both physically and spiritually while repelling page-eating vermin.

  • Place leaves of these botanicals between pages
  • Maintain living plants in and around libraries
  • Kabikaj is the name of the djinn who holds dominion over insects; writing his name in books and on manuscripts is said to magickally prevent damage from worms and other vermin
