The Sacred Rattle

The sistrum (shesheshat in Egyptian, named for its soothing, rattling sound) is a musical instrument sacred to Hathor and Isis. The Latin word 'sistrum' derives from this Egyptian term.

Form and Sound

The sistrum consists of a handle attached to a U-shaped metal frame. Crossbars threaded through the frame hold loose metal discs or loops that produce a jingling, rattling sound when shaken.

The soothing sound was believed to please the gods, particularly Hathor, and to drive away evil influences.

Religious Use

Sistra appear in tomb paintings of New Kingdom banquet scenes, where musicians and dancing girls perform in an intensely erotic atmosphere with incense, perfume, and flowers. These scenes may represent religious festivals, particularly those meant to lure Hathor, the sun-god's daughter, back to Egypt after she has departed in anger.

According to myth, Hathor's return was necessary for Ra to regenerate himself overnight. The continued survival of the cosmos depended on attracting her back through music, drunkenness, and celebration.

Offerings at Deir el-Bahri

Hathor had both male and female devotees, judging by offerings at her temple at Deir el-Bahri:

  • Faience bowls decorated with tilapia (fertility symbol), lotus flowers, and women strumming harps
  • These images echo the musicians in tomb banquet scenes

Fertility Connection

The sistrum's association with Hathor links it to sexuality, fertility, and the cosmic renewal that human reproduction mirrors. Playing the sistrum invited divine favor for matters of love, conception, and birth.
