Corpse Water

◇Notes/Warnings
The liquid reserved after bathing a deceased body. Virtually extinct as a magickal ingredient due to modern mortuary practices. Many documented uses involve covert administration without consent; these are historical records only.
↗Category1
💧Water
↗Dreams1
💀Dreaming of Corpses

Corpse water is the liquid retained after bathing a deceased body, typically in preparation for funerary rites. Contact with the dead transforms the water, imbuing it with the essence of the deceased. Like coffin nails and graveyard dirt, it appears across magickal traditions worldwide as a potent ingredient. Unlike those materials, corpse water has no substitute and cannot be replicated.

Traditional Uses

Because modern mortuary practices have removed the preparation of the body from the home, corpse water is now essentially inaccessible. Historically it was employed for a range of purposes, both protective and harmful. Many of its traditional uses involve covert administration and fall in the category of controlling or hexing magick.

Documented uses include preventing a man from betraying his marriage partner (mixed into food or drink), weakening the physical strength of an abusive person (by inducing them to wash their hands with it), dissolving romantic feelings in another, and easing prolonged grief.

Corpse water also appears in fertility workings with opposing applications depending on cultural context. In parts of the Middle East, the water from bathing a person who died suddenly in their physical prime is believed to hold residual life force. Drinking it may restore fertility, the vital essence being understood as seeking a new living vessel. Conversely, in parts of North Africa, feeding it covertly to a rival is believed to cause infertility, particularly when the corpse was elderly and the dying process prolonged, leaving no vital energy in the water.

Storage and Disposal

In many traditions, corpse water is kept beneath the bed for as long as the body remains in the home, or at minimum in the same room. It is not removed before the body: both depart together, or the water follows after. Prescribed disposal methods include pouring it onto the grave, discarding it outside the home property as the coffin departs, or pouring it on the spot where the deceased's placenta or umbilical cord was buried.
