Graveyard dirt is one of the most fundamental and widely used magickal ingredients across traditions worldwide. At its simplest it is soil taken from a cemetery or from a specific grave, though its nature, power, and appropriateness for use varies significantly by cultural framework.

What Graveyard Dirt Is

Graveyard dirt is not inherently malevolent. Whether it is understood as dangerous or benevolent depends on how a given tradition understands the dead. Traditions rooted in African and Asian spiritual frameworks that view ancestral spirits as helpful presences tend to use graveyard dirt for protective and beneficial workings. Traditions that view the dead as threatening or contaminating treat it as toxic power, usable only for cursing.

The term also serves as a nickname for several botanicals with no literal connection to graves:

Valerian is called graveyard dust for its reputation as a sleep aid so powerful it promises sleep like the dead, combined with its distinctly foul smell.

Patchouli is called graveyard dirt for its wet earth aroma.

Mullein shares this name through its associations with Hekate and Oya, two spirits with strong ties to death and cemeteries.

In historical grimoire tradition, graveyard dust could also refer to bone ash mixed with asphaltum, sometimes called mummy dust. This is a rare and archaic usage; the practical modern substitutes are dried powdered mullein or actual churchyard earth.

Some practitioners use one or more of these powdered botanicals in place of actual cemetery dirt. Some combine them with real dirt. Some use only the real thing.

How It Is Collected

Methods vary by tradition and intent. Surface dirt from within cemetery walls is considered sufficient by some. Others dig directly within a grave. British magickal tradition specifies dirt taken from above the heart of the deceased. Hoodoo tradition calls for three scoops: one from above the head, one from above the heart, one from below the feet. ⚠️ Hoodoo is a closed African American folk magick tradition. Research and respect the cultural context before working in this tradition.

When collecting discreetly, a small quantity may be gathered in a pocket or talisman bag. For larger amounts, bringing potted plants to transplant onto the grave is a traditional method: dig a hole for each plant, reserve the loosened dirt, and carry it home in the emptied plant container.

In some traditions, a coin or libation is placed in the hole as payment to the spirit of the grave being disturbed.

Malevolent Uses

The simplest recorded graveyard dirt hex is to throw a handful at the back of a person as they walk away from you. What distinguishes this from a protective toss is not the action but the intent: the practitioner's own suppressed rage and hatred is the active magickal ingredient, and the dirt is merely its vehicle. In Java, graveyard dirt is used to induce unnaturally deep sleep, whether in the context of robbery or unwanted romantic access, preventing others in a household from waking.

Context: Sacred Dirt Across Cultures

Graveyard dirt belongs to a much broader tradition of using earth from significant places as a magickal tool. For centuries, dirt from holy sites and shrines has been carried as protective amulets, placed in graves, sprinkled over sacred objects, or pressed against wounds for healing. Among the most visited examples today is the Shrine of Chimayo in New Mexico, where pilgrims come annually for its reputed healing dirt.

Selecting a Grave for Dark Workings

Graveyard dirt carries different properties depending on whose grave the soil comes from and the purpose of the working. Collecting from the grave of an ancestor is always preferred. When that is not possible, the choice of grave should align with the intent of the spell.

For baneful or aggressive workings, dirt from the grave of someone who died violently or who was a murderer is ideal. If neither is available, dirt from the grave of a child can be used. Ancestral dirt remains a reliable alternative if none of the above are accessible.

Some practitioners consider the full moon the best time to collect, though timing is ultimately a personal decision.

Collecting the Dirt

What to Bring

  • A digging utensil such as a trowel or spoon
  • A container for the dirt (a pill bottle or resealable bag works well)
  • Offerings for the spirit: 9 copper pennies, whisky or wine, and tobacco. If the grave belongs to a child, candy or a small toy is more appropriate.

Scouting the Grave

Visit the cemetery a day before you plan to collect. Choose a grave that feels right. When you return to collect, sit at the grave and meditate. State your purpose clearly and ask if you may purchase the spirit's services. Listen for a reply. If the answer feels like a yes, select from your offerings what you sense the spirit would prefer. If the answer feels like a no, reach out to another spirit who may be agreeable. You will eventually receive a response, or you will feel drawn toward a grave that calls to you.

The Collection

Gather the dirt from the area where you sense the heart would be. If there is grass, carefully remove a plug and set it aside. Dig roughly 3 to 6 inches into the ground and collect your soil. Drop a silver dime into the hole, or copper if you feel the spirit would prefer it. This is your payment. Thank the spirit for the help they are about to provide, then pour whisky, wine, or candy into the hole. Replace the grass plug to cover the site.

Record Keeping

Write down the name, birth date, death date, and the name of the cemetery on a piece of paper or label. Affix it to the container. Hold the bag or bottle of dirt in your hand and pray with conviction for the spirit to accompany you and lend their power through the dirt.

Leaving the Cemetery

Walk away from the grave without looking back. Leave the cemetery by a different route than you arrived. At the cemetery gate, turn around three times, whether on foot or by stepping out of your vehicle to do so. This confuses any spirits that may try to attach themselves to you.

Before entering your home, turn around three more times outside your front door. Be casual about it, as though you are simply surveying the area. Keep a pot filled with many small pebbles and sea salt beside your front door. Add one more pebble before you go inside. Spirits are compelled to try to count the contents, and if they are still counting at sunrise, they are forced to return to the cemetery.

Preparing and Storing the Dirt

The dirt must be dried before use. Two methods work well:

  1. Cloth drying: Tie the dirt, one container's worth at a time, inside an old cotton shirt or dish towel and hang it to dry. Keep batches separate so they do not mix.
  2. Foil drying: For smaller amounts, spread the dirt on a sheet of foil and allow it to dry thoroughly.

Once the dirt is fully dry, sift it. Do this step outside, or wear a mask indoors to avoid breathing in the dust. Separate the material into categories:

  • Fine dust
  • Larger clumps
  • Rocks and pebbles
  • Tiny roots
  • Dead insects

Any live creatures, worms, or insects found during sifting should be placed in a jar and released at the nearest open field.

Store each category in its own labeled jar or bag, all bearing the same name, dates, and cemetery. Keep a notebook logging whom you gathered from, when, and for what purpose. Different components serve different spells; some are better suited for mojo bags or packets, while others work best in powder form.

Every time you use the dirt, call the spirit by name and ask them to assist you by the contract you made at their grave.

Types of Dirt by Source

Mentally Disturbed or Insane Person

Used to cause insanity, mental instability, or psychological unraveling in a target.

Warning: You are dealing with the spirit of someone who was mentally disturbed in life. This energy is volatile and can backlash onto the practitioner.

Murderer or Killer

The older the dirt, the more potent it becomes. Soil from the grave of someone who took human life carries extreme power in matters of revenge and causing harm. Dirt from someone who was hanged is considered especially effective.

Warning: Incorporating this type of dirt into your workings can cause death. Like the dirt of an insane person, this is an unstable energy that demands caution and respect.

A Murdered Person

Highly useful for workings that address injustice or wrongdoing. The spirit's experience of being a victim gives this dirt a sharp edge in matters of retribution on behalf of others who have been harmed.

Nun, Priest, or Pastor

Used when spiritual guidance and spiritual protection are needed. However, some practitioners use this dirt dishonestly to project an illusion of goodness and innocence to the outside world. This is a form of glamoury, and it cannot hold up against someone who is genuinely good.

Oya: Queen of the Dead

Oya holds dominion over all cemeteries. It is essential to gain Her permission before entering Her kingdom. She loves gifts and is not known to have ever turned any down. She is especially fond of red wine and copper. Prepare an old pill bottle with 9 pennies and some red wine as an offering. When you ask Her permission to enter, let Her know you have brought Her a gift.
