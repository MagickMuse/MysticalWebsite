Ritual Uses

Horns and their derivatives (cornucopias) serve as vital ritual tools placed on altars and used especially for summoning spells for ghosts and spirits. The primeval admiration and awe for horns has not been forgotten but remains vital in modern magickal practice.

Traditional Applications

Day of the Dead: Horns are filled with candy as treats for child-ghosts during Day of the Dead celebrations.

Dionysian Rites: Horns are traditionally stuffed with grapes to summon Dionysus.

Wiccan Practice: Some Wiccan traditions (such as Seax-Wica) substitute a drinking horn for a chalice during rituals. Horned helmets or caps are sometimes worn during Neo-Pagan or Wiccan rituals, especially when a High Priest is impersonating a horned god.

The Symbel: In Heathen traditions, the symbel is a horn-sharing ritual with deep roots in Norse culture. The ritual appears in Beowulf, where warriors pass the horn and speak oaths, boasts, and blessings. The drink shared is called the byrelle. Words spoken over the horn are laid in the Well of Wyrd, the cosmic well at the roots of Yggdrasil where fate is woven, and become truth. This gives the symbel tremendous power: oaths become binding, boasts become challenges to fulfill, and blessings carry the weight of destiny. The horn's spiral shape mirrors the cyclical nature of fate itself.

Masonic Symbolism: Among modern Masons, the cornucopia remains symbolic of joy, peace, and plenty.

Flying Ointment Carriers: Easter witches carry flying ointment in horns in the same way that African witches carry hyena butter in gourds.

African Magickal Traditions

In sub-Saharan Africa, horns are frequently stuffed with botanical and other magickal material to create amulets and talismans.

Italian and North African Amulets

In Italian and many North African traditions, small horns or replicas of horns made from various natural and synthetic materials are popular amulets. They are used for many purposes but the most common are:

  • Protection of male fertility and libido
  • Destruction of the Evil Eye

The Cornucopia

The cornucopia is an ancient emblem of abundance - a large hollow horn from which fruits and other botanicals overflow. Deities who carry it implicitly promise peace and prosperity.

Goddesses of the Cornucopia

Numerous female deities are depicted displaying the cornucopia:

  • Demeter
  • Persephone
  • Fortuna (Rome's Lady Luck)
  • Fauna
  • Flora
  • Epona (Celtic horse goddess of fertility and abundance)

Modern Cornucopias

Cornucopias are still used today, often as festive table centerpieces, but they are rarely if ever made from real horns now. Paper or wicker cornucopias are far more common, thus many perceive it as an abstract, crescent shape and forget the associations with horns.

However, these associations are explicitly stated in the symbol's name: "cornucopia" derives from the Latin cornu ("horn") and copiae ("abundance," "plenty"). By definition, the cornucopia is the horn of plenty.

Amulet Uses

A horn from a cow, sheep, or ram carried as an amulet wards against the evil eye, promotes virility, and attracts plenty.

Origin Myth

Allegedly, the very first cornucopia was the horn of Amaltheia, the goat that suckled Zeus. He placed it in the sky as a constellation in honor of his wet-nurse.
