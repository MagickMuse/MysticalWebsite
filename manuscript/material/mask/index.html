🎃Halloween

Masks serve so many spiritual and magickal functions that they represent one of humanity's most ancient and universal magickal tools. The invention of the mask is so primordial as to be unknown and unknowable.

Ancient Origins

How old are masks? Masks are as old as art, religion, spirituality, and magick. They appear all over Earth and are common, in one form or another, to virtually every human culture.

  • A cave painting in Lascaux in southwest France (dated to c.17000–12000 BCE) depicts a birdheaded man, generally assumed to be a shaman wearing a bird mask. He is depicted near a birdcrowned staff, similar to modern African magickal and spiritual staffs.
  • The oldest known surviving mask is estimated to be about ten thousand years old. It depicts Coyote and was found in what is now Mexico.

Materials

Masks are crafted from and embellished with:

  • Wood, metal, fabric, leather, hemp
  • Clay, quartz and other crystals
  • Feathers, seashells, papier mâché
  • Stone, beads
  • Animal hair and horns

Spiritual and Magickal Functions

Shamanic Tools: Masks are portals to other realms and existences; they enable spiritual possession. Putting on the mask enables a person to enter the realm of the sacred, to become another person or another being.

Ceremonial Objects: Some masks are believed to possess or radiate their own personal power, such as Balinese barong masks.

Power Items: Masks are the receptacle of divine force or the manifestation of normally invisible divine forces. For centuries, masks were the most precious, valuable possessions of many spiritual traditions around the globe.

Purposes

Masks are created for countless purposes:

  • Used in religious ceremonies
  • Serve as talismanic shields; some are believed to deflect malevolent spirits
  • Have served as votive offerings
  • Used to cover faces of the dead; death masks cast from corpses but preserved by the living may have served as oracles or in necromantic rituals
  • Components of various magick spells, especially those for healing

Protection and Privacy

During the witch-hunt era, many witches wore full- or half-masks when attending gatherings or dances in order to maintain anonymity and prevent identification. Many wealthy nobles allegedly wore masks while attending witches' balls, whether as participants or observers (the medieval equivalent of slumming) to protect their privacy and prevent blackmail. The concept of the masked ball is believed rooted in witches' balls.

Historical and Cultural Examples

Horned Shamanic Masks: Continued to exist in Europe until the twentieth century when most finally ended up as children's toys (see Krampus).

Schemenlaufen Festival: Austria's most famous masked festival. "Witch masks" are worn. In medieval times, masked figures chased malevolent spirits away to ensure a good harvest. The festival is still held every three years in the town of Imst.

Central and South American Traditions: Ancient pre-Christian masks are now worn during Christian festivals; many of these festivals, however, are rooted in indigenous traditions. Sixteenth-century Spanish priests disapproved of masks representing ancestors and spirits. Horns were added to the masks, which were then renamed "devils."

Russian Traditions: In Russia, masks were traditionally associated with Pagan ritual, especially with mid-winter festivities. Tsar Ivan the Terrible was accused of dancing in masks as an example of his alleged sorcery.

Hekataion: A carved wooden image of Hecate. The earliest may have been a pole or post hung with masks, perhaps facing in three directions, placed where three roads met.

Artemis Masks: In Italy and Sparta, masks were associated with Artemis. Clay masks discovered in the Temple of Artemis Orthia in Sparta, dating from the seventh and early sixth centuries BCE, were made in imitation of wooden masks used in rituals and performances dedicated to Artemis.

Theater and Archetype

In ancient Greece and Rome the Chorus wore masks of archetypal emotion, the source of the familiar comedy and tragedy faces, while the masks of Japanese Noh theater portray universal types such as the old man, the young girl, the mother, and the father. A Persian sect called the Maskhara wore animal masks or blackened their faces during henbane-fueled rites, the anonymity helping them surrender to the drug.

A Dangerous Practice

Because a mask can so readily dissolve the ego, its ritual use was considered perilous. Wearing a mask of the "dark" or base aspect was thought to call those qualities forth in the wearer, and since the skin or head of a beast was believed to hold the creature's essence, the animal's spirit might come to possess whoever wore it.

Living Traditions

Among the Dogon of Mali the Konoga masks, whose name means "hand of God," are worn in masked dances that reenact God's making of the Universe. The Kachina masks of the Hopi are worn to welcome the benevolent spirits that dwell among the people for half of each year.

Modern Masks

The most famous modern Western masks are those associated with Halloween costumes and with Carnival/Mardi Gras traditions. Both derive from ancient shamanism. Halloween masks are now largely mass-produced. Fine Carnival masks are still handcrafted from beads, sequins, and (especially) feathers.
