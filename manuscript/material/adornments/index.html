Overview

The precious metals and gems that make up adornments are drawn from the deep places of the Earth. Old legends say the gems are mined by dwarves and the pieces fashioned by elves and goblins. Both metal and stone carry hidden meaning, standing not only for wealth but for wisdom and the riches of mind and spirit; in Buddhism the core doctrines are themselves called jewels. Like the stones, the worked materials are seen as eternal and incorruptible, and ancient pieces often look as fresh as the day they were made.

People have adorned themselves for as long as forty thousand years, the earliest pieces made from shells, bones, and teeth and valued so highly that the dead were buried still wearing them. Adornment is also practical: it fastens clothing as buckles, brooches, pins, and clips, and it lets wealth be carried on the body. It can take the form of protective Amulet and Talisman, many designed to turn away the Evil Eye. It marks status and belonging, from the wedding ring as a sign of binding to the jet mourning jewelry of the Victorians, and in Rome the Sumptuary Laws even dictated who could wear which kinds.

Because adornment can carry faith, it deserves respect. Religious pieces such as a crucifix or a Star of David, and cultural adornment like the nose ring, drawn from Indian traditions where piercing is linked to fertility, have at times been misread where their meaning is not understood. Understanding why people wear what they wear can only bring more harmony between cultures.

Rings

A ring is a Circle, and so it carries the meaning of that shape: eternity, unity, and the wholeness of the universe. It has long been tied to sun, moon, and zodiac symbolism and worn as a protective object to ward off negativity. Above all it signals a link or a bond, the wedding ring being the clearest example; in J.R.R. Tolkien's The Fellowship of the Ring the one ring is inscribed one ring to bind them, and the Fisherman's Ring, worn only by the Pope as his personal seal, is broken when he dies. The signet ring carries this further, engraved with a seal or device as a mark of identity.

Some rings are vessels of power. Solomon owned a famously magickal ring said to be the source of all his wisdom, with which he summoned the demons that became his slaves; when he lost it his wisdom vanished until it was returned. Plato tells of the shepherd Gyges, whose ring made him invisible when its bezel was turned inward.

Magickal practitioners have traditionally removed rings before ritual work, believing they bind energy within the body and block its release during spellwork and invocation. Modern practice cares more for a ring's material and design than its appearance or value. The finger matters too: the fourth finger of the left hand, set aside for the wedding or engagement ring, was believed to connect directly to the Heart and to amplify healing, while the middle finger was thought unlucky. Archers in China and Persia wore rings to guard the thumb, so a thumb ring came to mark military rank, and a ring on the index or pointing finger signals authority. In India, toe rings, or bichiya, mark status; worn on the second toe of each foot they show that a woman is married, and because gold is traditionally not worn below the waist they are usually silver.

Necklaces

A necklace is a sign of identity, more visible and immediate than a ring. It can mark a chain of office, like the ornate mayoral chain, or a chain of bondage, like the collars once forced on the enslaved. Worn close to the heart, it lends itself to emotional work and love magick, and in contemporary Wiccan practice stone necklaces represent reincarnation and the Goddess, several stones together amplifying their combined energies more powerfully than any single one.

Necklaces also carry power and memory. The goddess Kali is known at once by her necklace of human skulls, and witches have traditionally worn a necklace of acorns. Amulets and talismans often hang as pendants, and lockets of every kind hide secret information close to the heart.

Earrings

Earrings were first worn to guard the ears from negativity and disease, though they later became markers of enslavement. Piercing the ears is one of humanity's oldest forms of body modification. Folklore credits pierced earrings with strengthening the eyes, especially when set with emerald, and gold earrings were thought to ease headaches, with some traditions recommending one gold and one silver earring for the purpose.
