Rune Media & Materials

Runes carry an air of permanence. They can be additive (painted onto surfaces) or subtractive (carved, removing material).

Natural Inorganic Materials

  • Stone, rock, minerals
  • Metal (silver, gold, iron)
  • Example: Gallehus drinking horns (gold, Old Norse inscription), discovered 17th-18th century, stolen 19th century for gold value.

Natural Organic Materials

  • Wood (staves, branch cross-sections)
  • Bone and antler (deer, sheep, buffalo)
  • Shell
  • Paper (cellulose plant material)

Modern Options

  • Semiprecious stones with metallic staves
  • Ceramic tiles
  • Gems and crystals (add energetic properties used in meditation, grounding, spellcraft)

Old Norse Terminology for Rune Media

The connection between runes and the materials they were carved upon runs deep in the language itself. Three Old Norse words illustrate this bond:

  • Stafr: Stave, letter, secret lore. The word for a piece of wood is inseparable from the word for the rune inscribed upon it.
  • Teinn: Twig, talismanic word for divination.
  • Hlutr: Lot for divination, talismanic object, on which runes were also carved.

Wood was the most common medium for magickal runic work. Stone talismans exist but are relatively few; the large runestones served cultic and funerary purposes. Metal was extremely popular for talismanic work, especially bracteates: thin metal disks inscribed with runes and various designs, representing an important tradition in magickal practice. Utilitarian metal objects (particularly swords) were also inscribed with runes to impart special power, enhancing their function or protecting the user. Bone objects, typically connected with magickal practices, are not uncommon. Runes were carved with knife points or special pointed instruments dedicated to the runic art.
