Rune Grimoire

A Brief History of Runology

Runology, the systematic study of runes, originated with Johannes Bureus (1568-1652). As a member of the Swedish court, Bureus first proposed that runes served as vessels for ancient wisdom beyond their standard alphabetic function. Despite his Christian faith, he maintained that runes possessed mystical properties. While modern scholars often reject these esoteric associations, excluding them ignores a fundamental component of runic history.

Runic writing and speech declined by the late Middle Ages, yet the symbols retained their significance. By the mid-nineteenth century, runology emerged as a formal academic field. This multidisciplinary study incorporates linguistics, archaeology, anthropology, and philology to analyze runes as symbols, sounds, objects, and oracles.

Stages of Runic Development

The runic system may have been fully developed as early as 200 BCE. The magickal and religious practices of the ancient Germanic priesthood relied on many runic and pre-runic signs, still visible etched into stone across Scandinavia.

Rune development moved through three overlapping stages, all of which remain valid for modern practice:

  1. Ideographic. The earliest period, and the cradle of rune magick. Individual ideographs appeared in isolation, each one a self-contained symbol of sacred meaning.
  2. Sound-formulaic (galdrar). Magickal sound and number formulas began to be produced, combining runes into sequences intended to generate specific magickal effects.
  3. Phonetic. Runes were adapted to represent the common Germanic dialect in writing. This phonetic function arrived last, layered on top of the existing magickal system.

All three modes continued in use simultaneously. The runes were born from a magickal tradition, not a purely linguistic one, and that magickal association never left them. Runes have been found carved on wood, stone, metal, and bone. The vast majority of runic talismans were crafted in wood, which decayed quickly, meaning the surviving archaeological record represents only a fraction of what once existed.

The Rök Runestone

Sweden holds the majority of surviving runic inscriptions. The Rök Runestone, carved circa 800 C.E., represents a significant discovery in the field. Recent analysis interprets its text as the narrative of the Sun Goddess, whom a great wolf slew. Odin commands humanity to avenge her death. This myth links directly to the climate catastrophe following a massive volcanic eruption in 536 C.E. that obscured the sun for over a decade. The inscription describes a new Goddess rising to replace her mother, preserving the memory of this environmental disaster through ancient ciphers.

The Kylver Stone

The historical record includes over four hundred examples of the Futhark alphabet. The Kylver Stone in Gotland, Sweden, stands as a premier example. Dating to the fifth century, this carving displays the complete sequence of the Elder Rune Row.

Runic Artifacts and Trade Routes

Approximately 2,500 runestones are known, out of a total of nearly 5,000 runic artifacts. These stones were raised as sacred landmarks and memorials across Scandinavian countries, with controversial examples also found in the United States. Runic inscriptions have marked Nordic trade routes from Eastern Europe to Greenland and from the Arctic Circle to Greece and Constantinople.

Uses of Runes, Then and Now

Beyond historical records, ancients used runes for magick. Applications included weather influence, battle protection, crop harvesting, and love attraction. Runes blessed, honored, and protected people and places. These ancient purposes validate a modern runic tradition. The human condition remains largely unchanged. We still grapple with climate change, loss, and the need for protection. Runes remain valuable tools in these pursuits.

Ethics and Power

Modern practitioners must reconcile runic power with its potential for misuse. Some groups attempt to use runes as emblems of domination. This exclusion is particularly acute for witches, who historically faced scapegoating for societal maladies.

Domination of one group over another contradicts moral magickal practice. Closing oneself off from the collective culture denies wisdom. Practitioners must approach runes with awareness of the sacredness and validity of all beings. This inclusivity is key to successful divination.

The Younger Rune Row

Language complexity ironically led to runic simplification. Around the tenth century dawn of the Viking age, the Elder Rune Row (24 characters) evolved into the Younger Rune Row (16 characters). This system uses only four vowel sounds. Notably, the Elhaz rune appears inverted in this row.

Form and Variation

The Younger Futhark is also known as "long branch" (Danish term) or "staveless" runes. The system emphasizes economy. Each character uses a single vertical stave with curved or crossed offshoots, allowing for quick carving. Because the alphabet is concise, single characters represent multiple sounds.

Variations include "short twig" runes (Scandinavian term), which resemble script. Inscriptions with dotted characters suggest further sound differentiations.

Expansion and Evolution

Runic usage expanded beyond Scandinavia. Explorers carried writing systems across the seas to places like the Isle of Man. Syncretism took root as Latin phrases appeared in runic carvings and variations blended together.

The evolution of runes is counterintuitive. Instead of growing in complexity, the system became smaller and more simplified until falling out of common use. Note that terms like Younger Rune Row, Younger Futhark, and Norse runes are often used interchangeably.

Runes in Manuscripts

Few runic manuscripts survive, and fewer carry overt magickal intent, though they may reflect magickal practices. The rune poems found in Anglo-Saxon and Scandinavian traditions are of extreme importance for understanding each rune's symbolic meaning.

The oldest manuscript containing runic symbols is the Abecedarium Nordmannicum from the ninth century CE. The Codex Runicus, a fourteenth-century Danish manuscript written entirely in runes, contains the laws of the province of Sconia. During the Swedish phase of the Thirty Years War (1630-1635), the forces of Gustavus Adolphus used runes as a cipher to confuse Austrian intelligence.

Runes in Art and Architecture

Glyph runes frequently appear within runic inscriptions. The most common is the Thórshamarr (the hammer of Thor), also called the fylfot. This symbol represents the dynamic magickal force of the Æsir and the explosive power of the Thunderer. The sun wheel or cross is equally ancient, appearing from the Bronze Age onward. This common European symbol originally denoted holy places and was later adopted by Christians in their conversion efforts.

In the twentieth century, the Nazis revived certain runes as manipulative tools, adopting the Hakenkreuz (hook-cross) and a doubled S-rune as identifying marks. These appropriations are widely regarded as imbalanced misdirections of runic forces. The S-rune, for instance, is a rune of the sun, not of "victory."

Runic forms continue surfacing in modern life. The peace symbol popularized in the 1960s takes the form of the Ýr-rune of the Younger Futhark, an alternate form of Eihwaz and Elhaz from the Elder row. This shape became a symbol of "death" in early twentieth-century German runology and was used as such by anti-Nazi activists during the war. The bright blue Star of Life carried on ambulances echoes the Hagall rune in its later letter-rune form.

Germanic half-timbered architecture (Fachwerk) preserves runic shapes in its structural timbers. Originally, builders placed the timbers to form specific rune shapes, imparting that rune's power to the building and its inhabitants. The practice continued long after builders forgot the reason behind the configurations, surviving as simple tradition. The familiar Dutch hex signs also derive ultimately from a runic source.

The golden ritual drinking horns of Gallehus depict human figures in stances and postures that represent cultic signs and runes. These figures illustrate the ránastödhur (sometimes called "runic asanas"), bodily postures through which the vitki physically embodies runic forms. The Herrgott von Bentheim (Lord God of Bentheim) offers another example: a piece of statuary based on a wooden original brought from a nearby Thing-stead, its arms bent in the shape of an Anglo-Frisian rune. Though later given Christian significance, it is still used today as an object on which solemn oaths are sworn.

The Three Futhark Codifications

Three ancient codifications of the letter runes exist:

  1. The Elder Futhark (24 runes). The original system, in use from approximately 200 BCE to 800 CE.
  2. The Anglo-Saxon or Frisian Futhork (up to 33 runes). An expansion of the Elder system that survived the Christianization process until the tenth century CE.
  3. The Younger (Norse) Futhark (16 runes). A condensed system that began developing in the seventh century CE and was complete by approximately 800 CE.

Both the Anglo-Saxon and Younger systems are modifications of the Elder Futhark. The methodical manner in which the order and correspondences of the runes were maintained across all three systems strongly indicates that these modifications took place within a traditionally controlled cult. Across the various systems, individual rune shapes and names were sometimes altered, offering valuable clues and correspondences to their inner nature. All three systems remain magickally valid.

House Marks and Heraldry

After the Middle Ages, runes were driven underground in continental Germanic territory, finding expression through graphic arts attributed to the early nobility and farmers. House marks are symbolic or literal monograms formed from two or more runes, often stylistically modified. They served as property identification, inscribed into objects owned by lord and freeman alike. Unlike crests and coats-of-arms, a house mark could be drawn without color and by an unpracticed hand.

The history of house marks traces a three-stage degeneration:

  1. Cultic period (through the mid-fifteenth century). Marks were still formed and interpreted according to ancient runes and holy signs. Their value was highly symbolic and sacred.
  2. Phonetic period (mid-fifteenth to mid-eighteenth century). Runic letter forms spelling a surname or initials were welded together into a single composite shape. The deeper symbolic value had faded, and runes functioned only as phonetic symbols.
  3. Latin period (after the mid-eighteenth century). Runic forms were dropped entirely. Latin script replaced them, typically forming a person's initials.

Stonemasons' signs, merchants' signs, and masters' signs may in some cases be interpreted according to the same rules that govern house marks. This three-stage decline reveals how runic knowledge gradually eroded into modern times, but by retracing the path, the practitioner can recover some of what would otherwise have been lost.

Heraldic art also conceals runic forms in two ways: through the color pattern composing a coat-of-arms, or through symbolic figures that encode a rune's concept without reproducing its visible shape.

The Runic Revival

Many runic practices and much rune lore continued in various forms long after the arrival of Christianity, but the systematic transmission of sacred rune knowledge belonging to the ancient vitkar and eriloz slowly disappeared. By the nineteenth century, only scattered pockets of rune singers remained in the remotest areas of Scandinavia. Yet the runic mysteries are indigenous to the innermost selves of their people. This inner rune lore could never be eliminated entirely; it only retreated into the deepest shelters of human consciousness, waiting to be reborn.

As the Völuspá (stanza 59) describes the renewed world after Ragnarök:

Again the Æsir meet
on Idha-plain,
and speak of the mighty
Midgard-serpent,
and again remember
the mighty World-doom
and Fimbultýr's
elder runes.

The Armanen Movement

At the dawn of the twentieth century, the Pan-Germanic mystic Guido von List received a spontaneous runic initiation while recovering from a cataract operation. Lying in darkness with bandaged eyes for several months, the "secrets of the runes" were revealed to him. From 1902 until his death in 1919, List worked toward restoring what he called Armanentum. He founded the Guido-von-List-Gesellschaft to support his investigations and the Armanen Orden to carry out its esoteric functions.

List developed a magickal system of eighteen runes (the Armanen system) based solely on the textual authority of the Rune-Song in the Eddic Hávamál. He maintained this was the primal system, though no hard evidence beyond the Hávamál supports that claim. List's personal force and the influence of his Armanen Orden shaped the runic theories of German magickians from that time forward.

List's aims were often political, and his theories incorporated anti-Semitic ideas prevalent in his era. His runic teachings were later absorbed into the Germanen Orden, an esoteric precursor to the National Socialist movement.

Kummer, Marby, and Runic Yoga

List's ideas were further developed by two magickians, Siegfried Adolf Kummer and Friedrich Bernhard Marby, who created a system of "runic yoga" or runic gymnastics. Their publications and experiments laid foundations for a practical system, though they retained the Armanen rune row and reinforced its associated prejudices. Rudolf John Gorsleben produced an enormous compilation of rune lore based on the Armanen system as well. The early 1930s mark the twilight of this first esoteric runic movement.

The Nazi Period

In 1933, the Nazi regime began, and by 1935 the Ahnenerbe (Institute for Ancestral Heritage) was formed around the ideas of Professor Hermann Wirth, among others. During the Nazi regime, all things Germanic were mobilized toward politically manipulative ends. The runic forces themselves were used far less than some believe. The Ahnenerbe and Totenkopf Orden drew more heavily on Judeo-Christian and Manichean ideas in their magickal traditions and organizational principles.

The Nazis employed ancient Germanic symbolism (which was very popular at the time) and infused it with non-Germanic concepts for manipulative purposes, mirroring the very technique Christian evangelists had used centuries before: adopting old pagan symbols to convert the populace, then gradually replacing their original significance with contrary meanings. Runes and holy signs were abundant in Nazi symbolism, but the degree to which this perverted the tribal systems of the ancients into a totalitarian imperial scheme is evident at a glance.

The practical use of runes as a system of magick and personal development virtually died out or was driven underground during this period.

Spiesberger and the Post-War Reformation

After the war, the Armanen system was revived and reformed by the German magickian Karl Spiesberger, a widely qualified occultist who authored works in both the Hermetic and runic traditions. Spiesberger synthesized the work of all the German runic magickians who preceded him within a pansophical framework. He eliminated all the racist and volkisch elements while retaining the Armanen system of eighteen runes, which by the 1950s had become nearly traditional in German circles.

This eighteen-rune system forms a valid and working magickal system. However, it is not a traditional form, and to some extent it remains cut off from the innate powers slumbering in the ancient runes and their system. Whereas the Armanen tradition dates only from around 1904 CE, the oldest runic evidence extends roughly two thousand years earlier.

The Return to the Elder Tradition

Documents from the Viking and pre-Viking ages provide ample clues about how the runes were used, but the Armanen framework cannot fully accommodate this material because it is the product of a different organic system. The elder tradition is known to have worked, and to have worked for hundreds of years within well-developed cults. Unlocking those ancient runic treasure mounds, where the hoard of rune wisdom has been stored over millennia, is the most fruitful path forward. The psychic keys provided by the pioneering technical work of modern magickians in the Armanen tradition remain extremely helpful in this endeavor.

Traditional Rune Lore and Ásatrú

The traditional runic systems are rooted in the three ancient Futharks. The magickal system and cosmology of the Elder Futhark (twenty-four signs) draws its modern rebirth from a synthesis of comparative religion, anthropology, archaeology, and linguistics (collectively known as linguistic paleontology), combined with the methods of magick. This holistic view also incorporates the natural sciences.

The ancient eriloz still speak through their runestones and through the mind runes (ON hugrunir) they encode in consciousness. Their magickal system, however, was only part of a much larger cosmological pattern encompassing all the known and unknown worlds. For the ancient Germanic tribes, magick, religion, and law (including social organization) were expressions of the same basic divine force. For traditional magickal forms to be truly reborn, a vessel for this holistic conception first had to be nurtured.

That vessel arrived on May 16, 1973, when the Ásatrúarmenn ("those trusting in the Æsir") was founded in Iceland. Though precursors existed earlier in the decade, this event crystallized the movement now known as Ásatrú (the faith of the Æsir). Early major organizations included groups in Iceland, California, and England. Over time these larger bodies have evolved, disbanded, and reformed, with the movement shifting increasingly toward localized and regionalized tribal organizations rather than large international structures.

These groups maintain a healthy independence from one another, though this independence has at times devolved into polarized frictions. The organizations are primarily religious in orientation, and their remarkable breadth of expression and deliberate lack of dogmatism defies quick summary. The efficiency of any runic magickal system is strengthened by an understanding of the holistic principles of this faith, since this is the atmosphere in which rune lore was first practiced.

A runic order known as the Rune-Gild has existed since 1979 and now operates worldwide. As in ancient times when the runes brought people of various tribes and lineages together in the search for the mysteries, the problems of cultural polarization found elsewhere tend not to be an issue within this tradition.
