Rune Grimoire

Icelandic Stave Symbols

Overview

Early Icelandic grimoires, the magickal handbooks full of spells and occult lore, are filled with long lists of curious, angular signs known as staves. Each was drawn for a specific purpose, and their roots reach back to the ancient rune system, with a measure of later medieval and Renaissance magick woven in. Popularly called Magickal Staves, or galdrastafir, some are bind runes built from several runic signs merged into a single figure, while others stand entirely on their own.

A Book of Household Magick

The intentions behind the staves are wonderfully varied, with one for almost every occasion. They read like a magickal book of household management and tell the story of daily life down the years: the importance of a good catch for fishermen, protection against thieves and ghosts, ways to frighten off enemies, and even charms to keep butter wholesome, to break locks, and to raise the dead.

Notable Staves

Two of the best known staves have their own entries in the Rune Grimoire:

  • Helm of Awe (Aegishjalmur): an eight-armed sign, like a snowflake, meant to instil fear in enemies and to guard the wearer against the misuse of their own power. It became a cornerstone symbol of the Asatru faith.
  • Hulinhjalmur: a stave said to grant invisibility, recorded in the old texts with an infamous and grisly recipe and no counter stave to undo its effect.

A Word of Caution

The old stave recipes belong to their time and are preserved here as historical lore, not as instructions. Some, like Hulinhjalmur, call for ingredients that should never be sought or recreated. Study the staves for their history and symbolism rather than as literal workings.
