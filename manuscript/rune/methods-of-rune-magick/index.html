Methods of Rune Magick

Runes may be magickally employed in a wide variety of ways, all of which remain valid and potent for modern practice.

Talismanic Magick

The most common method of rune magick in ancient times was talismanic: runes were inscribed into objects and infused with psychic power to effect change in the vitki or their environment. Hundreds of runic talismans have survived, and studying them reveals deep insight into the magickal techniques used to create them.

One dramatic example comes from the Egilssaga (chapter 44): the hero Egill suspects poison in a drink offered to him, so he stabs his palm, carves runes on the drinking horn, and colors the runic forms with his blood. The horn shatters and the poison flows away.

The Tine as a Living Being

A rune tine is not merely an inscribed object. It is a living being with an ørlög bestowed upon it by the vitki. The runester gives the object life and then provides it with ørlög through the nature of the runic power used to load it. The living nature of the tine may become so strongly enforced that it develops a discernible personality. To facilitate this state of autonomous, vitki-willed force, the runester may choose to give the tine a name during the loading ritual. This is the mystery behind the many runic talismans, especially weapons, that bear personal names.

Loading and Unloading

The technical theories behind tine magick align perfectly with the laws of action within the runic cosmology. The rune tine acts as a key to unlock the power of particular rune streams. During the loading process, these streams (identical with hamingja) are willfully blended in the causal worlds and infused into the object, which has been prepared by the vitki with signs and staves receptive to those forces. There the streams are intensified or modified and released again, bearing a specific character imparted by the ørlög-giving galdrar and formalár of the vitki and the innate power of the symbols depicted on the tine. The talismanic form becomes linked to the essence of the particular rune or runes through a great concentration and energizing of forces directed by the vitki into the tine, using the shape, sound, and color of the runes.

Once the tine has been properly loaded, this power is then unloaded according to the form the vitki impressed upon it. The object becomes the center of a vortex of force, receiving energy, formulating it in accordance with its ørlög, and re-expressing it in the causal realms to produce the desired result. This power may also be retained within the personal sphere. The efficiency of the process depends on the level of strength in the vitki's hamingja and the quality of concentration and visualization brought to bear during the loading operation.

Death of Rune Tines

In most runic formalar, not only should the definite purpose of the talismanic being be stated but also the length of time the wight is to live, that is, be vivified with magickal force. A convenient formula for this duration is "until thy work is wrought."

Since the wight of the tine is a living being, its death should be attended with proper ritual. This ensures that the magickal force stored in the form is redirected back to its source (the vitki), or offered as a form of sacrifice.

There are two principal methods for effecting this important ritual act:

1. Funeral Rites of Reabsorption

This method emphasizes the animate nature of the tine wight and is modeled on funeral rites. There are two types:

  • Cremation: Most effective for returning power to the personal sphere of the vitki through the heavenly streams
  • Burial: A powerful way to direct the power through the chthonic streams

2. Ritual Removal of the Runes

This method emphasizes the dynamistic nature of the rune might. The runes are ritually removed from the tine using the knife, and the scrapings are burned in the brazier. This is also a method of banishing the rune magick of another runester.

In all cases, this should be done with simple dignity, attended by proper formalar of the vitki's own composition. Proper respect should be paid to the wight, the rune might, and the shaper of the tine. This "ecology of power" mirrors the ancient Norse lore of rebirth, which postulates that the innate might of the ancestors is continually reformed in the descendants.

Magickal Linkage

Another vital aspect of tine magick is the establishment of a magickal link to the person or thing the taufr is intended to affect. This linkage may be created by attaching a runic formula representing the target, such as a name transliterated into runes, to the object. Physical proximity of the tine to the intended recipient also establishes a connection. Other techniques of sympathetic magick may be employed as well.

Talismanic Construction

Before attempting to construct a runic talisman, the vitki should be well versed in the intellectual content of the rune lore and suitably advanced in the psychic faculties necessary to a successful talismanic operation. Rune tines should be constructed in accordance with the theories and ideology expressed by the runic system to achieve maximal effect.

Materials

  • Wood: Should be of a kind sympathetic to the aim of the talisman. The possibilities are limitless; consult correspondences or let informed intuition guide the selection.
  • Metal: Disks, plates, or rings of copper, bronze, silver, or gold make excellent runic talismans.
  • Stone and Bone: Small, appropriately shaped stones or pieces of bone are also favored. Larger stones serve as stationary talismans; a hammer and chisel dedicated to the runic arts are useful for constructing runestones.
  • Earthenware: Highly receptive to rune loading. Runes may be etched into the finished product or cut into the soft unfired clay, colored properly, and then fired in a ritualistic process of powerful potential.
  • Parchment: Colored with pens, inks, and paints dedicated to ritual practice. Parchment talismans may be carried (in lockets, for example) or serve as stationary symbols.

The imagination of the vitki is the only limit to the possibilities.

Shapes

Certain shapes are best suited to receive runic forms:

  • Rectangular solid
  • Thin wooden stave (1/16 to 1/8 inch thick)
  • Thin disk or rectangular plate
  • Natural tree branch segment
  • Cylindrical shape of various lengths
  • Jewelry of all types
  • Thin rhomboid: A unique and fairly common talismanic shape, usually cut from wood or bone. This form provides four smooth surfaces for longer inscriptions and is extremely convenient and comfortable for tines designed to be carried on the person.

Design Requirements

The main requirement in the external formation of a tine is that it contain:

  1. A symbol or symbols describing the purpose and aims of the talisman
  2. A signature representing the person, persons, or thing to be affected or altered by the force of the first symbol

The signature may be the target's name transliterated into runes, or some other sympathetic link; even physical proximity may serve to form this connection. Space on the object should be aesthetically allocated and divided according to rune staves, holy signs, and the signature. Any combination of these elements is acceptable.

Cutting Technique

The vitki should experiment with various surfaces and tools to determine the best cutting methods for each material. Time invested in the practice of carving runes pays dividends, as greater mechanical skill frees more energy and concentration for the magickal work at hand. One general technique that works well across all material types is the precutting of rune bands: cutting two grooves to act as upper and lower limits for the staves.

Bind Runes

While a tine may express a single runic force, one of the most potent techniques for blending several runic forces together toward a specific purpose is the bind rune (Old Norse bandrún). A bind rune combines individual runic powers into one mighty field of action. This method has the distinct advantage of using only the ideographic essence of the rune, freeing the modern vitki from concerns about linguistic correctness in inscription. To properly build and load such a form, the vitki must possess a deep understanding of the separate runes and how they fit together to produce a single powerful expression of force with a single harmonious will. The principles of spiritual and physical aesthetics are important here.

There are two main types of bandrúnar. The first type connects two or more runes together when inscribing words. This is done to form a magickal link between the runes, to represent two or more words in a coded form, or to reduce the overall count of runes in the inscription. A bind rune always counts as a single rune in the rune count. The common grammatical ending -az (-aR) is frequently written as a bind rune, linking the forces of the A-rune and Z-rune into a special expression. One of the oldest bind runes represents the magickal formula gibu auja (give good luck) and is often carved on talismanic or ritual weapons. Any runes with adjacent vertical staves are prime candidates for binding.

The second type is the purely ideographic bind rune, which may contain a word concealed in its form as a kind of simultaneous anagram. These are the most useful in tine magick, and their multiplicity of levels makes them highly effective in refined operations. A great amount of artistic license is available to the vitki in the construction of bind runes. The alternate forms of the various staves should help in the formation of an aesthetically pleasing shape. When formulating bind runes, the runester should always keep the elements of numerological symbolism and ideological harmony in mind.

One of the oldest examples of an ideographic bind rune is found on the brooch of Soest, dating to approximately 600 to 650 CE. This galdrastafr is a love talisman carved on a brooch and given to a woman. Its power draws upon Ódhinic force combined with justice, a call for success, need (with sexual symbolism), erotic union, and ancestral principles. The rune count and its numerical total emphasize the erotic nature of the talisman. Analysis of old inscriptions such as this provides many clues to modern practice.

Another ideographic example is the "three-headed thurs," essentially a threefold intensification of the Thurisaz rune. This form is used in curses.

Runagaldrar (Runic Incantations)

Poetic runic incantations were a common magickal method. The Poetic Edda, along with numerous runic artifacts, preserves the voice of many ancient incantations.

Stadhagaldr (Posture Magick)

Stadhagaldr is the practice of physically embodying runic forms through posture and incantation. Evidence of this practice appears on the golden drinking horns of Gallehus, which portray humanoid figures striking runic postures as part of magickal formulas. Later Icelandic customs confirm this usage, such as teaching children the alphabet by having them strike a posture resembling each letter.

Magickal Applications

The runes and holy signs serve as focal points for evocatory magick, meditation, self-transmutation, and mystical communication. They are especially potent in workings concerning:

  • Victory and success
  • Protection
  • Rescue from restriction
  • Love
  • The gaining of wisdom

Other Forms of Magick

The runic system encompasses several areas beyond the methods described above. Their scope is vast, and awareness of them opens the door to further exploration.

Oath Magick is bound up with the liturgical forms of Ásatrú. It involves the swearing of oaths upon sacred objects such as the altar ring or a sacrificial animal, channeling binding force through the spoken word and the sanctity of the object.

Invocation and Evocation of gods, goddesses, wights, and aspects of the personal soul may be performed to communicate with the hugr of the vitki. These forms belong more properly to the function of the erilaz, the master of rune lore who works at the highest ceremonial level. Several types of initiation are also proper to the life of the vitki and erilaz.

Seidhr is a magickal form independent of runic symbolism, though it can be woven into the rune world as an integral practice. The Ynglinga Saga recounts how Ódhinn learned the art of seidhr from its mistress, Freyja. Seidhr encompasses shape shifting, soul projection, traveling through the worlds, sex magick, and other techniques that carry what some consider a shamanistic character. The Eddas and sagas offer many inspirational examples for the vitki who wishes to explore these paths.
