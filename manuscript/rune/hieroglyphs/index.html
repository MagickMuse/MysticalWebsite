Overview

The pyramid texts are among the earliest known examples of symbols used with magical intent, inscribed as spells to guide the soul of the deceased into the Afterlife. Hieroglyphs are still widely used in magical scripts some five thousand years later.

The word comes from the Greek for "sacred carvings," while the Egyptians themselves called the symbols the "Speech of the Gods." Hieroglyphs are pictorial motifs, used at first for the magical purposes described above, which is how they earned their name.

Mastery of hieroglyphic writing was no simple matter, for there were around nine hundred pictorial symbols, and generally only the priestly castes held the skill to unlock their mysteries. A script reserved for everyday, more secular use was called demotic, a simplified form of the hieratic script used by the priests.

In AD 391 the Roman emperor Theodosius closed all non-Christian temples, driving the final nail into the sarcophagus for hieroglyphs, whose use had already been fading. The Rosetta Stone, unearthed by Napoleon's troops in 1799, proved invaluable to deciphering both hieroglyphic and demotic writing, since it carried the same text in both forms alongside a Greek version that supplied the key.

Many hieroglyphs work on the principle that the sound of a word is shown by a picture: both "I" and "eye," for instance, could be drawn as an eye. This looks simple enough, the sort of rebus found in children's puzzle books, but once such pictograms are compounded into longer words and the cultural context of each symbol is added, the obstacles to full comprehension become clear.

Many well-known magical symbols were originally hieroglyphs whose meaning, once bound to an ancient language, has broadened into universal significance. The Ankh, for example, is recognized the world over as a symbol of eternal life and protection, a meaning that transcends the barriers of spoken language and culture.

The hieroglyphic alphabet in common use is based on the values assigned to the symbols by the renowned Egyptologist Sir E.A. Wallis Budge. The glyphs can serve as a cipher and appear on magical talismans and amulets.
