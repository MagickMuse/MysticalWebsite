🌳Ogham Tree Alphabet

Type
Ogham Runes
Also called
Tree Alphabet

Overview

The Ogham Tree alphabet wears its sacred nature in its name. It is named for Ogma, the Irish Celtic god of learning and poetry, who was said to have invented it, and "Ogham" itself means "language."

The alphabet has twenty-five characters, every one associated with a tree or shrub, which gives it its other name, the Tree alphabet. The "letters" are arranged in sets of five, and the whole is known as a "grove." This image speaks not only of a stand of trees but of the body of knowledge held within each one, every small part building toward a greater whole. It is no accident that the Celtic words for "knowledge" and "wood" sound so alike.

There is no firm evidence for the alphabet's origins. Some held that it was based on the runes, and the letters do resemble them, though this may simply be because straight lines are easiest to carve into wood. Surviving Ogham inscriptions are found across the British Isles and date between the fourth and seventh centuries AD, although the alphabet itself is probably older. The only records to endure are those cut into stone, since leather or bark would long since have decayed; some stones set the Ogham symbols beside Latin letters, and such markers were generally used to define the ownership and boundaries of land. The inscriptions are read from the bottom left-hand corner, upward, then across the top to the next vertical line.

The script is sometimes called the Beth-Luis-Nuin alphabet, much as we speak of our A-B-C. From the chart one might expect the name to be Beth-Luis-Fearn, but Beth-Luis-Nuin harks back to an older sequence of trees and letters.

Simple as the individual symbols are, each stands for a richer whole: along with its tree, every letter carries a hand signal, a spirit, and a concept. Like the runes, the Ogham letters are also used as a tool of divination. The idea of the Tree Alphabet has even been extended into a tree calendar; because the Druids measured their months by the phases of the Moon, that system has thirteen months.

The Magick of Trees

The wood of sacred trees was used in ritual fires and to make magick wands of power. Sabbath need-fires were traditionally made of nine woods: oak, ash, cherry, rowan, birch, holly, hawthorn, fir, and pine. Elder and willow were not burned, for it might result in very bad luck. A special fire for visions and pyromancy was called the Fire of Azrael, composed of juniper, cedar, and sandalwood.

Magick wands for various purposes were made from the branches of sacred trees. Willow wands were for Moon magick and divining for water, and hazel and hawthorn for general magick. The wood of sacred trees was also used for pentacles, knife handles, talismans, altars, statues, and other ritual objects. Branches, leaves, and flowers were also used as circle and temple decorations.

Because it was believed that dryads and other spirits lived in many trees, it was important to ask permission before cutting or picking parts of trees. Permission was granted by the tree's behavior. If the tree remained silent, permission was granted. If there was any sudden stirring of the branches or trembling of the leaves, permission was not granted. Cutting without permission would raise the ire of its fairy, dryad, or sprite, and bad luck would come the person's way.

The seven trees of the sacred Irish groves were alder, birch, willow, holly, hazel, oak, and apple.
