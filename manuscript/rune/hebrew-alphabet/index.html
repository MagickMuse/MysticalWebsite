Overview

The Kabbalah calls the Hebrew alphabet the "letters of the angels." Its sacred origin is told in the legend that Moses received it at the summit of Mount Sinai, in a moment of direct communication with God. As with other lettering systems such as the Greek Alphabet, Runic, and Ogham alphabets, every Hebrew letter holds specific concepts within it, both mundane and esoteric, and each also corresponds to a number, giving rise to the mystery tradition of Gematria.

There is a still more profound dimension, though: the alphabet is inseparable from the cohesive philosophical system of the Kabbalah, helping to encode that mystery tradition, itself a deeply rooted part of magical custom whose tendrils reach, web-like, through many esoteric disciplines.

Because of the dual meanings carried by the twenty-two letters, they serve as an everyday tool of communication while holding a second face for the initiate. Each letter conceals secrets, bound up not only with the allegories of the Kabbalah and of the Tarot (whose Major Arcana correspond to the letters) but also with the planets, the seasons and elements, the days of the week, and the stages and concerns of a human life. The alphabet also provides the means to spell and pronounce the Names of God.

From all this we can see that Hebrew was "invented" with mystical intent, each letter representing far more than the sum of its parts. It reads from right to left, and knowing this makes the characters a little more approachable for the neophyte. Once the meaning of a letter is known, its shape begins to suggest that meaning: the second letter, Beth, means "house," and plainly resembles one, while "house" in turn carries ideas of home, mother, and domesticity.

Because the alphabet carries a resonance born of nearly three thousand years of mystical use, it appears often in magical texts and grimoires as a handy means of concealing occult knowledge. Yet those who compile such texts are not always as learned as they seem, and Hebrew is frequently used not as a language at all but as a set of symbols, with English simply transcribed into Hebrew characters.
