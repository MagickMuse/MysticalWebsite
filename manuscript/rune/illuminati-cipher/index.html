Overview

For a supposedly secret society, the Illuminati are remarkably famous, though an air of mystery still clings to the name and much about the organization remains the subject of speculation. Founded in Bavaria in 1776 by Adam Weishaupt, it is arguably the focus of more controversy and hysteria than any other closed order, yet a look at the social, religious, and political climate of its founding, and at Weishaupt's reasons for shaping its tenets, helps to clarify some of what is actually known.

Weishaupt was a former Jesuit and a professor of law at Ingolstadt University. He has been called a Kabbalistic magician, an ungodly atheist, a fascist, and an anarchist, though Thomas Jefferson described him as an "enthusiastic philanthropist." He built the Illuminati from within existing Masonic orders, making it effectively a society within a society. As new and progressive ideas spread across Europe, he hoped the order would foster debate and discussion, choosing a name to reflect this ideal of enlightened individuals determined to better the world.

Membership grew slowly, in no small part because of the rigorous study demanded of members, the intent being that they would rise to positions of power well versed in philosophy and progressive thought and so help shape an idealized society. By 1784 the order had extended its influence across most of central Europe.

Secret scripts and ciphers naturally played a large part within the organization, given its known opposition to the religious and political ideals of the day. Members took secret names: Weishaupt was Spartacus, while others answered to Cato, Hermes Trismegistus, Menelaus, and Agrippa. They even kept their own calendar, based on an ancient Persian system. Initiates were first taught a relatively simple substitution cipher and required to write to their teachers in it until a more elaborate code was introduced.

In 1785 the Bavarian government banned secret organizations, naming the Illuminati directly. Weishaupt fled and instructed the lodges to go underground, and many members were imprisoned. The order's modern fame rests on two things: the Bavarian government's publication in 1786 of papers seized from the society, and the outbreak of the French Revolution in 1789, with the suggestion that the Illuminati had a hand in the era's turmoil.
