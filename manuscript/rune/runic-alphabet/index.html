Rune Grimoire

🗿Runic Alphabet

Type
Magical Alphabet
Also called
Futhark; Futhork

Overview

Also called the Futhark, or Futhork, alphabet, named for the sound of its first few letters much as the keyboard is nicknamed Qwerty, the runic alphabet has been studied at length for its divinatory properties, though the letters served secular as well as spiritual ends. The oldest writing of the ancient Germanic peoples, runes were used in Britain, Scandinavia, and Germany before the Latin alphabet superseded them. The early Christian Church destroyed many runic inscriptions, yet fine examples survive on ancient artifacts, standing stones, and the like.

The word "rune" comes from an Old English or Norse word meaning "mystery," "secret," or "whisper," and there is also a Finnish word, runo, meaning "song." Like other sacred alphabets, the runes were held to be of divine origin.

The Scandinavian epic poems known as the Eddas tell how the god Odin brought the runes to humankind after a strange ordeal: he hung from the great ash tree Yggdrasil for nine days until he saw the runic symbols reflected in the water below. The tale echoes the story of Edward Kelley glimpsing the Enochian Script while scrying with a crystal ball. So great was the power of the runes that they were said to be able to bring the dead back to life, and knowledge of them was likely an esoteric matter restricted at first to an elite few. Odin's own supernatural gifts, among them flight, shape-shifting, raising the dead, and seeing the future, were all credited to his mastery of the runes.

The shape of the runes is distinctive, built from upright strokes called staves and diagonal lines, with no horizontal lines at all, since they were first scored into wood, where cutting across the grain is difficult; later they were carved into rock and stone. Writing in AD 98 about the lands and customs of the Germanic peoples, the Roman historian Tacitus describes augury or divination by "lot," itself another meaning of the word "rune." He seems to be describing runes when he tells of small pieces of wood, usually cut from fruit trees, scored with distinguishing marks and cast onto a white cloth, then read to determine the will of the gods, though exactly how the runes were used for augury remains unclear.

Several runic systems exist, but the most familiar is the oldest, the Elder Futhark. Its twenty-four symbols each hold a small universe of meaning: every rune has a sound, a shape, and a name, both a mundane and a mystical meaning, and a connection to a god or spirit as well as to an idea or concept. Each rune can also stand for a whole word that carried real significance for the people who devised it. Where "a," "b," and "c" are merely sounds, the first three runes, Fehu, Uruz, and Thurisaz, are complete words meaning "cattle," "aurochs," and "giant." To read them well is to step back in time and sense the concerns of their makers, for whom survival, food, and protection from enemies and the elements stood uppermost; each rune, too, carries a complete story.

The material mattered as well. Ancient peoples believed everything on Earth was alive and animated by spirit, so the stone, wood, or leather bearing a rune added to the sacred status of the object. Runes were used for spell-casting, for healing and fertility, to sway the tides and the weather, to curse and to lift curses, to protect, and to aid in both birth and death.

The runes are arranged in groups called aett (plural aettir). The Elder Futhark consists of three aettir of eight runes each. A fourth aett, "the aett of the gods," sacred to the Norse Aesir, adds a further five runes rather than eight and was developed in Britain, bringing the total to twenty-nine. Around AD 800 the Northumbrian Anglo-Saxons added four more, Cweorth, Calc, Stan, and Gar, which open a final group sometimes called the fifth aett. These last five runes have known meanings but are rarely used.
