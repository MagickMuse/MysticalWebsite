Overview

The hippopotamus was both feared and revered in ancient Egypt. As one of the most dangerous animals in Africa, hippos posed a genuine threat to people working on or near the Nile. Yet their habit of moving between land and water echoed Egyptian ideas about creation, and they became central to both protective magic and divine imagery.

Faience Hippopotamus Figures

Archaeologists have found shiny blue hippopotamus figures in Middle Kingdom burials. These striking objects share two characteristics:

  1. Made of faience: The glazed paste was so tricky to produce that it was believed to have magical qualities. The hippos' bodies are adorned with lotus leaves, buds, and blossoms, as if the animal merged with the plants of its marshy home.
  2. Deliberately broken: Each had a foot broken off or damaged when placed in the tomb. Some were broken in two.

The breakage is all that remains of the magic ritual that was their entire reason for being. Reduced to manageable size in magic-imbued faience form, these hippopotami helped the priest-magician control the destructive power of the animal and all it symbolized. Once hobbled and tamed by magic words and actions, the figure could protect the tomb and the dead from harm.

Association with Seth

The hippopotamus's dangerous side encouraged its association with Seth, god of disorder. A conquered hippopotamus symbolized triumph over the forces of disorder, exactly the metaphor a magician would want for rites aimed at righting wrongs, healing the sick, or averting disaster.

In ritual hunts depicted on temple walls, pharaohs are shown harpooning hippopotami as a symbolic victory over chaos.

Hippopotamus Ivory Magic

Objects made of hippopotamus ivory had special magical potency. Curved ivory wands were used for health and protection, especially during childbirth and care of small children. These wands were often carved with protective deities and symbols.

Taweret: The Great One

The goddess Taweret had a hippopotamus body standing upright, with wildcat paws, crocodile tail, and nursing breasts. Her frightening hybrid form channeled divine protective power. A monstrous-looking body did not mean monstrous character; quite the opposite was often true.

Taweret carried the sa-hieroglyph of protection before her, like an amulet within the amulet of her own incredible animal form. Images of Taweret appeared throughout Egyptian homes on amulets, beds, headrests, murals, and nursing vessels.

Ipet and the Constellation

A similar hippopotamus figure with an entire crocodile forming her backbone was identified with the constellation Ursa Major (the Great Bear) and the goddess Ipet. Through her mighty body, the creator-gods (including a fusion of Sobek and Ra) could be reborn as they traveled through the starry night.

Symbolism

The hippopotamus has fared poorly in symbolic terms. It was widely believed to prefer mating with its own mother, and the Egyptians gave one of their evil deities, Typhon, the head of a hippopotamus. In general the animal was regarded as a cowardly and irreligious character.
