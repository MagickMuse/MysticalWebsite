Birds as Divine Communicators

Birds have walked, and flown, this world far longer than humankind, and though we see them every day they have always carried more than meets the eye. Their oldest symbolic role is that of messengers between the worlds, bearing word from the spiritual realms and from the Gods, and few symbols are as universal.

An Ancient Bird Cult

The place of birds in human imagination runs very deep. Among the prehistoric cave paintings at Lascaux is a scene now called the Death of the Bird Man, a hint that some form of bird cult may reach back as far as Upper Paleolithic times. Writing in Jung and His Symbols, Joseph L. Henderson called the bird the most fitting symbol of transcendence, an image of intuition working through a medium, one able to gather knowledge of distant events by entering a trancelike state.

Birds, Fate, and Augury

The link between birds and destiny is woven into language itself: in the Qur'an the word for bird stands also for fate, and in Greek the words for bird and omen are one and the same. Orthomancy, divination by watching birds, was practised the world over. Diviners noted a bird's flight, its kind, its calls and cries, and sometimes read its entrails. The Romans would settle no important matter without first consulting their augurs, and the Latin root that gives us augury, an omen or portent, lies in the word for bird, as does inauguration. The practice was far from Roman alone, being shared by Tibetans, Indians, Native Americans, Mexicans, Egyptians, Mayans, and Celts. Whole sacred sites were founded on bird signs, among them Mexico City, Rome, and the place of the Omphalos at Delphi.

The Gift of the Alphabet

Far-reaching, too, is the idea that birds revealed the secrets of the alphabet. The best-known tale holds that Hermes, messenger of the Gods, devised the letters by watching the flight of birds, and of cranes above all. Robert Graves, in The White Goddess, records that the secret signs were kept in a crane-skin bag, fitting enough given that Alaskan peoples used the skins of cranes, swans, and eagles for bags, these being the strongest. Little wonder that we lend our Gods and their messengers the marks of birds: wings, feathers, and the power of flight that stands for both bodily and spiritual transcendence and for movement in a realm closed to humankind. Hermes wears wings not only on his sandals but on the caduceus that is his emblem; and in a neat circle of meaning, the feather or quill became a favoured tool for setting down the written word, the bird speaking once again.
