Andalusian Horse: Soul Carrier

Equus ferus

Horse's ability to carry your body is widely known. Less recognized is Horse's capacity to transport your soul. In times past, calling on Horse meant both visiting your sister in the next village and visiting your great-grandmother in lands beyond this life. Horse serves as companion and guide whose gift is freedom to transcend physical incarnation.

Whether your body needs to move through space or your soul needs to glide through time, Horse beckons you to journey. When you doubt you can make it alone, call on Horse to carry you. Horse says: it's time to transcend your current way of being.

Melding into Oneness

Our alliance with Horse drastically altered human history. Distances became crossable, loads haulable. Thus Horse became associated with journeys in both this world and dreamworlds—companion, guide, and most importantly, catalyst making the journey possible. Horse represents freedom.

Horse lends strength and stamina, giving form to our passion and purpose. Riding on Horse's back allows experiencing oneness with something beyond ourselves. This transcendence of individual humanity, melding with Horse, is represented by the mythical centaur—horse body with human torso and head.

Many long for this kind of melding and transcendence, while simultaneously fearing loss of individuality. The centaur myth encodes this tension: the human element retains its identity within the merged form.

Symbolism

For thousands of years the horse has carried a spiritual, symbolic, and mythical weight that arguably outstrips that of any other living creature. Its vital bond with humankind, as hunting companion, beast of burden, means of travel, and agricultural mainstay, reaches back at least to the Paleolithic cave paintings at Lascaux, made roughly thirty thousand years ago. The horse's symbolism is woven so tightly into the story of humanity that the two can scarcely be separated.

The horse belongs to the Sun and the element of fire, proudly drawing the chariot of the Sun God, yet it belongs equally to the Moon and the element of water, since it bears the God of the Oceans on its back. Where a horse stamped its hoof, a spring was said to rise, making the animal a giver of life. In Greece the sacred well Hippocrene, the Horse's Well, is shaped like a hoof and dedicated to the Muses, and Pegasus, the winged horse, was held to have created it.

Depending on its context, the horse stands for life and death, darkness and light, good and evil. As well as bringing life, it serves as a psychopomp, guiding the souls of the dead toward the next world, much like the dog, with which it shares many traits. In practical terms it signifies power and wealth, since owning horses lends superiority through both monetary value and the distances and speeds a rider can command. From this springs another of its meanings, freedom, for though it can be trained, the horse is regarded not as inferior to its rider but as an equal.

Color deepens the horse's meaning. A white horse stands for the virginal, the spiritual, and the godly, and the Buddha chose to ride one, while Elizabeth I, the Virgin Queen, is shown on a white palfrey. A black horse, by contrast, often signals death and dark forces, while a golden horse is an emblem of the Sun and the solar deities.

Because warriors rode them, horses are linked to Mars, the God of War, and to the masculine attributes of virility, sexuality, and strength. Odin's steed Sleipnir held supernatural power, with eight legs and the ability to gallop over water as though it were solid ground. Yet the horse is also bound to the feminine, and medieval texts drew parallels between women and horses, advising that both be kept on a firm rein. A mare commonly leads the herd, and the Horse Goddesses include Epona, mother-goddess of the ancient Britons, and Rhiannon. The animal's sacred standing made its sacrifice a rare and solemn act, and the lingering repugnance many feel toward eating horseflesh is itself a sign that the creature is held sacred.
