The crow is one of the most widely distributed and symbolically loaded birds in the world, recognized across cultures as a creature of intelligence, magick, death, and transformation. A member of the Corvidae family, it shares deep kinship with the raven and carries many of the same associations, though where ravens lean toward transformative magick, crows are more closely linked with healing, prophecy, and psychopomp work.

Overview

The corvids are a large, widely distributed family of birds including crows, ravens, and jackdaws, powerfully associated with witchcraft and magick. Crows and ravens are big, loud, glossy black birds often referenced interchangeably. Ravens tend to be slightly larger with shaggy, disheveled-looking feathers, while crows are sleeker. Ravens are somewhat more solitary; crows may roost in communities numbering in the hundreds. Ravens hunt more actively, while crows are primarily scavengers. When mythology distinguishes between the two, ravens are usually associated with transformative magick while crows are identified with healing. Both are teachers and sponsors of magick and shamanism.

Physical Description

The genus Corvus includes approximately forty species worldwide. Crows are generally recognized by their glossy black plumage, black beaks, and black legs. The American crow measures approximately 17 inches in length with a wingspan of about 35 inches and an average weight of 16 ounces.

Physical Characteristics and Behavior

Crows are characterized by their shiny black coloring and loud, raucous voices. Diurnal birds, they noisily greet the sun each morning. Like baboons and roosters, crows are strongly identified with solar power and may be understood as venerating the sun themselves. In a Pacific Northwest myth, Earth is enshrouded in darkness and it is Crow who hangs the sun in the sky. Crows are classified among the world's most intelligent creatures, capable not only of tool use but of tool improvisation and construction. They also engage in play with one another and with other bird species. A collective of crows is known as a murder.

Associations with Death and War

Most crows are carrion eaters. They do not hunt or kill but consume the remains of the dead, wasting nothing and contributing to the cycle of decomposition and renewal. Historically, corvids gathered over battlefields to feed on the fallen, forging deep associations with death, war, and disaster. In Irish Gaelic, badbh, meaning crow, is also a word for witch, and is the name of the Celtic battle goddess Badb, one of the three aspects of the Morrígan alongside Nemain and Macha. Badb could take the form of a crow to fly over battlefields. The Scottish expression for death, "going away up the Crow Road," reflects the folk belief that crows serve as psychopomps, guiding spirits between the living world and the afterlife.

Oracular Nature and Associated Deities

Crows are oracular birds, evoking the spirit of prophecy and affiliated with divinatory traditions worldwide. Among the deities associated with crows and ravens are Amaterasu, Apollo, Badb, Cathubodua, Epona, Kali, Macha, the Morrígan, Nantosuelta, Nephthys, Odin, and Tlazolteotl.

Cross-Cultural Associations

In Hindu tradition, crows act as intermediaries during Shraddha, a ritual of ancestral gratitude in which offerings of food and water are brought to deceased relatives on the anniversary of their death. In ancient Egypt, the crow symbolized faithful love, a reflection of the bird's monogamous nature. In Chinese and Japanese mythology, crows serve as emblems of filial duty and familial devotion.

One of Aesop's most celebrated fables, "The Crow and the Pitcher," shows a thirsty crow unable to reach the little water remaining at the bottom of a jug. Rather than giving up, the crow drops pebble after pebble into the vessel until the water level rises within reach. The story encodes both a moral about patience and small persistent actions, and an observation about the crow's genuine capacity for problem-solving and creative tool use.

Trickster Heroes and World Mythology

Trickster heroes, Crow and Raven play an enormous role in world mythology across Celtic, Greek, Hungarian, Japanese, Jewish, Native American, Scandinavian, and Vietnamese traditions, but most especially in the indigenous traditions of Siberia and North America's Pacific Northwest. Crows truly are tricksters and clowns. They are assertive, unafraid of people, and as willing to play a trick on a human as on a dog or on each other. Shiny objects attract them and they have been known to steal. Crows coexist well with people and occasionally become companion animals, sometimes mimicking human speech or the sounds of other animals.

Post-Christian Demonization

The associations of crow and raven with witchcraft, magick, and shamanism predate Christianity. What shifts in the post-Christian period is the valuation of those associations. Rather than positive links with wise-women, shamans, and seers, crows came to be seen as signs of diabolism and sin. Their color, raucousness, sharp intelligence, and assertive nature led the Church to identify them with Satan as Lucifer, the fallen light-bringer. Legends emerged explaining why crows were black, typically framing their dark color as punishment for some ancient wrongdoing, once pure white but stained permanently by sin.

Omens and Divinatory Meaning

The crow urges inventiveness and lateral thinking when facing seemingly insurmountable problems. It asks what tools and skills are already at hand, and how they might be applied in unconventional ways. Change is the crow's central teaching. Death and ending are not losses but transformations, and every ending feeds what comes next. As a psychopomp, the crow is a companion through difficult transitions, whether literal or symbolic. If change feels overwhelming, the crow offers to walk through it alongside you. It also reminds you not to take the work of understanding life too seriously. Play is not a distraction from wisdom. It is part of it.
