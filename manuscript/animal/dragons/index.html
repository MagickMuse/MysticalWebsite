Dragons are among the most universally potent archetypal symbols in magickal practice, appearing in virtually every major world culture. Dragon energy (elemental mastery, transformation, accumulated wisdom, and raw sovereign power) is fully invocable through the creature's correspondences regardless of physical existence.

Dragons appear in Chinese, European, Norse, Mesoamerican, Middle Eastern, and countless other traditions, making them one of the few truly cross-cultural magickal archetypes. Whether as benevolent celestial beings (East Asian traditions), hoarding guardians of treasure (European), or primordial forces of creation and chaos (Norse Jörmungandr, Babylonian Tiamat), dragons consistently represent power at its most elemental and least domesticated.

Amulet Uses

A dragon image carried as an amulet represents life, power, and wisdom.

Etymology

The word dragon comes from the Greek for snake, the creature with which it is most closely associated, and it is sometimes called a worm, from the Germanic wurm. Old maps marked uncharted territory with the legend Here Be Dragons.

Guardian of Treasure and Secrets

Dragons have keen eyesight, which often makes them guardians of treasure and keepers of secrets. This watchfulness lends them great wisdom as well as clairvoyant power. A dragon may be an agent of good, to be encouraged, or an agent of evil, to be destroyed.

The Dragon in the East

In China the dragon is chief of the Four Divine Creatures, only one of which, the tortoise, is real; the others are the unicorn and the phoenix. The dragon embodies the spirit of change, a central principle of Taoism. In both China and Japan it stood for royalty and appeared on the robes of the Emperor and the Mikado, so that the word dragon could be used in place of Emperor: the Dragon's Face meant the Emperor's face, and the Dragon's Pace his majestic walk. In the Chinese zodiac the dragon is the fifth creature and corresponds to Leo.

The Dragon in the West

The dragon was painted on the shields of Norse warriors, meant both to protect the bearer and to terrify the enemy. In Britain, Uther Pendragon, father of King Arthur, was shown a flaming dragon in the sky as a sign that Arthur would be king, and this red dragon became the emblem of Wales. Across the border, the Englishman George was made a saint after killing a marauding dragon that had been devouring the young girls for miles around. Here the dragon stands for the Devil, who is likewise personified as a dragon in the battle with the Archangel Michael.
