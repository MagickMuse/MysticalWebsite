The rooster, or cock, is one of the great solar and masculine symbols of the world. Descended from jungle birds of India and Sri Lanka, it has been domesticated by humankind for at least four thousand years, spreading across the globe less for its eggs and meat, which came to be prized later, than for its use in the so-called sport of cockfighting.

The Ultimate Masculine Symbol

Easy to overlook as a creature of mystic significance, the rooster in fact carries a rich body of magical lore, much of it bound up with its name. The word cock, also a slang term for the penis, says a great deal: the bird is the ultimate masculine symbol, standing for the Sun, for power, for pugnacity, and for sexual prowess, and it forms the head of the mystical Abraxas figure. Its solar links are universal, and the frilled red comb upon its head was likened to the rays of the Sun.

War, Church, and the Crowing Cock

The rooster's aggressive bearing made it a symbol of war for the Greeks and Romans, and Aristophanes called it the chicken of Ares, after the god of war. It crowns the weathervanes of many Christian churches, both because it heralds the coming of physical light, and so of spiritual enlightenment, and because it is a poignant symbol within the faith: it is said to have crowed once at the birth of Christ, again when Saint Peter denied him, and that it will crow once more to warn of the Day of Judgment. The Qu'ran holds the reverse, that the end of the world will come when the cock at last falls silent.

Sun, Sacrifice, and the Three Poisons

All of the rooster's solar power and masculine energy made it a fitting sacrifice to Apollo, and the belief that one might take in that energy by eating the bird lies in part behind the fortifying reputation of a bowl of chicken soup. In the East, cockerels are bred to live in and around Shinto temples, and the Rooster is one of the twelve signs of the Chinese zodiac, lending those born under it enthusiasm and a sense of humour. For its associations with lust and pride, however, the cockerel is no favourable creature in Tibetan Buddhism, where it is one of the three poisons at the hub of the Wheel of Life, set beside the serpent and the pig as a warning against attachment to material things.

Herald of Dawn, Terror of Night Spirits

Though poultry is often thought of as humble, the rooster is a fearless, virile and territorial creature of potent magickal power. Because it crows at the first light of day, many malevolent low level nocturnal spirits fear it. These spirits are not clever enough to check the sky for the sunrise, so the moment they hear a rooster crow, or even see a rooster, they vanish.
