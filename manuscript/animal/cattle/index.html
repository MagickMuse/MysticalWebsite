The Calf

When Moses received the Ten Commandments from God on Mount Sinai, he was gone longer than expected. Growing restless, the Israelites asked Aaron to make a god for them to worship. Aaron gathered all the gold trinkets and jewelry the crowd could muster, melted the metal down, and built a statue of a Golden Calf that was given worship and prayers. Meanwhile an angry God told Moses what was happening, that the people were worshipping false idols, and that he intended to destroy them all. Moses interceded to spare the Israelites and destroyed the statue.

The story becomes clearer in light of Egyptian belief, where the calf was the child of the great goddess Isis and one of the forms taken by her son Horus. The Egyptians worshipped a golden calf called the Horus of Gold, and the Israelites likewise held this so-called false god in great regard, willing to give up their wealth to build his effigy. It would have made sense for Aaron to provide his people with the object they desired as a focus for their worship.

As a symbol of innocence and a creature commonly used in ritual sacrifice, the calf came to be associated with Christ. The fatted calf was one kept especially well fed so that its meat would be particularly tasty, and it was slaughtered only on very special occasions, such as the biblical tale of the prodigal son returning to his family.

The Vedic tales of India refer to the Sun as the golden calf.

The Cow

Because its milk is a staple food for so many people, the cow is one of the most useful animals in the world. It was among the first animals domesticated by humans, and its symbolism is rich. The cow is emblematic of fertility, abundance, wealth, the universal Mother, and rebirth, and it is also a gentle and compliant creature.

It is no surprise that the cow is widely thought of as a mother figure. In Egypt the cow was personified as Ahet, the mother of the Sun itself, and Egyptian women wore amulets of the cow-headed goddess Hathor in her guise as the Creator to make themselves fertile. In Europe the cow was likewise the mother-ancestor of all living things, called Audumla, the milch-cow. In Greek myth one of the names for the great Cow was Europa, meaning full Moon, and the stars were said to be the children of Europa.

The cow was also regarded as a psychopomp, a creature able to lead the souls of the dead to the Underworld. There was once a custom of bringing a cow to the sickroom so that the dying person could grasp its tail while breathing a last breath. This role is still honored in the Nepali festival called Gai Jatra, the cow festival, in which every family that has lost a member during the year joins a procession leading a cow, or a child dressed in a cow costume when no animal is available. The cow is believed to guide the soul on its final journey, including a passage through the Milky Way, the band of stars said to be milk splashed by the great Cow that mothered the Universe.

The sacred cow is a fundamental part of Hindu iconography. In India the cow is so revered and treated so gently that it often causes a traffic hazard as it wanders along busy city roads. The Hindu faith respects all animals and holds vegetarianism as a tenet, and the god Krishna was a cowherd for a time, one of his names being Bala Gopala, the child who protects the cows. Hindu deities rule over every part of the cow. In times of famine a cow that gives limitless milk is far more useful alive than as a dead beast that would provide meat only briefly.

The Bull

The sacred standing of the bull reaches back to at least 3000 BC, when early Hebrews carved the effigy of a God named El in the form of a bull at the head of their ritual staffs.

The bull is the archetype of brute masculinity, fertility, tyranny, and ferocity. In Greece it was sacred to both Poseidon and Dionysus. Zeus took the shape of a bull to seduce the mortal Europa; once she had been tricked into trusting the kneeling creature and climbed onto its back, it carried her away, an emblem of passion and lust. The bull also tested the strength of Heracles, who strangled the Cretan bull that was wreaking havoc and had it shipped away to Athens.

Bull Sacrifice

The ritual sacrifice of the bull is so ancient that its origins cannot be fixed. A painting in the Ellora Caves shows the Goddess Kali slaying a bull, yet the rite is far older than the artwork, which is dated to around AD 500 to 1000. To early people the bull was so supremely powerful that being splashed with its blood was thought to confer immortality. In Rome a bull cult introduced from Asia Minor in the second century BC inspired the taurobolium, in which an initiate stood in a trench beneath a board pierced with holes while a bull was slain above, so that the hot blood poured down over the devotee. The rite reenacted a Mithraic legend that named the bull's blood as the origin of all life, renewing body and soul.

The Spanish bullfight is a remnant of this same tradition and draws on the same Mithraic legend, which tells that the bull was the first creature on Earth and that when Mithras killed it, all other living things sprang from the blood spilled on the ground. Many of the oldest bullrings in Spain stand on or close to the sites of former Mithraic temples, and bullrings also appear in South America and France.

The Celts shared these beliefs and kept their own bull sacrifice, though theirs was shamanistic in purpose. The tribal seer ate the flesh and drank the blood in the hope of inducing prophetic dreams, specifically to choose candidates for kingship.

The bull is immortalized in the night sky as the constellation Taurus, one of the signs of the astrological Zodiac.
