Sphinxes function as archetypal symbols of the threshold guardian, the keeper of sacred knowledge who demands wisdom before granting passage. Their energy encompasses mystery, protected secrets, and the challenge of genuine self-knowledge.

The Sphinx appears in Egyptian tradition as the ultimate guardian, a composite creature of human, lion, hawk, and bull embodying mastery of all four elements and the four pillars of creation. The Greek Sphinx posed riddles to travelers and destroyed those who could not answer, a metaphor for the spiritual tests that precede initiation.

In magickal practice, Sphinx energy is invoked for divination requiring deep honesty, protection of sacred knowledge or private matters, and workings where a practitioner must confront uncomfortable truths about themselves before moving forward.

A Woman's Head, a Lion's Body

The sphinx generally has the head of a woman and the body of a lion. The most famous of all, the Egyptian sphinxes, bear no wings, unlike their Assyrian and Greek counterparts.

The Riddle and the Devourer

As the very emblem of mystery and hidden secrets, the sphinx has endured for centuries. Her riddle, what goes on four feet in the morning, on two feet at midday, and on three feet in the evening, was answered long ago, yet the true meaning behind her implacable expression can still only be guessed at. She has a reputation as a devourer, and the wings granted her by the Greeks and Assyrians suggest a divine origin. In Greek legend the sphinx is an evil monster that ravaged the land of Thebes, putting riddles to all who passed and devouring those who answered wrongly or could not answer at all.

Strangler and Guardian

The word sphinx comes from a Greek root meaning strangler, perhaps a nod to the way a lion kills its prey, though whatever the Egyptians called the creature in their own tongue is now lost. Sphinxes may be male or female, and they are fierce, secretive guardian spirits.

The Great Sphinx

Egypt's sixty-five-foot Great Sphinx, known in Arabic as the Father of Fear, remains the most famous of all, its purpose, age, and even identity still debated. Greek sphinxes, which appear from an early date, are winged, and were said to derive originally from Ethiopia.

Setting a Guardian

Sphinxes are primeval spirits of guarding and protection and a repository of great secrets rather than mundane ones. Their power is worked through their image: a personal sphinx may be asked to stand watch over a home, building, shop, or object, the request made through dreams, visualization, or shamanic journey, or by speaking to the image and offering candles, water, and incense. Its head is turned toward the direction from which danger may come.

Borrowed Power

Beings of immense might, sphinxes can pass some of their power to their devotees, which is why people have long wished to set their own head upon a sphinx's body. Pharaohs, Hatshepsut among them, had themselves portrayed as sphinxes, and the actress and sculptor Sarah Bernhardt made a self-portrait in sphinx form.

The Erotic Sphinx

Though many sphinxes are male, the figure is also tied to a profoundly erotic female power. Franz von Stuck's 1901 painting Sphinx shows a nude woman reclining in the sphinx pose, and women such as Colette and the dancer Ida Rubinstein likewise posed as sphinxes.

In Art and Memory

The memorial over Oscar Wilde's grave in the Père Lachaise cemetery in Paris takes the form of a sphinx; its sculptor, Jacob Epstein, called it a Messenger of Beauty, while others saw in it a demon-angel. The sphinx has drawn countless artists, among them Gustave Moreau, Gustave Doré, Odilon Redon, and Fernand Khnopff.
