Overview

Bats are ancient creatures, having inhabited Earth for about 50 million years. Nearly one thousand species exist, comprising nearly one quarter of all mammal species. Bats are unique as the only mammal capable of true flight. Virtually everywhere bats are found, they are identified with witchcraft, perceived as witches' familiars, mounts, and alter egos.

Physical Form and Behavior

Bats inspired awe because their form was ambiguous: they resemble a cross between an animal and a bird. In ancient Asian belief, bats were understood to be the most perfect bird because they nurse their young. Most bats are nocturnal, sleeping through the day hanging upside down in huge colonies, emerging at dusk from the caves they inhabit in huge swarms. Animals that live in caves, grottoes, or underground are metaphysically perceived as being especially close to Earth, privy to her deepest secrets.

Medieval European Associations

Medieval Europeans associated bats with dragons, magical winged creatures that live in caves and grottos. This association proved unfortunate, as by the Middle Ages, dragons were targets for questing knights, and small bats are much easier to kill than fire-breathing dragons. Dragons were also associated with Satan, and this association rubbed off on bats. They became closely associated with devils, demons, and the anti-Christ. Medieval artwork frequently depicts Satan with bat's wings, while angels were consistently painted with wings of white birds.

In medieval Europe, the sight of a flying bat was often interpreted as a transformed witch. Witches were believed to transform into bats, to ride bats like horses, and to smear their broomsticks with bat's blood to achieve lift-off. In 1322, Lady Jacaume of Bayonne, France was publicly burned at the stake as a witch. The evidence was that swarms of bats had been observed flying about her house and garden.

Vampire Bats and Dracula

The original major fear regarding bats was that they would become entangled in a woman's hair, not that they would suck blood. With the exception of a very few blood-consuming species, bats eat either fruit or insects. Vampire bats are indigenous only to the Western Hemisphere, ranging from Argentina to Mexico. They were never found in Central Europe, where the concept of an undead creature who survives by sapping the vitality of the living has existed since time immemorial.

Vampire bats received their name from the mythic vampire, not vice versa. Bram Stoker incorporated blood-consuming bats into his novel Dracula, whose success forever changed perceptions of bats and mythical vampires. The concept of a mythic blood-consuming "vampiric" spirit was well-known in Central and South America prior to European contact, where blood-consuming bats exist and have associations with death and blood sacrifice.

Positive Associations

Not all associations with bats are negative. The Kogi people of northern Columbia associate the vampire with human fertility. Their euphemistic expression for a girl who begins to menstruate is that she has been "bitten by the bat." According to the Kogi, the bat was the very first animal to be created, emerging directly from the Creator's body. Some tribes in New Guinea also perceive bats as fertility symbols.

In China, bats are regarded as especially auspicious, their very name a pun for luck. The Chinese five-bat design (Wu Fu) represents five blessings: longevity, prosperity, health, righteousness, and a natural death. Bat images abound in Chinese art and ornamentation.

African Folklore

Bats figure prominently in African folklore. In East Africa, bats are witches' mounts. In the Ivory Coast, bats represent souls of the departed, while in Madagascar, bats are souls of criminals, sorcerers, and the unburied dead. Bats have powerful associations with death and ghosts.

Magical Uses and Substitutions

Associations of bats with witches and magic inspired the use of whole bat corpses and various anatomical parts (hearts, wings, blood) in magick spells. References to bat's wings in magick spells may refer to holly leaves, which may always be substituted. Bat nuts (dried ling nuts), which if held from one angle resemble bats, may be substituted for bats in any spell. Bat's blood may have been a euphemism for another magical ingredient, perhaps a resin. Since the 1920s, commercially marketed Bat's Blood Ink is scented red ink.

Conservation and Modern Practice

Negative associations have taken a deadly toll. Many species of bats are extremely endangered due to loss of habitat and extermination as vermin. This terribly upsets the balance of nature: bats are genuine fertility figures, responsible for pollination of many plant species, particularly in the desert. Bats are also responsible for insect control, and one bat can consume as many as 600 mosquitoes in one hour. Modern witchcraft practices suggest that maintaining a bat house on your property will bring joy and good luck.

Longevity and Immortality

Like the Taoist Immortals, bats dwell in caves, and so they too became emblems of longevity and immortality. Some eastern bat caves have remained unchanged for thousands of years, and the bats living within them are revered as sacred.

The Night Devouring the Day

The bat's nocturnal nature has also drawn darker meanings. It is seen as a symbol of the night devouring the day, said to swallow the light because it stirs at dusk, the threshold between day and night. Indigenous peoples of Brazil tell that a bat swallowing the Sun will herald the end of the world, and the Maya regarded the bat as a harbinger of death.

Keeper of Hidden Knowledge

The same nocturnal habit makes the bat, like the owl, a creature with access to hidden knowledge and secret information, able to perceive in darkness what daytime creatures cannot. Before echolocation was understood, the bat's effortless navigation through the night was a source of fascination that only deepened its air of mystery.

A Good Mother

Because female bats are the only flying creatures that suckle their young, the bat is also honored as a symbol of the good mother.
