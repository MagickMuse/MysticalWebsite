Overview

The corvids are a large, widely distributed family of birds including crows, ravens, and jackdaws, powerfully associated with witchcraft and magick. Crows and ravens are big, loud, noisy, black birds often referred to interchangeably. Technically ravens may be slightly larger, with shaggy, disheveled-looking feathers, unlike shiny, sleek black crows. Ravens are somewhat more solitary than crows, which may roost together in communities numbering hundreds. Ravens will also hunt more than crows, which are mainly scavengers.

When mythology distinguishes between the two, ravens are usually associated with transformative magick while crows are identified with healing. Both are teachers and sponsors of magick and shamanism.

Physical Characteristics and Behavior

Crows and ravens are characterized by their shiny black color and their raucous, loud voices. Diurnal birds, like roosters, they noisily greet the sun. Crows, like baboons and roosters, are strongly identified with solar power and may be understood to venerate or worship the sun themselves. In a Pacific Northwest myth, Earth is enshrouded in darkness; Crow is literally the one who hangs the sun in the sky.

Associations with Death and War

Crows and ravens, like bears and humans, are omnivores; crows do not like to hunt, however, but prefer to scavenge. Historically corvids hover over battlefields waiting for an opportunity to feast on the dead. Crows thus have profound associations with spirits presiding over death, war, and disaster. In Irish Gaelic, badbh meaning "crow" is a synonym for "witch." It is also the name of a Celtic battle goddess, who may manifest in the shape of her namesake bird.

Oracular Nature and Associated Deities

Crows are oracular birds; they evoke the spirit of prophecy and are also affiliated with oracular spirits. Among the deities with whom crows and ravens are associated are: Amaterasu, Apollo, Badbh, Cathubodua, Epona, Kali, Macha, The Morrigan (known as the "battle raven" or "battle crow"), Nantosuelta, Nephthys, Odin (known as "God of the Ravens"), and Tlazolteotl.

Trickster Heroes and World Mythology

Trickster heroes, Crow and Raven play an enormous role in world mythology, including Celtic, Greek, Hungarian, Japanese, Jewish, Native American, Scandinavian, and Vietnamese traditions, but most especially in the indigenous traditions of Siberia and North America's Pacific Northwest.

Crows truly are tricksters and clowns; it isn't just mythological affectation. If you spend time with them, you will observe their games and tricks. They are assertive, unafraid of people and as willing to play a joke on a person as they are on a dog, cat or on each other. Shiny things appeal to them and they've been known to steal them. Crows coexist well with people and occasionally become companion animals. Crows will, on occasion, mimic human language or the sounds of other animals.

Post-Christian Demonization

The associations of crow and raven with witchcraft, magick, and shamanism pre-date Christianity. What changes post-Christianity is the perception of those associations. Rather than positive associations with wise-women, shaman, and seers, crows were now associated with diabolism and sin. In the eyes of Christian Europe, crows and ravens were linked with witchcraft because of their color, their raucousness, harsh voices, sharp intelligence, and assertive nature.

Legends emerged explaining why crows were black, typically involving punishment for some kind of sin. According to the standard story, once upon a time, crows were pure white but they did something very bad and so were punished by being painted permanently black. Because crows are black birds that daily herald the light, the Church identified them with Satan in his guise as Lucifer, the fallen angel and light bringer.

Raven's Ancient Memory

Raven carries a secret few recall: the memory of white plumage. This personal transformation, from snow-white to shadow-black, gives Raven unique insight into reality's fluid nature. Having experienced such radical change firsthand, Raven knows that appearances shift, that light and shadow are not opposites but partners in an eternal dance.

This lived wisdom places Raven outside conventional morality. The trickster asks uncomfortable questions: Which rules serve the highest good, and which merely maintain comfortable structures? What sacrifices are you willing to make to bring what truly matters into the light? These questions challenge us to trust our inner compass over external authorities.

How Raven Brought the Sun

Pacific Northwest peoples tell of how Raven's transformation happened. In the world's beginning, darkness covered everything. Raven, then white-feathered, loved Eagle's daughter and visited Eagle's mountain home. There he discovered a blazing orb that could illuminate the entire suffering world, kept selfishly warm only Eagle's family.

Raven faced a choice between personal love and collective freedom. He chose the world. Seizing the fire-sphere, he fled up the chimney while Eagle gave chase. The soot permanently darkened Raven's once-pristine feathers as he flew. At his highest point, Raven hurled the sphere beyond Eagle's reach, and it became the Sun, bringing light to all.

Raven's black feathers mark the cost he paid for this gift. His story illustrates that the most fixed-seeming conditions may be as mutable as the transformation of his plumage, and that the bringing of necessary light often demands personal sacrifice.

The Cleverest Bird

The raven ranks among the most intelligent of all birds. Its reasoning has been compared favorably to that of humans, and its linguistic gifts are so pronounced that it may understand human words as well as imitate them. This intelligence, paired with a playful and mischievous streak, is what makes the raven the ultimate emblem of the trickster.

Teamwork and the Raven Mother

In some cultures the raven rivals or even surpasses the eagle in importance. Like the eagle it has few natural enemies apart from humankind, but unlike the eagle ravens cooperate for the good of the group and so came to symbolize the rewards of working together. A darker reading survives in Denmark, where the term Raven Mother is a euphemism for poor parenting, drawn from the belief that the bird waits to see the color of its chicks' feathers before deciding to feed them.

Hugin and Munin

The raven's voice carries an oddly human inflection, which fed the belief that the bird knew everything. This is embodied in the two ravens of the Norse god Odin, named Thought and Memory, who flew across the world each day and returned at dusk to whisper into his ears all the doings of mankind. Odin himself was called the Raven God, his Valkyrie daughters could appear as ravens, and witches were said to take raven form to travel unseen to their gatherings.

Harbinger and Helper

As a carrion bird seen feasting at the sites of battle, the raven became a harbinger of death, personified by the Morrigan, the Celtic battle goddess who takes raven shape. Yet the omen is not everywhere unlucky. In one Hindu epic the raven is a messenger of death without being a sign of ill fortune. The tale of Noah, whose raven flew off and never returned while the dove came back bearing an olive sprig, cast the bird as no friend to humankind, but the ravens released by Viking explorers in search of land proved far more reliable, and across parts of northern Europe the raven is regarded as a helper of people.

The Ravens of the Tower

The ravens kept at the Tower of London are protective birds beyond compare. They have been maintained there for over a thousand years on the strength of an old legend that the kingdom will stand safe from invaders so long as the ravens remain. The belief is so deeply rooted that when their numbers fell during the Second World War, ravens were brought in from Wales to keep the country secure. Despite its black plumage the raven was a solar symbol to the ancient Greeks, dedicated to both Athena and Apollo, and in Native American belief it is the Creator who made the universe, discovered and watched over the first people, and remains the favored bird of shamans who speak with it to learn what the gods intend.
