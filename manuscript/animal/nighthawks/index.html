The nighthawk is a nocturnal caprimulgid, a member of the so-called goatsucker family whose nearly one hundred species range across Africa, Australia, the Americas, Europe, and Asia. Alongside owls and corvids, nighthawks and their relatives are among the birds most intensely identified with witchcraft across the widest range of human cultures.

Physical Description

The common nighthawk (Chordeiles minor) measures approximately 10 inches in length with a wingspan of roughly 12 inches and a weight of about 3 ounces. Its plumage is a dense speckle of browns, blacks, and whites that blends seamlessly into leaf litter, scrubby growth, and bare ground. Long-winged, long-tailed, and big-eyed, the bird has proportionately short legs and weak feet unsuitable for walking. It is built for life in the air. The nighthawk does not build a nest; it lays its eggs directly on the ground, relying on its camouflage for protection. When perching, it rests lengthwise along its branch rather than across it like most birds.

Habitat and Distribution

The common nighthawk ranges across Canada, the United States, Central America, and most of South America. It is a bird of open country, most at home in grasslands where it hunts insects on the wing from dusk until well into the night.

Behavior

The nighthawk is most active from dusk onward. Its flight display is distinctive: it swoops sharply downward, then flares its wingtips to redirect upward in a tight arc. This sudden change in direction produces a booming sound from the wings, making the bird both audible and visible in the fading light.

Mythology and Folklore

Nighthawks and their relatives are rarely observed during the day but are more readily seen at twilight or night. Shadowy and mysterious, they have long been associated with witchcraft and magic across a remarkable number of cultures. Nightjar blood is an ingredient in ancient Egyptian magic spells. In Africa, Yoruba witches are said to fly at night in the form of nightjars, sucking victims' blood, while the Tukana of South America believe that dead souls transform into nightjars and feed vampirically on the living. Their gaping, bristle-fringed mouths were compared to genitalia in various traditions, giving them simultaneous associations with fertility power and the diabolical. In German, the nightjar is called hexe, the word for witch.

During the witch-hunt era, goats were identified as emblems of Satan who allegedly gave witches imps that they suckled. With a family name like goatsucker, these birds could hardly escape association with witchcraft and the devil. A species of nightjar from Sulawesi is actually named the diabolical nightjar (Eurostopodus diabolicus). Owls are their nearest relatives, believed to share descent from a common ancestor from around 100 million years ago.

In Patagonia, the nighthawk's call was regarded as an omen of illness and death. The booming produced by its wing display was associated with thunder and the coming of rain. Colloquially, nighthawk also describes a person who is most productive after dark, the counterpart of an early-rising lark.

Omens and Divinatory Meaning

The nighthawk inhabits the liminal hour of dusk, a threshold time that is neither fully day nor fully night. Sighting one signals that a period of transition is either approaching or already underway. This is a moment to act rather than wait: to step through the opening before it closes.

The bird's erratic, acrobatic flight is itself a lesson in adaptability. The nighthawk does not commit to a single trajectory; it pivots the instant the situation shifts. The message it carries is one of flexible awareness: stay observant, resist rigid habits, and be prepared to change course when an opportunity presents itself.
