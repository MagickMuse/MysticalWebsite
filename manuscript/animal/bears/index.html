Overview

Bears are conspicuous in witchcraft lore by their very absence. They are creatures so sacred that many fear to mention their name. Bears are the animals of shamanism par excellence. Throughout Northern lands, whether North America, Europe or Asia, bears are the original sacred animal, sponsors and symbols of shamanic healing societies. They are the teachers and perhaps originators of shamanism. Because bears dig in the Earth, they are understood as the original root-workers and possess profound connections with healing, herbalism, and root magick. Bears are simultaneously sacred and dangerous creatures, benevolent and frightening, possessing powers too strong for the uninitiated to withstand.

Sacred Names and Euphemisms

In traditional shamanic cultures, bears were worshipped and venerated. These bear cultures typically never utter the name "bear": that would be like taking the Lord's name in vain. Euphemisms are substituted: "Big Brother," "Old Honey Thief," and the like. In a similar manner, ancient Greeks never mentioned the name of the Lord of the Dead; Hades, which names his realm, and Pluto, meaning "The Rich One," are both euphemisms.

Ancient Bear Religion

Bear religion is among Earth's original religions. Fairly soon after people began worshipping mothers, they began worshipping bears, too. Sometimes both were worshipped simultaneously. Paleolithic goddess statuettes depict huge mother bears nursing petite human infants. Bears possess a great resemblance to humans. They stand upright and eat a similar diet. In a Native American story, a boy abandoned in the woods discovers that out of all the forest animals, the only animal that he as a human can live with comfortably is the bear.

Cave Bears and Neanderthal Shrines

Ursus spelaeus, the cave bear, appeared on Earth approximately 300,000 years ago and was physically similar to the modern brown (grizzly) bear. Cave bears were huge, weighing up to one ton and perhaps 30 percent taller than brown bears. They hibernated in caves, where they also gave birth and frequently died, leaving their skeletons behind.

Among the very first indications of human spiritual traditions are ancient cave bear shrines. In Alpine grottoes dating to c.100,000 BCE, cave bear skulls are marked with red ochre and carefully arranged alongside ritual hearths. Bear skulls were also arranged on stone slabs and placed in wall niches. The caves contain altars, flagstone flooring, benches, and tables. This is literally Neanderthal religion; Neanderthal people built these shrines. The bones of at least 30,000 cave bears formed a deep layer in the Dragon Cave near Mixnitz, Austria.

Distribution and Endangerment

Vestiges of the sacred nature of bears survive in place names, like Berne, Switzerland, city of the bears. Europe is now largely devoid of bears. They have lost virtually all of their former territory in North America as well. Atlas bears once ranged from Morocco to Libya; the last Atlas bear is believed to have been shot in 1840 in the Tetuan Mountains. Bears of one type or another are indigenous to all continents except Australia and Antarctica. A healthy adult bear has no enemies other than people. Wherever bears survive, they are endangered because of loss of habitat and extermination as a competitive species, for sport, and for museum collections. Bears' bodies are valued in East Asian medicine, making them targets for poachers.

Norse Berserkers and Odin

The ancient Norse associated bears with the shaman god Odin. Warriors who fought under his protection were known as "berserkers" (berserk means bear shirt). They fought naked but for bear skins, ritually channeling bear power, temporarily incorporating the bear's spirit to become fierce, formidable, and virtually unbeatable, striking terror into their opponents as they went berserk. In a sense, they become temporary were-bears; their comrades, also under Odin's protection, were wolf warriors.

Native American Bear Spirituality

Bear-centered spirituality survives wherever traditional Northern shamanism survives, particularly among Native Americans. Native American bear doctors and bear societies still exist. Among the few explicit linkages of bears to witchcraft occurs in the Ojibwa tradition of bear-walking, a form of shape-shifting sorcery.

Artemis and Greek Tradition

Bears were sacred in warmer climates, too. The Greek goddess Artemis's name may derive from her affiliation with bears, which were among her most sacred animals and sometime her alter-ego. Her young temple priestesses were known as "bears."

Fairy Tales and Hidden Meanings

The few references to bears in European fairy tales usually tell of men doomed to wear the bear's form because of unhappy encounters with witches, as in Snow White and Rose Red and some versions of East of the Sun, West of the Moon. These stories read negatively if one assumes that transformation into a bear's shape is negative; reading between the lines, if one understands wearing the bear's skin as a secret reference to shamanism, different conclusions can be drawn.

Black Bear's Teaching

Ursus americanus

Watching Black Bear stand upright on hind legs might briefly fool you into thinking a human approaches. This bipedal capacity makes her a bridge between earth and sky, positioning Black Bear as humanity's mirror-self, a wild sister still connected to healing wisdom and primal instinct we've largely forgotten.

Black Bear speaks to the part of us longing to hibernate all winter, then emerge renewed with creative energy trailing behind like cubs at our heels. Her teaching is simple: stop fighting seasonal cycles. Call on Bear to help you rediscover your natural rhythms and reclaim your wildness.

When nights lengthen and cold arrives, Black Bear retreats into dreaming without resistance. She embodies the understanding that dark nights exist for dreaming, requiring no justification. By contrast, human cultures characteristically resist the long night, filling it with artificial light and social activity. Black Bear's teaching is that the slowing, deepening, and even the subtle sorrow of winter are appropriate seasonal textures to be aligned with rather than escaped. Her medicine is the healing that comes through surrender to natural cycles.
