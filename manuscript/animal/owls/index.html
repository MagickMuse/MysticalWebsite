Overview

"Strega," "strix," "estrie": these terms are synonyms for "witch," although literally what they mean is "owl." Owls were witches' familiars from ancient Egypt, Rome, and Asia to modern Africa and Native America, with many stops in between. They represent divine yin: night, darkness, magick, and sacred lunar and feminine mysteries.

Owls are associated with wisdom, both conventional and secret, witchcraft, magick, sex, death, and birth. In the Eastern Hemisphere, owls were understood as emblematic of the uterus and as embodying the Great Mother's power over life and death. Owls are sponsors of shamanism. They bestow gifts of clairvoyance and teach the arts of astral projection. They serve as guides to the realms of the spirits and the dead because, of course, owls can navigate the darkness.

Physical Characteristics

There are approximately 135 living species in the order Strigiformes, varying in size from the six-inch elf owl to the three-foot long Great Gray Owl. Owls have a very distinctive shape. Only their silhouette may be required for identification. Compared to other birds, owls are fairly odd looking, resembling cats with wings. Their eyes are circular, evoking the full moon's shape and glow. Some owls are even horned, or at least they appear to be. Owls announce the night like crows herald the day. No bird or animal is more associated with night than owls.

Ancient History

Owls made a very early impression on people: in Les Trois Frères cave in France, home of the "Dancing Sorcerer," an unmistakable outline of a pair of snowy owls together with their chicks is chipped from the rock face. Paleolithic "Eye-Goddesses" may represent stylized owls.

Roman Strix

"Striges" was the Roman name for witch, typically understood malevolently. Owls were perceived as harbingers of doom, trouble, and death. Strix came to be understood as a specific kind of witch: grotesque, sexually voracious, baby killing, female cannibals. This isn't an integral part of the word's meaning, however. Strix and its linguistic derivatives may also be understood to denote witchcraft's positive attributes: knowledge of Earth's powers, the ability to journey between realms, and acquisition of great wisdom, especially of crucial, secret topics.

Sacred Female Spirits

Various sacred female spirits are profoundly identified with owls:

Athena: Owls are sacred to Athena. The small screech owl is her emblem and Homer describes Athena as "owl-faced." It was popularly believed that Athena appeared on the battlefield as an owl during a Greek battle with the Persians.

Lilith: Owls are identified with the Semitic wind spirit Lilith, whose name is cognate with "screech owl." Unlike most other formerly prominent Middle Eastern deities, Lilith, identified as Earth's real first woman, survived to star in worldwide Jewish folklore, where she serves as the prototype of the witch.

Blodeuwedd: The Welsh magical woman is formed from flowers. Since she is a magical being, she is immortal and cannot be punished by death for betraying and killing her husband Lleu; instead she is transformed into an owl, condemned to hunt alone at night for ever. The implication is that Blodeuwedd now displays her true form, that of a witch.

Tlazolteotl: Owls fly with this Aztec witch-goddess who has dominion over life, death, magic, and spiritual purification. Tlazolteotl cleans up sin like owls gobble up rats.

Marinette: Vodou sorceress lwa, manifests as a screech owl. Those whom she temporarily possesses demonstrate her presence by behaving like owls too.

Owls as Witch Forms

Owls signify witchcraft. Whether this is understood positively, negatively or neutrally reflects cultural and individual perceptions of witchcraft. Owls famously serve as witches' familiars and messengers and most frequently as the guise into which witches transform.

Some Siberian shamans' coats are cut to resemble owl wings and tail. Apuleius witnessed the successful transformation of the witch Pamphile into an owl in his novel, The Golden Ass. In central and southern Africa, sorcerers are believed to fly at night like owls to steal food and valuables from their neighbors. African witches who prefer not to shape-shift into hyenas are believed most likely to choose the shape of an owl instead. Aztec nocturnal shape-shifting sorcerers were known as "tlacatecolotl," "owl-men." The Aztecs associated owls with caves and mirrors, the same magical world of sorcery inhabited by sacred jaguars and presided over by the divine sorcerer, Tezcatlipoca.

Birth and Women's Power

Owls signify birth and women's power, especially their reproductive and sexual powers. In Ecuador and Peru, dating back at least as far as 300 BCE, owls representing the divine mother are favored decorative motifs on spindle whorls. In parts of France and Wales, the hooting of owls doesn't signify death but its opposite. It's believed to foretell the birth of daughters. In the nineteenth century, "owl" became slang for whore or harlot.

Cailleach Oidhche: Scottish Night Hag

Scottish folk name meaning "night hag/old woman of night." Linguistic link between owls and hags runs throughout cultures, from Roman "strix" to Scottish "cailleach oidhche", revealing universal recognition of owls as manifestations of sacred feminine in crone aspect.

Neolithic Owl Goddesses: Ancient statuettes establish owls as divine figures from humanity's earliest spiritual expressions. "Eye Goddesses" emphasize large owl-like eyes combined with female anatomical features, linking owl's appearance to feminine sacred power and ability to see in darkness.

Psychopomps: Owls are natural psychopomps, spirits escorting dead souls to next destination. Hag-goddesses who preside over death/transformation sometimes manifest as owls when performing this sacred work. Owl's ability to navigate darkness becomes metaphor for guiding souls through darkness of death. This psychopomp role connects to Cailleach and other hag-spirits governing boundary between life and death.

Screech Owl's Innovations

Megascops spp.

Screech Owl demonstrates remarkable ingenuity. She's discovered that bringing a blind snake, a tiny creature that feeds on ants and flies, into her nest provides natural pest control for her food stores. This symbiotic solution shows creative problem-solving beyond typical predator-prey relationships.

Her camouflage skills match her cleverness. Screech Owl can mimic tree bark with her feathers' patterns and colors. When extra concealment is needed, she'll elongate her body and sway slightly, imitating a branch moving in the breeze. When her melancholy trill fills the night, it signals time to apply creative thinking to your own challenges.

Creativity expresses itself countless ways. It may manifest in domestic ingenuity, in gathering and connecting people, or in unconventional problem-solving. Creativity is not limited to galleries and poetry journals but is present in the daily crafting of human existence.

Barn Owl: Between the Worlds

Tyto alba

Barn Owl glides low across fields on moonless nights. Her wingbeats produce not just silence but something deeper, a wake of quietude, a vortex of otherness. Crossing her flight path feels like stepping into elsewhere. This quality establishes Barn Owl as ancient messenger between worlds, carrying communications from the realm of spirits and ghosts.

Even in complete darkness, Barn Owl hunts successfully. Her exceptional hearing detects mice chattering and grass brushing against rabbit fur, or perhaps she simply senses their souls moving through nightly routines. Her teaching whispers: close your eyes and open your ears to spirit's voice.

Sometimes strengthening one sense requires dampening another. Vision is the habitual primary mode of perception, with the demand to see empirical evidence before belief. Barn Owl's teaching is that sight is not the sole method of connection. Intuition and inner knowing cannot be perceived visually, and information received through dreams and meditation represents an equally valid form of perception.

Little Owl: Illuminating the Blind Side

Athene noctua

Little Owl has been goddess companion and city patron; don't let "little" mislead you. This wisdom icon sat on Athena's blind side, illuminating what even a Goddess couldn't see. Athena, Greek Goddess of Wisdom, knew that being wise requires acknowledging your own weaknesses, understanding that even Goddesses aren't all-knowing.

Blindness itself isn't a fault; failure comes from refusing to call on those who help you understand the whole. Little Owl sees what you cannot. Call on her when you're ready to listen.

Admitting one's own blind side requires bravery and self-awareness. Little Owl's mythological role is to illuminate what even a Goddess could not perceive from her own vantage point. Acknowledging limitation is the precondition for seeking counsel and support. Those who provide that support, whether accountant, therapist, or trusted advisor, serve the same function as the owl on Athena's shoulder. The willingness to receive counsel is what moves a practitioner toward genuine wisdom.

Additional Mythological Associations

Lakshmi: Lakshmi, the Hindu goddess of prosperity, wealth, and wisdom, is accompanied by an owl. In this context the owl is not a death omen but a symbol of discernment in the accumulation and stewardship of wealth, and the ability to see opportunity in darkness where others cannot.

Ancient Egypt: The owl hieroglyph represented the letter M in the Egyptian writing system and carried associations of wisdom, mystical goddess-related knowledge, and sacred secrets. It was one of the system's most charged symbolic glyphs.

Native American traditions: In various Native American tribes, a sighting or the call of an owl was understood as a warning of impending illness, misfortune, or death. The specifics varied widely by tribe and region, but the association with ominous foreknowledge was widespread across North America.

Ancient Greece and Rome: Because Athena was not only the goddess of wisdom but of war, the owl was considered a lucky omen specifically for soldiers going into battle. This martial association carried over to Athena's Roman equivalent, Minerva.

Rome (protective): A dead owl nailed to the door of a house was believed to protect the home from evil. The Romans also associated living owls with shapeshifting witches who could take owl form at night.

Wales: The cry of the owl was said to be a harbinger of the loss of virginity for a young woman who heard it, which was not necessarily understood as a negative omen.

Language and Lore

People who are most productive and active during the night hours are called night owls, a usage that recognizes the owl's natural domain without the negative connotations attached to its more ominous folklore. The collective noun for a group of owls is a parliament, a word that speaks to the owl's ancient association with deliberation and wisdom.

Omens and Divinatory Meaning

The owl's extraordinary sensory precision cuts through obscuration. It can hear a mouse moving under snow and see in near-total darkness. If the owl comes to you, it may be telling you that you have exactly this ability right now: the capacity to see what is actually happening in a situation others find confusing. Trust that perception and focus it.

Be aware, however, that the owl cannot see what is close to it. Its farsightedness is a caution: are you so focused on the distant or the abstract that you are missing what is happening immediately around you? Do not be blind to what is right in front of you.

Do not allow the owl's darker cultural associations to prevent you from receiving its message. The owl arrives from the Otherworld, from the subconscious, from the uncertain edges of things. Its news may not be comfortable, but early warning is a gift. The forewarned can prepare, adjust, and meet what is coming with their eyes open rather than stumbling into it in the dark.

Finally, the owl calls you to your own inner knowing: your wisdom, your intuition, your ability to see through illusion and deception. In times of uncertainty, the owl is the reminder that you already possess more perception than you are currently trusting.

The Wisdom of Seeing Behind

The owl's reputation for wisdom is reinforced by its body. It can rotate its head roughly 270 degrees, so it can quite literally look behind itself, and its enormous eyes deepen the impression of a knowing creature. Because it is nocturnal, it is imagined to hold covert information, occult knowledge, and secrets denied to ordinary people. This is why the little owl became the attribute of the goddess of wisdom, Athena and her Roman form Minerva, and why the bird is so often shown perched atop a stack of books.

Solitary Hunter

Some of the owl's witch associations grow from its habits. It lives alone, gathering only to breed and parting again once the young have left the nest. Its specially adapted feathers let it fly in silence, and it hunts so efficiently that it can take many times the small rodents a cat would in a single night. Its kills were often accompanied by an eerie screech that superstitious country people blamed on supernatural forces.

The Moon's Mirror

As a creature of the feminine, the Moon, and prophecy, the owl is bound to clairvoyance. The Moon mirrors the Sun's light, and that reflected sight is itself a symbol of the second sight attributed to witches. This gift tends to carry gloomy news: in most places the shriek of an owl in the night foretells a death, though in Wales it instead signals the loss of virginity. Because the bird makes its home in derelict and abandoned buildings, it also gathered associations with destruction and decay.

The Owls of Laurium

The Athenians kept the owl as guardian of the Acropolis and esteemed it so highly as the companion of their goddess of wisdom that they stamped its image onto their coins, which became known as the Owls of Laurium. The phrase to bring owls to Athens carried the same sense as the modern saying about selling something to those who already have it in abundance.

Companion of Hecate

The owl is also a companion of Hecate, the witch queen and goddess of the underworld. The belief that the owl could pass freely between the lands of the living and the dead reached well beyond Rome and Britain, appearing among Native Americans and Africans and in China, Japan, and India, where the death god Yama is shown with an owl. In this role the owl is a psychopomp, guiding the souls of the dead into the afterlife.
