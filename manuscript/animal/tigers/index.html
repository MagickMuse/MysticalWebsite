Fierceness and Beauty

The word tiger alone calls up fierceness, swiftness, and great beauty. To fight like a tiger is to fight with utter savagery.

King of the Beasts

In China it is the tiger rather than the lion that rules as King of the Beasts, an emblem of nobility, power, ferocity, and authority. A distinction is drawn between the tamed tiger, able to master and direct its own savage nature, and the untamed one, a far more dangerous thing ruled by raw passion rather than reason. The tiger is also seen as an angry creature, a notion explained by the legend of the first tiger: a young boy beaten again and again by his teacher, the origin of its stripes, who was at last driven too far, fled into the forest in a blind rage, and was transformed into the beast.

Dominion and Taboo

To eat the flesh of the tiger or to wear its skin was said to grant a warrior dominion over the animal, a belief echoed in the old image of the hunter posed atop a tiger-skin rug, parading the triumph of his ego over the slain beast. Women were forbidden the meat for fear it would make them wayward or hard to govern.

Sexuality and the Were-Tiger

Like the wolf, the tiger serves as a symbol of raw sexuality. Chinese myth holds a tale much like the Western story of the girl and the wolf, with a tiger set in the wolf's place, and tells too of the were-tiger, a nocturnal monster driven to terrible deeds by the full Moon. The tiger is thought to be a shapeshifter, and certain people are said to hold the power to take its form. In these stories the tiger keeps to a village of its own, where every house is thatched with human hair. As with all powerful and magical animals, its name is seldom spoken aloud, and it is called instead by euphemism, such as the King of the Mountains.

Guardian and Protector

The tiger's savage strength also makes it a guardian, a mighty ally to have at one's side, all the more so because it was believed to devour evil spirits without harm to itself. For this reason effigies of tigers were set upon graves, and gamblers carried the tiger as a charm, since the animal was said to guard wealth. In Chinese myth five tigers protect the Universe from every quarter: a red tiger in the South, a black in the North, a blue in the East, a white in the West, and a golden tiger at the center ruling over all. That golden tiger is also the emblem of the Chinese emperor.

The Devoted Mother

The tiger is a fierce mother who will defend her cub to the death. An old tale offers counsel to anyone who has stolen a cub: to keep the mother from tearing you apart, cast a mirror in her path, and she will be fooled into mistaking her own reflection for her lost young.

The Buddhist Dichotomy

The tiger's double nature, savage yet protective, is captured by its place in Buddhist thought. Alongside the monkey, which stands for greed, and the deer, which stands for lovesickness, the tiger, embodying anger, is one of the Three Senseless Creatures of Chinese Buddhism. In Tibetan Buddhism, by contrast, it stands among the Four Dignities, representing confidence, awareness, kindness, and modesty, and Buddhists also find in the tiger a symbol of enlightenment and spiritual illumination.
