The magpie is one of the most symbolically complex birds in the world: simultaneously a herald of good luck and bad, a figure of love and death, a witch's familiar and a heavenly bridge-builder. Highly intelligent, deeply social, and impossible to overlook, it carries teachings about duality, the dangers of distraction, the balance of extremes, and the power of communication.

Physical Description

The magpie is a striking bird with the deep, glossy black plumage of a crow or raven set against a pure white chest and bold white blazes across the wings. In certain light its black feathers shift to blue, green, or purple iridescence. It measures approximately 20 inches in length, nearly half of which is its dramatic long tail, with a wingspan of about 23 inches and a weight of roughly 6 ounces.

Habitat and Distribution

Magpies are found in western and central Canada and the United States. They inhabit open woodland, meadows, and grasslands, and are highly comfortable in residential areas and around people. Broadly, magpie species range across North America, Europe, northwestern Africa, the Middle East, and Central and East Asia.

Behavior

The magpie is believed to be among the most intelligent of all animals. Its chattering call is one of its most recognizable features, and like all corvids it is highly social. Magpie nests are architectural feats: domed, complex, and large enough to take over a month to build. The magpie is omnivorous and opportunistic, eating insects, seeds, fruit, small mammals, and carrion. It is famous for its attraction to shiny objects, filching tinfoil, ribbons, and small coins to hoard. It will also land on large mammals such as deer to eat ticks.

Mythology and Folklore

East Asian traditions

In China and Korea the magpie is a bird of good luck and happiness. A chattering magpie means guests are arriving or good news is on its way; hearing one at the start of a journey blesses the errand with fortune. A Chinese festival called the Night of Sevens, or the Magpie Festival, is celebrated on the seventh day of the seventh lunar month, a celebration of lovers akin to Valentine's Day. The stars Vega and Altair, high in the sky at this time, represent two lovers, a princess and a cowherd, separated forever by the Milky Way. Once a year, all the magpies in the world rise in pity and form a bridge with their wings so the lovers may spend a single night together. In Chinese, the epithet Heavenly Woman is shared by celestial goddesses and magpies, which may be goddesses in disguise.

Western and British traditions

In Western folklore the magpie carries the opposite reputation: a thief, a wastrel, and a symbol of bad luck, much like its corvid kin. In England, seeing a single magpie is considered deeply unlucky; the charm against it requires tipping your hat, making the sign of the cross, or crossing two sticks on the ground. The well-known counting rhyme assigns a meaning to each number of magpies seen:

One for sorrow, two for mirth,
Three for a wedding, four for a birth,
Five for silver, six for gold,
Seven for a secret not to be told.
Eight for heaven, nine for hell,
And ten for the devil's own self.

In some American folklore a magpie landing on a roof means the house is sturdy and storm-proof; in other traditions the same omen signals the death of one of the inhabitants.

Russian and European witchlore

Virtually wherever magpies are found in Europe and Russia, they are associated with witchcraft. Scottish, Swedish, and Russian witches were said to commonly take magpie form; Latvian witches allegedly did so especially on Midsummer's Eve. One Russian nickname for witch is soroka-veschchitsa, magpie-witch. Several legends describe Ivan the Terrible gathering witches to burn them, only to have them transform into magpies and fly away. Metropolitan Alexei allegedly forbade magpies from flying over Moscow, recognizing them for what they truly were. The False Dmitrii's Polish wife, widely believed to be a sorceress, was said to have fled Moscow by flying away as a magpie. In some traditions, murdered witches reincarnate as magpies: they are true birds but their souls remain those of witches, and it is wise to treat them with respect.

Greek mythology

The nine daughters of King Peirus of Emathia challenged the Muses to a singing contest. Having lost, they were punished for their presumption by being transformed into nine chattering magpies, forever imitating the eloquence they could not match.

Name, Devil, and Mirror

The very name of the magpie speaks to its nature: "Mag" means chatterer, while "pie" comes from "pied," meaning black and white. Its sharp black-and-white markings made it, in Christian belief, a symbol of the Devil, said to be the one bird that refused to wear full mourning at the Crucifixion, and its gift for mimicking the human voice only deepened this devilish reputation among the superstitious. In Scotland the magpie was said to carry a drop of the Devil's blood on its tongue, so that it was wise to appease the bird, greeting it ("Good morning, Mr. Magpie") or doffing one's cap.

In Scandinavian lands witches were thought to take magpie form, or to shrink small enough to ride upon a magpie's back, though in Norway the bird is looked on more kindly and given corn to eat at Christmas. Its love of shiny things tied it to mirrors and reflections: in Japan a wife might receive her husband's gift of a mirror with suspicion, for the glass was believed able to turn into a magpie that would spy on her for him, and in China the backs of mirrors are still sometimes adorned with the magpie. In Greece the bird was the attribute of Dionysus, god of wine, since wine loosens the tongue and sets people chattering like magpies; from the Italian for magpie, gazza, comes our word gazette, a journal full of gossip.

Omens and Divinatory Meaning

The magpie's black-and-white coloring is its most fundamental symbol: duality, the coexistence of opposites, and the necessity of balance. Luck and misfortune, sacred and profane, hoarding and generosity, chatter and silence. Seeing a magpie often calls you to examine where your own life has fallen out of balance between these poles.

The magpie mind is a specific caution: the tendency to be drawn from one bright, attractive thing to the next, accumulating distractions rather than depth. Are shiny ideas, pleasures, or novelties pulling your attention away from what actually matters? Collect with discernment.

At the same time, the magpie is not only a warning. In East Asian tradition it is purely fortunate, a harbinger of guests, good news, and reunited love. Note the direction the magpie faces and what it does; the full picture may be more nuanced than the first impression.
