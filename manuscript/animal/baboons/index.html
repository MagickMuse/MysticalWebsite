Overview

Baboons are especially identified with witchcraft throughout Africa, alongside bats, hyenas, and owls. Historically and currently, baboons are understood to serve as witches' familiars or mounts, or even to be witches themselves. According to Egyptian myth, a baboon deity is responsible for the invention of magic. Baboons demonstrate how a creature once beheld as sacred, powerful, and godlike can become diabolized and perceived as worthless pests.

Species and Distribution

Baboons are descended from Old World monkeys. There are two subspecies: gelada and savanna. Savanna baboons are further divided into five sub-species: Chacma, Guinea, Olive, Yellow, and Hamadryas (the sacred or dogfaced baboon). Gelada baboons are found only in Ethiopia's Simien Mountains. Baboons are the most successful of all Africa's monkeys and are widely distributed throughout the continent. They are also found on the Arabian Peninsula.

Behavior and Characteristics

Baboons are smart, aggressive, organized, and clannish. They are wary and suspicious but will enter human territory looking for food as wild territory becomes scarce, taking fruit, produce, and occasionally baby goats. Male baboons possess huge, sharp canine teeth, which they display as signs of aggression and dominance. Baboons are highly intelligent and manually dexterous creatures.

Baboons have particularly prominent genitalia. They greet each other via genital presentation. The females' round genital swellings fluctuate in monthly rhythms similar to those of the moon and women's menstrual periods, which may explain their lunar associations. Similar to roosters and crows, baboons greet the sun with noisy chatter.

Egyptian Mythology

Hamadryas baboons were central to Egyptian religious practice. These impressive, regal creatures possess a square, symmetrical head and flowing leonine mane. They resemble a composite creature: part dog, part lion, part human, and part monkey. Hamadryas baboons were imported from the land of Punt (Horn of Africa) and were never indigenous to Egypt. Two of Egypt's most ancient deities share their shape: Thoth and Babi.

Living hamadryas baboons were perceived as either a manifestation of Lord Thoth or members of his retinue. Many baboons lived in temple complexes. Egyptian priests tested male baboons by placing writing implements before them. If a baboon picked one up and began scribbling, he was consecrated to Thoth or Ra.

Thoth: The Wise Deity

Lord Thoth was the supreme god Ra's right-hand man. Ra is the sun; Thoth is affiliated with the moon. Thoth rides through the skies as protective escort for Ra's solar barque. Thoth's nature is calm, rational, and sharply intellectual. He is a "cool" deity who doesn't anger easily, thinks before reacting, argues rather than attacks, and defuses volatile situations. During a mythological episode when Ra's daughter Sekhmet descended to Earth in an uncontrollable murderous rampage, it was Thoth who successfully disarmed her and led her back home.

Babi: The Fierce Deity

Babi (a.k.a. Baba) is a primordial god from whose name the word "baboon" derives. Lord of the Night Sky, Babi is called the Bull of the Baboons, the pre-eminent alpha male. He is the god of testosterone. Babi is fierce, aggressive, and belligerent. He steals offerings from other spirits and devours human entrails. He was a role model for pharaohs, who prayed to possess Babi's power, ferocity, instant reactions, and virility.

Babi controls the darkness. His phallus serves as the bolt on the gates of heaven. The boat that ferries souls to the next life uses Babi's phallus as its mast. Although recognized as a destructive force allied with the spirit Set, his powers were also perceived as potentially beneficial. Babi wards off snakes, controls darkness and turbulent waters. He had no formal cult; his relationships with people derive entirely through magical action, including spells and amulets. Babi heals afflictions of the penis and is Master of Sex in the afterlife. Men were buried with magic spells identifying their sexuality with Babi's to retain virility after death.

Cultural Shifts and Modern Persecution

Perceptions changed dramatically over time. In medieval Europe, the hamadryas baboon became a symbol of lust as deadly sin. Baboons in general came to represent evil spirits. Ironically, once sacred symbols of fertility, baboons' proliferation caused them to be exterminated as vermin, with some communities offering bounties on their heads.
