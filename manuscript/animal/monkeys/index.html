Symbolism

Many animals simply embody aspects of their own nature, and the monkey is mischievous, agile, and possessed of a wily intelligence. It is a fine mimic, though it is not always clear that it grasps what it imitates, and in this sense the monkey becomes a symbol of randomness, a recurring emblem of chaotic, unguided, unconscious action. In Christian symbolism it takes on a rather unfavorable aspect, standing for humanity degraded by sin and so reverting to a lower form of life.

In the Ramayana it is the monkey Hanuman who helps Rama, an avatar of Vishnu, to rescue his wife from a demon, and he is deified as his reward. The monkey is a sexually active and fertile creature, and in India women have been known to strip off their clothes and embrace an effigy of Hanuman in the belief that this will help them conceive.

In ancient Egypt apes were held in such high esteem that they received burial rites of a status similar to those given to people, perhaps because the monkey shares so many of our traits. Monkeys also have a propensity for anger, and the Egyptians, recognizing this, used the hieroglyph of the monkey to mean angry.

In the Mayan calendar the monkey held a place of prominence, regarded as hardworking, a gifted orator, and talented in artistic pursuits, so that to call someone a monkey was a great compliment. In Japan, by contrast, it is unlucky to speak the word monkey during a wedding ceremony, owing to a curious superstition that doing so might cause the bride to run away. Even so, monkey dolls are given to children for protection, since all monkeys, even stuffed toys, are believed to drive away evil spirits. The monkey also appears in the Chinese Zodiac, and those born under its sign are said to be intelligent, agile, decisive, and entertaining.
