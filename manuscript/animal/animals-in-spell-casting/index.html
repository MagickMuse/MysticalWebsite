Animal Grimoire

Animals in Spell-casting

Spell-casting draws on the power of animal allies and familiars, not their physical bodies. The magickal energy carried by specific animal species can support your workings when approached with respect and clear intention.

The End of Anatomy-Based Spells

For much of recorded history, cultures around the world folded animal body parts into their spellwork. Whether those practices ever delivered genuine results is beside the point. The natural world has been thrown severely out of balance, and workings that depend on animal remains are no longer viable. Every such spell ultimately depends on the cooperation of the presiding Animal Spirit, and those spirits will not lend their power to practitioners who cause harm to the creatures in their care. Anatomy-based workings risk actively backfiring. Magick is not static; it shifts with the conditions of the world it operates in. Techniques that served one era must be rethought for the present.

The historical prominence of these practices may also be exaggerated. Outside observers and storytellers have always gravitated toward the most sensational elements of magick. Hoodoo, for instance, is a deeply sophisticated tradition, yet popular imagination reduces it to the notorious black cat bone while ignoring the enormous body of plant-based and spiritual work that actually defines the system. Historical context matters as well. In periods when people raised and butchered their own livestock, leftover parts found secondary uses as a matter of course, magickal uses among them. A Romany protective amulet known as "eyes of the crayfish" sounds grisly until you discover it refers to calcium deposits scraped from the inside of a crayfish shell, one that was already destined for the cooking pot.

Image Magick: The Ancient Alternative

Animal spells can be updated for modern practice without sacrificing potency. Candles molded into animal shapes, charms cast in metal or carved from gemstone, and sculpted figurines all channel a species' inherent energy while leaving living creatures untouched. A copper hummingbird charm, for example, pairs the bird's longstanding role in Central American love magick with the complementary energy of the metal itself. Consecrating such a charm to the animal's spirit deepens its power, something that simply cannot be achieved with body parts obtained through suffering.

This is not a modern concession to squeamishness. Animal image magick is among the oldest and most potent branches of the art. The Biblical golden calf was exactly this: a magickal image that fused animal symbolism with metallic energy, shaped by collective human fear and longing into a powerful conduit. A living calf would have been far easier to come by, but the crafted image held greater magickal force.

Sacrifice Belongs to Religion, Not Magick

Animal sacrifice is a religious act, and it is entirely separate from spell-casting. Every major religious tradition has practiced it at some point. Some still do, others have moved on. In every case, rigorous training and formal initiation are prerequisites. No tradition permits untrained individuals to perform sacrificial rites on their own.

Where magick brushes up against religious territory, symbolic substitution is the long-established norm. Petition Aphrodite with a dove-shaped candle rather than injuring one of her sacred birds. Present Hekate with a miniature stone dog rather than a living animal. This is not a softened modern adaptation. More than two thousand years ago, worshippers who could not afford formal temple sacrifices, or who simply preferred private devotion, offered small stone fetishes to Hekate and obtained the results they sought.

Decoding the Grimoires

Many of the classical European grimoires trace their lineage to fragments of Alexandrian spell-books that circulated through Europe, Arabia, and North Africa long before the invention of the printing press. The professional magickians of Roman-era Alexandria developed an intricate coded vocabulary to keep their formulas out of unauthorized hands. That cipher was eventually recovered and translated from a collection known as the Greek Magickal Papyri.

The decrypted results are revealing. A formula calling for "heart of a hawk" actually specifies wormwood. "Lion's tongue" turns out to be turnip. "Hercules' semen" is arugula. A "physician's bone" is sandstone. Many, and quite possibly all, animal references in these texts originally pointed to plants and minerals. But centuries of hand-copying without the accompanying key led later magickians to read the ingredients at face value, never recognizing the professional shorthand underneath.

This kind of coded terminology is not limited to antiquity. Certain contemporary Amazonian shamans still maintain a secret professional lexicon specifically to prevent their knowledge from being exploited by outsiders.

The practical lesson is simple: never perform a working that makes you uncomfortable. Botanical and mineral ingredients are routinely named after animals. Deer's tongue is a grass prized for promoting eloquence. Swallow's blood is a red mineral powder with no blood whatsoever. Dragon's blood, one of the most widely used spell ingredients on the planet, is resin tapped from the Indonesian tree Dracaena draco, so named for its vivid red color. (A completely separate substance also called dragon's blood appears in Peruvian magickal practice, equally botanical and entirely unrelated to the Indonesian variety.)

Animal-Sourced Fragrances and Their Substitutes

Four historically significant magickal fragrances originate from animal sources: civet, musk, ambergris, and castoreum. In practical terms, only synthetic reproductions are available today, since the genuine materials are extraordinarily scarce and prohibitively expensive. Because scarcity has always been the norm, botanical stand-ins have existed for centuries:

  • Musk substitute: Ambrette (Abelmoschus moschatus), an Indian shrub also known as musk mallow, prized for its aromatic seed
  • Ambergris substitute: Labdanum, a resin harvested from the rock-rose (Cistus creticus, Cistus ladanifer). Cretan labdanum is said to offer the closest approximation, though French, Moroccan, and Spanish sources also produce usable material

When Spells Involve Living Animals

Certain workings call for a living creature's participation. Any animal involved must be treated with the same care and respect extended to a human or spirit collaborator, and must emerge from the spell in the same or better condition than when it entered. Spells that incorporate cuts of meat assume the practitioner acquires them the same way they would for an ordinary meal. Many workings call for eggs, honey, or milk. Practitioners who avoid animal products can simply choose from the enormous number of spells that require none. Magick and everyday life are not separate domains. If something clashes with your ethics outside of a ritual context, it will clash inside one too.

Collar Protection Magick

A pet's collar is a natural framework for protective spellwork. Any charged object can be fastened to it, and incorporating knot magick into the attachment strengthens the working further. Common additions include:

  • Clear quartz to amplify the animal's aura and provide a layer of energetic shielding
  • Rose quartz to extend the protective force of your love around the animal
  • Other crystals, protective amulets, religious medals, or similar charms selected to suit the animal's needs

Always charge and consecrate objects before attaching them to the collar. The collar itself can also be consecrated as a whole protective unit once everything is in place.

Magick Day Protection

Certain days of the year are believed to carry an extra charge of magickal power. Spells performed on these days benefit people and animals alike. The most important dates for animal protection include:

  • Beltane / May Day / Walpurgis (May 1): Fire rites dominate. Driving cattle between twin bonfires is the classic Beltane blessing for health, fertility, and protection. Water charged with a crystal ball on May Day shields cattle from bewitchment.
  • Midsummer's Day / Saint John's Day (June 24): Both fire and water rites are traditional. Animals may be led through flaming hoops of hemp, blessed with burning branches of hazel or rowan, or bathed in ocean waves or salted water. Some practitioners substitute the exact date of the summer solstice.
  • Saint George's Day (April 23): Traditionally the day livestock were first led out to pasture. Sunwise processions around the barn or field, torchlit parades, and elaborate blessing rites involving palm fronds, barley, and holy water are all performed on this date.

Barn and Livestock Protection

Folk traditions for shielding domestic animals from malevolent magick center on the barn and its immediate surroundings. These practices layer multiple forms of protection and can be combined freely.

  • Birch trees planted near the barn ward off harmful magick. If the barn is already standing, transplant birch trees as close as possible.
  • Red and white ribbons tied to the birch trees, or to other nearby trees, reinforce the protective boundary.
  • Lilies and primroses planted around the barn's perimeter create a living protective ring. Keep them maintained and replace as needed.
  • A hatchet buried sharp side up beneath the barn's threshold forces any malevolent energy to be cut as animals cross over it entering and leaving.
  • Horseshoes posted above the barn door protect animals just as they protect humans. In the Hungarian tradition, drawing a horseshoe over the door with black chalk serves the same purpose.
  • Silver water: Keep a piece of real silver (a small charm or coin) in a dish or bucket of water out of the animals' reach. Sprinkle the animals with this water once a week, then replace with fresh water.
  • Rose of Jericho Water sprinkled over the animals once a week provides an additional layer of cleansing and protection.

Cattle Spells

SATOR Spell

The SATOR square is one of the oldest known word squares, used historically in protective and binding magick. This version channels its power directly into the livestock.

  1. Write the following on a piece of paper:
SATOR
AREPO
TENET
OPERA
ROTAS
  1. Grind it up and add it to the cattle feed. Have the cows eat the words.

Dog Spells

Child's Dog Hair Medicine Bag

Dog hair carries the loyal, protective energy of its owner. This simple charm extends that protection to a child.

  1. Fill a medicine bag with loose dog hair.
  2. Add any other lucky charms, as desired.
  3. Give to a child to wear or carry for protection.

Greyhound Spirit Protection

A home where a well cared for, happy greyhound lives will allegedly never be haunted by malicious spirits.

Pacify Threatening Dogs Spell (Christian Pow-Wow)

Pow-Wow strongly incorporates the use of spoken charms. This one is used to soothe a threatening dog and provide protection, excellent for those forced to walk past a snarling, snapping canine.

The charm must be repeated three times before you reach the dog (or the dog reaches you!):

Dog, hold thy nose to the ground!
God has made thee, me and hound.
In the name of the Father, the Son and the Holy Ghost.

Then form the sign of the cross three times.

This spell as recorded in 1820 by John George Hohman in his definitive Book of Pow-Wows: The Long Lost Friend omits the last line of the charm, but includes the crosses.

Pacify Threatening Dogs Spell (Pagan-Friendly Version)

Historically, there have always been two movements within the Pow-Wow community: those who emphasize Christian orientation and those who do not. Rhymes were adapted as needed and continue to evolve. This pagan-friendly version calls upon those Greek divinities that exert great influence over hounds:

Dog, hold your nose to the ground!
Creator made you, me and hound.
In the name of the triple goddess, Selene, Artemis and Hecate.

Repeat three times. The cross, as a pre-Christian symbol of protection, may still be formed. A pentacle, crescent moon, downward facing triangle or other shape may also be substituted, as desired.

Rhodium Oil Spell to Lure Dogs Away

This perfume oil allegedly attracts dogs and was once used by thieves in order to lure dogs away. Play with the proportions until you achieve an effective fragrance. It may work on other animals too. Experiment and see.

Rhodium Oil consists of:

  • Essential oil of cedarwood
  • Essential oil of rose geranium
  • Essential oil of palmarosa
  • Essential oil of sandalwood
  • Blend with a carrier oil, such as jojoba oil.

Rhodium Oil may be used in various ways:

  • Wear it as a perfume
  • Use the oil to mark a trail

Stop Dogs Barking Spell

Place leaves of hound's tongue in your shoes, beneath your feet. Alternatively, carry within a charm bag.
