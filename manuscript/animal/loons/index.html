The loon is a striking aquatic bird of northern lakes, as celebrated for its haunting voice as it is for its impressive diving ability. It carries deep associations with the dream world, hidden knowledge, and the mysteries of the night.

Physical Description

The common loon measures approximately 31 inches in length with a wingspan of roughly 45 inches, weighing around 9 pounds. Its plumage is dramatically patterned: black head and neck, a white-spotted black back, and a clean white belly. A ringed collar of white feathers speckled with black encircles the neck. The bill, legs, and webbed feet are black. The eyes are edged with white or grey and shift from vivid red in summer to grey in winter.

Habitat and Distribution

Loons inhabit lakes and ponds across Canada, Iceland, Greenland, Alaska, and the northern United States. They winter along the coasts of the British Isles and parts of Europe, favoring open water for both feeding and flight. Because their legs are positioned far back on the body for maximum diving efficiency, loons are clumsy on land and nearly incapable of taking off from the ground. They require a long water runway, running across the surface to build enough speed to lift into flight.

Behavior

Loons are exceptional swimmers and divers, propelling themselves underwater with powerful strokes to pursue fish. They also swallow small amounts of gravel from the lake bed to aid digestion. Their calls are among the most recognizable sounds in the natural world: a wavering yodel, a trembling wail, and a slow, resonant hoot that carries across water after dark. The cry is most often heard at night, lending the loon its strong associations with darkness and the unseen.

Mythology and Folklore

The name loon may derive from the Swedish word lom, meaning lame, a reference to the bird's labored movement on land. In English, the word shares a sound family with lunatic and loony, both rooted in the French lune for moon, reinforcing the loon's folkloric tie to lunar energy and altered states of mind.

In a Native American origin myth, Loon and Raven were both originally white birds. The two agreed to tattoo each other, and Loon was marked first. When it was Raven's turn to hold still, he could not stop fidgeting, and Loon ended up covering him entirely in ink. As Loon fled, Raven hurled an oil lamp after him, striking him in the legs and pushing them backward on his body, explaining why loons move so awkwardly on land.

The common loon is the state bird of Minnesota and the provincial bird of Ontario. It appears on the reverse of the Canadian one-dollar coin, which is colloquially known as the "loonie."

Omens and Divinatory Meaning

The loon's voice carries both peace and an undercurrent of longing, making it a powerful omen of emotional depth. As a water bird, the loon connects to the subconscious, the dream world, and all that moves beneath the surface of waking life. Encountering a loon invites you to pay closer attention to your dreams and inner knowing.

Its cry most often rises after sunset, linking the loon to the moon, hidden secrets, and nocturnal inspiration. The creative and intuitive gifts that surface in the night are under its domain. The loon may also be nudging you toward a touch of wildness: to release rigid thinking and allow yourself to be a little unruly, a little untamed.
