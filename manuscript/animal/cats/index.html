Overview

The cat, that animal which famously does not come when called, has long been an emblem of independence and of free, independent, autonomous women. Historically, attitudes toward cats parallel those toward women: when women have been respected and honored, their psychic gifts cherished, cats are beloved, sometimes deified; when women are perceived as dangerous and perverse, cats have been degraded and demonized. No other animal in the Western world is as identified with witchcraft as the domestic cat, particularly black cats.

Nature and Characteristics

Cats are beautiful, intelligent creatures that cannot be ruled. The entire concept of obedience is foreign to their nature. Cats perform various services for humans, but only on their own terms; they can only be enticed, not commanded. Cats epitomize the pleasure principle: sensuous creatures, they crave warmth, fine foods, soft fabrics, the choice seat in the house. Nocturnal, independent and mysterious, cats come and go as they please. Although domestic creatures, cats are never entirely tame.

Sexual Associations and Fertility

Cats have profound associations with sex. Both male and female cats have reputations as lusty, prodigiously sexual creatures. Since at least the time of Aristotle, cats have been used to symbolize lasciviousness and sexual insatiability. Cats are affiliated with sexually autonomous female deities like Bastet, Diana, Freya, Hathor, Hecate, Cybele, and Lilith. By the eighteenth century, "cat" had become slang for a prostitute.

Cats possess lush fertility. Felis, the scientific name for cats, derives from the Latin root fe, "to bear young." Other words from this source are feline, fecund and fetus. Cats, like frogs, are often considered weather harbingers, announcing the start of fertilizing rain. Jewelry depicting a mother cat surrounded by a large litter of kittens was a popular fertility charm in ancient Egypt.

Early Domestication

The earliest indication of intimate relations between people and cats derives from a burial in Cyprus, dating back approximately 9500 years ago. A carefully buried cat was discovered inches from a human burial. The cat's bones were arranged to parallel that of the human and displayed no signs of butchering. It appears to have been a beloved companion animal.

General wisdom suggests cats were first domesticated in Egypt, where domestic cats were bred at least four thousand years ago. As Egyptian culture became increasingly agrarian, stored grain attracted vermin, who attracted cats, who quickly demonstrated their usefulness. Cats became sacred guardians of the grain. Wide distribution of domestic cats occurred only after the Egyptian kingdom lost its independence to Rome. Not until the fourth century CE were domestic cats widely distributed in Italy.

Bastet: Egyptian Sacred Cat

The most famous sacred Egyptian cat is Bastet, whose titles included Mistress of the Oracle and Great Conjuress of the Casket. She is depicted as a cat, often bejeweled, or as a woman with a cat's head, sometimes surrounded by kittens. Bastet has dominion over sex, fertility, marriage, magic, music, childbirth, prosperity, joy, dance, and healing. She provides protections against infertility, the dangers of childbirth, evil spirits, illness and bodily injuries. Bastet offers special protection to women and children and serves as matron of magicians and healers.

Egyptian Magic and Cats

In Egyptian magickal practice, cats were admired as hunters, and hunting served as a metaphor for overcoming dangerous forces. A tawny wildcat helped the sun-god Ra defeat the evil serpent Apep. In Book of the Dead illustrations, a cat slices a snake's body in two with a knife.

This spell animates a cat amulet (specified as lapis lazuli, though faience was more common):

"Oh cat of lapis lazuli, great of forms, mistress of the embalming house, grant offerings to So-and-so in the Beautiful West!"

Being "great of forms" meant having multiple manifestations or bau, a sign of how powerful a deity was.

Sacred Cat Mummies

Thousands of mummified cats and kittens were deposited at temple sites and cemeteries as offerings to Bastet. Most were hastened to the afterlife by human hands (snapped necks or drowning provided embalmers with bodies for mummification). Some "cat mummies" contained only earth and pebbles. It was the wrapping and priestly blessing that mattered when offering a cat mummy and asking for divine intervention.

From Lioness to Cat

In her earliest forms, Bastet had a lioness's head like her sister Sekhmet. The two may represent different aspects of the same primordial power: Sekhmet the fierce, destructive heat of the sun, and Bastet the gentler, protective warmth. Later, Bastet appeared as a beautiful woman with the head of a domesticated cat.

Worship of Bastet reached its zenith during the reign of Osorkon II (874-853 BCE). Bastet's annual festival in Bubastis was Egypt's most popular festival, renowned for parties, revelry, and drunkenness. The festival celebrated female sexuality and generative power. Celebrants would shout sexual obscenities, dance wildly, and perform anasuromai, the ritual act of lifting up the skirts to expose the vulva, associated with laughter, healing, and defiance of grief.

Even house cats maintained their mysterious, sacred nature in Egypt. Herodotus reports that anyone convicted of intentionally killing a sacred animal was sentenced to death. When a pet cat died, it was customary for the human family to shave their eyebrows as a sign of mourning and respect.

Freya and Norse Tradition

Cats have potent associations with Freya, Norse Lady of love, romance, sex, fertility, childbirth, shamanism, enchantments, witchcraft, and death. An oracular, sexually autonomous spirit, Freya's chariot is drawn by her familiars, two huge gray cats named Bee Gold (honey) and Tree Gold (amber), who embody Freya's twin qualities of ferociousness and fecundity. Cats are Freya's sacred animals; a traditional method of petitioning or pleasing the goddess was to offer pans of milk to cats. To be kind to a cat is to entreat Freya's blessings.

Eventually cats would be tortured and killed specifically because of their associations with Freya. After the introduction of Christianity, Freya became among the most demonized of spirits, coming to embody the stereotype of the seductive witch. Cats shared Freya's demonization.

The Witchcraze and Persecution

By the Middle Ages, cats had become so identified with witchcraft that in 1484, Pope Innocent VIII issued a decree, understood by many scholars as the official start of the Witchcraze, denouncing cats and their owners. Any cat in the company of a woman could be assumed to be a familiar. Pope Innocent commanded that when a witch was burned, her cats were to be burned with her. He decreed that all European cat-worshippers be burned as witches. He authorized the killing of cats even without an accompanying witch.

A vicious cycle emerged: the destruction of European cats is believed to have encouraged the proliferation of rodents, which in turn encouraged the spread of deadly disease, blamed on witchcraft and heresy, which in turn led to fear, panic, and more killing of cats. The degradation and destruction of cats parallels that of women and their increasing loss of autonomy.

Cats in Witch-Hunter Beliefs

According to witch-hunter perceptions, cats played various roles: Cats were believed to be common familiars. A close relationship with a cat was considered telltale evidence of witchcraft, sufficient to warrant death for woman and cat. Cats provided witches with magical transportation. Witches rode to sabbats on cats' backs or traveled in feline form. Shape-shifting witches were believed able to assume the form of cats.

Sometimes the cats are the witches. Cats are traditionally Hungarian witches' alter egos. Witch-hunter Jean Bodin insisted that all cats are witches in disguise. Nicholas Remy argued they were demons instead. Hungarian witch-lore suggests cats become witches between ages 7 and 12, though shaving a cross into the cat's fur was believed sufficient to prevent this. According to Somerset folklore, cats born in May were especially inclined to be witches in disguise and hence were frequently killed.

Documented Witch Trials

Not all legends are fictional. In 1586, Anna Winkelz Ipfel was burned as a witch in Bergtheim, Germany for allegedly disguising herself as a black cat. In 1607, Bartie Paterson was hanged as a witch in England. According to witnesses, Bartie transformed into a cat and, together with other witches disguised as cats, "sang" in the backyard. In March 1607, Isobel Grierson was brought to court in Scotland, charged with witchcraft. She allegedly invaded the Clarke household in the middle of the night in the form of a cat, accompanied by other cats. Isobel was burned to death.

In 1233 Pope Gregory IX declared that heretics worshipped the devil in the form of a black tom cat. In Slavic areas, especially Russia and Poland, the ovinnik, guardian spirit of the threshing barn takes the form of a huge disheveled black cat with glowing eyes. Offerings of blini or the last sheaves of grain are offered to him in exchange for protection and divination services.

Ritual Killings

The torture and killing of cats occurred in various contexts: in conjunction with human witch trials, as random acts of violence, but also as organized, documented ritual killings. In Paris, it was customary to burn a sack or basket filled with cats in the Place de Greve on St John's Eve, a tradition also popular in other parts of France. Although Louis XIV abolished the Parisian custom in 1648, it continued in the provinces until at least as late as 1796. Various French towns built bonfires to burn masses of cats on the first Sunday in Lent. Cats were burned in Alsace at Easter.

Black Cat Superstitions

Although all cats are associated with witchcraft, the black cat is most powerfully identified. Black cats' special identification with witchcraft is not limited to Western European or Christian perspectives. Chinese, Hindu, Japanese, Jewish, North African, and Romany witch-lore make the same connection, although whether it is understood to be a sacred or malevolent connection depends upon cultural and individual perceptions.

People tend to fear or love black cats, frequently revealing their attitudes toward witchcraft. The major superstition regarding black cats is that they bring bad luck should they cross your path and people will cross the street to avoid them. This isn't so ridiculous if one recalls that black cats were once commonly believed to be witches in disguise. In Britain, however, black cats are lucky; white cats are identified with bad luck, perhaps because of associations with ghosts and death.

Tabby Cat: Unquestioned Worthiness

Felis catus

Twitching in sunlight, Tabby Cat dreams ancestral visions of savannahs and sleeping in rocky crevices. As a wild cat descendant, Tabby still bears stripe and spot remnants. But she's pleased to have moved indoors—adopting humans was brilliant strategy, she purrs, stretching and kneading paws.

Tabby never questions whether she's earned the warm window sunpatch or evening tuna. She never doubts her worthiness. Tabby licks a paw to smooth the fur behind her ear. You are already enough, she reminds you. Breathe into that knowing.

Independence and Interdependence

Some theorize that domesticated cats, compared to their ancestors, remain permanently in a kitten-like developmental stage, never acquiring the capacity for independent hunting or self-care, with humans serving as surrogate mothers. Yet Tabby Cat's aura of self-sufficiency and independence stands at odds with this notion, suggesting that dependence and autonomy are not mutually exclusive states.

Mountain Lion is the wild, full-grown cousin of Cat. Mountain Lion elevates Tabby Cat's teachings, helping us grasp not only our own worth but others' worth; to watch and observe for understanding; and to move through the world balancing power with intentional action. Mountain Lion leads herself without worrying who follows—ironically, this sometimes leaves her with a trail of initiates.

Cross-Cultural Cat Lore

Even the most ordinary household cat carries a mystique and the promise of the supernatural powers people have attributed to cats for thousands of years. In the West the cat, especially the black cat, belongs to the witch as her familiar, her companion, and her alter ego. It shares magickal secrets and arcane knowledge that it cannot explain to ordinary people, since they do not speak its language, and an unspoken understanding passes between the witch and her grimalkin that surpasses any language used by other creatures. The Saga of Eric the Red preserves a full description of a witch and prophetess skilled in rune-craft whose costume included a hood lined with white cat skin and cat-skin mittens.

The ancient Egyptians honored the cat so highly that they revered it as the deity Bast. Cats whose fur carried three different colors, or whose eyes were of differing shades, were especially prized for their Bast-like appearance, a reminder that power did not belong to the black cat alone. Egyptian priests believed that cats carried the magnetic forces of nature, so that close company with them opened access to those powers.

Artemis, also called Diana and goddess of the hunt, was associated with the cat, itself renowned as a hunter. When a cat sleeps it curls into a circle with its head touching its tail, a shape close to the ouroboros, and like that ancient mythical creature the cat became a symbol of immortality.

The cat does not enjoy an honorable reputation everywhere. In the Buddhist tradition it is regarded with suspicion as a base, earthly creature lacking respect, because it was absent at the death and spiritual liberation of the Buddha, when it should have been present. The only other creature missing was the serpent. This connection between cat and serpent appears in the Kabbalah as well, and in Christianity, where a cat placed at the feet of Christ carries the same negative imagery as the snake.

Although the black cat is the archetypal good luck symbol in the West, the reverse holds in Islam, where cats are viewed favorably unless they are black, since djinn can take the shape of black cats. The magickal powers of the cat are ambivalent, used either for or against people, a trait tied to the indifference with which a cat treats its prey. In Western tradition the cat has nine lives, while its Eastern counterpart manages with only seven.

A Persian belief echoes the idea of the witch and her familiar. Some people are born with a hemzad, a spirit that accompanies them throughout life in the form of a cat. The cat's blood was thought to be especially potent for writing charms, which further underlines the magickal nature attributed to the animal.
