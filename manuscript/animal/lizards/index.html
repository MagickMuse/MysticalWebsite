Five-lined Skink: The Daring Risk-Taker

Plestiodon fasciatus

Five-lined Skink is an adrenaline junkie, the Evel Knievel of the lizard world. This speed demon flashes his bright blue tail, baiting hawks and snakes. "Catch me if you can!" Skink trills, diving into crevices. Skink knows he's superfast, but that's not why he baits the big predators. His superpower transcends speed: Skink can lose his tail and survive. So he flicks it around like a bright blue flag then scurries for shadows, cackling as he goes.

Skink reminds you to delight in daring—that to really live, you must be willing to risk your tail.

Growth Through Loss

Regret often links to times you didn't dare, opportunities missed because you feared what you might lose or have to surrender. Skink knows that when he loses his tail, a new one grows in its place.

This is one of life's great secrets: When you lose something, you inadvertently create room for something new to grow. You've probably already experienced this in your own life—people, jobs, homes lost. Loss breaks your heart but also reveals your strength, and that's where growth comes in.

What have you learned about yourself through loss? What new growth has emerged because you've lost your metaphoric tail?

Symbolism

Drawn to the heat of the Sun and closely tied to its light, the lizard stands for the soul that seeks knowledge and enlightenment. Christian symbolism reads the stillness of the lizard basking in the warmth of the sun as the equal of contemplative religious ecstasy. Egyptian hieroglyphs treat it as a benevolent creature, its sign carrying the meaning of plentiful, while elsewhere in Africa the lizard is a messenger between humankind and God, one of whose stranger messages was that no life should be expected after death.

Like the snake, the lizard sloughs off its dead skin, so both creatures symbolize rebirth and regeneration. For this reason the lizard was the favored animal of the shaman, seeming to hold power of life over death, and it was believed able to walk in both the seen and the unseen worlds, the realms of the living and the dead.

The importance of the lizard to the Inca shows in its dramatic appearance among the Nazca Lines carved into the Pampas Colorado of Peru, where it is the largest single figure at around 180 meters long, properly visible only from above, from the viewpoint of the Gods. Because the lizard is hard to kill and wears a tough, scaly, armor-like skin, warriors wore that skin as a protective talisman.
