Honeybee: Solar Navigation

Apis mellifera

Honeybee flies a zigzag path searching for pollen patches. Upon discovering one, she returns home to dance, showing her sisters the exact angle where sun hits the pollen location. Through this aerial communication, her sisters triangulate the feast's location. Bee's internal compass proves incredibly accurate, and she can locate the sun even on cloudy days.

The sun traditionally represents expansive, moving energies: our passions and our ability to manifest them in the world. When life feels cloudy and dull, call on Honeybee to help you navigate toward joy. During dark times, she reminds you that clouds pass and your passion and purpose will reveal themselves again.

Honeybee's Lessons

Two and a half million years ago, our ancestors' brains grew exponentially. The human brain uses incredible amounts of glucose, which honey provides. Some say Honeybee played a key role in our evolution. Ironically, our big brains cause overanalysis and rumination, two things most likely to extinguish our inner fire and dampen passion.

Honeybee creates honey surplus (her own liquid sunshine) to sustain her hive through winter months.

Made of interlocking hexagons that bees build cooperatively and efficiently, beehives are highly organized places with nurseries for young and rooms where worker bees pack pollen and nectar into wax-capped cells.

Sweetness and the Ancient World

In the ancient world the bee was an insect of great importance, since the honey it produced was one of the few naturally sweet substances then available and also served as a good preservative. That importance shows in the bee's appearance on the coins of Ephesus and in Minoan imagery that depicted a figure as half woman and half bee. In Egypt the bee stood as a symbol of the Lower Kingdom.

The bee is an emblem of industry and mutual cooperation, though it carries a spiritual side as well. The golden honeycomb served as one of the symbols of a goddess whose priestesses were believed to live on within the bodies of bees. These priestesses were called Melissae, a name drawn from the same root as the words for honey and bees. Their male counterparts, equivalent to the drones, were called Essenes and were eunuchs. Because bees produce wax, they came to be closely linked with places of Christian worship such as abbeys, where monks and nuns used that wax to make candles for the church.

Order, Immortality, and Sacrifice

Bees stand as symbols of order and of immortality. A queen needs to mate only once to spend the rest of her life producing offspring, and the drone whose task it is to impregnate her splits in half once that task is done. The workers feed the drones, which cannot feed themselves, but once the queen is mated the efficient workers simply stop feeding them and let them starve before dragging them to the entrance of the hive. Because a bee dies when it uses its sting, it became a symbol of great heroism and sacrifice, unlike the wasp, which can sting again and again.

Prophecy, Transmutation, and Sacred Geometry

The bee performs an elaborate dance to reveal the whereabouts of a particularly rich crop of flowers. Bees are believed to hold magickal powers to foresee the future and are regarded by many as deities in their own right. For this reason it has long been customary, over hundreds of years, for the beekeeper to tell the bees of a death in the household and to send word of the deceased to them. Because bees feed on the nectar of flowers, and so ultimately on sunlight, they act as agents of transmutation, making something from nothing. They are mystical creatures that both understand and embody some of our most ancient and powerful symbols, building their honeycomb from thousands of perfectly symmetrical hexagons, a structure that holds the secrets of the Flower of Life and of the six pointed star, or hexagram.
