The parrot is best known for its gift of mimicry, prized for centuries as an affectionate companion and revered across cultures as a solar bird, a sacred emblem of love, and a teacher who speaks in borrowed words.

The Gift of Mimicry

The parrot's most famous quality is its ability to talk, or more precisely to mimic. To learn something parrot fashion is to memorize words without grasping their meaning. A parrot said to have belonged to Julius Caesar was trained to cry Hail Caesar whenever its master drew near, though whether the bird ever understood its own greeting was never settled.

Companion of Sailors

Appealing and affectionate, parrots have been kept as domestic pets for centuries, and the Greeks and Romans were among those charmed by them. The bird is especially tied to sailors, and to pirates in particular, who carried parrots home from their native range in southern China as living trophies of their voyages.

Solar Bird and Sacred to Love

Native American traditions link the parrot's bright feathers to the brilliance of the Sun, making it a solar symbol. For Brahmans the parrot is sacred and must never be harmed; it is dedicated to Kama, the god of love, because people in love unconsciously mirror one another's gestures and speech just as the parrot mirrors sound. For this reason parrots are emblems of marriage in northern India, and their images are hung in the temples where weddings take place.

The Teaching Parrot

A traditional cycle of folktales first written in Sanskrit takes the form of linked stories nested one inside another. In it a parrot tells seventy tales to keep a woman from straying onto the wrong path in life, casting the bird as a moral guide whose endless talk serves a protective purpose.
