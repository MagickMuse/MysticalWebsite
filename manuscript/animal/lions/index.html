Overview

The lion is one of the most powerful symbols in Egyptian magick. The lioness in particular was not to be trifled with. Given to fury and foul moods during which she brought disease to humankind, the lioness represented the fierce, protective, and destructive aspects of divine power.

Sekhmet: The Lioness Goddess

Ra's daughter Sekhmet always appeared with the head of a lioness. Offerings of beer helped pacify her, while her priests worked to cure ailments she caused.

Some demons were messengers from Sekhmet, who brought disease, warfare, and strife into the world. During the five epagomenal "demon days" at the end of the Egyptian year, Sekhmet's twelve demons ran amok. Magicians made special amulets to protect clients from these forces.

The Sphinx

The pharaoh could be represented as the sphinx, a lion's body with a human head covered by the royal nemes headdress. The massive sphinx at Giza came to be worshipped as a form of the sun-god.

Aker: The Twin Lions

Two lions sitting back-to-back, with the sun rising or setting between them, represented the god Aker. He guards the western horizon, the entrance to the netherworld. One lion was named "yesterday" and the other "tomorrow."

Tutu: Master of Demons

Later in Egyptian history, a sphinx-shaped god named Tutu developed a popular following. With knives in his paws and solar rays streaming from his head, Tutu was known as "master of demons." He was a manifestation of the creator-god Amun-Ra who could control demons.

Symbolism

One of the most powerful of all animals and fittingly laden with rich meaning, the lion is synonymous with the Sun and is best personified by Leo, the zodiac sign the great golden star rules. The lion even resembles the Sun, with its tawny coat and shaggy golden mane. It is the totem of kings and emperors, and of Apollo, Mithras, Christ, Krishna, and the Buddha, while its counterpart the eagle is called the Lion of the Skies. Christ is known as the Lion of Judah, Ali, the son-in-law of Mohammed and mediator between the Prophet and the people, was called the Lion of Allah, Krishna is the Lion among Wild Creatures, and the Buddha the Lion of the Shakyas.

Though its shaggy halo of a mane might make the lion seem the ultimate image of male energy, several female deities share its attributes, and the lioness is a ferociously protective mother. Hathor takes the head of a lion when she appears as the Destroyer, while Cybele, the Phrygian Earth Mother, rides in a chariot drawn by lions, the lion and the bee both being sacred to her. In Hindu iconography the lion also embodies feminine power, for Shardula the lioness represents the power of the spoken word, held to be the very essence of Creation.

Because of its great strength and the ferocity with which a she-lion guards her cubs, the lion naturally carries a protective influence, and amulets and charms bearing its image were once very popular. Different gemstones governed different forms of protection, so that a lion carved on garnet shielded the traveler, while a lion on jasper guarded against fevers.

There is a darker side to such power. The collective noun for lions is a pride, and the sin of pride is said to be the negative aspect of Leo, for power can corrupt where there is no awareness of moral and ethical values. The male lion can also stand for laziness, since it is the lioness who does most of the hunting and cub-rearing. Yet for all that it is an effective killer, the lion is not a confrontational creature and generally prefers a peaceful life, and when lions hunt they hunt together, a symbol of fellowship as well as of strength and safety in numbers.

In Japan the lion has its own feast day on January 1, when the Lion Dance is performed by three or more dancers. The first wears a fearsome lion mask and the rest form the body, and the whole creature winds its colorful way through the streets, driving out evil spirits and bringing prosperity for the year ahead.
