Because we picture the heavens as the dwelling of the gods, it is no wonder that the constellations trace out the shapes of those deities along with a host of fabulous celestial animals. The most famous of these patterns is the circle of animals that forms the zodiac. The stars themselves seem deathless, and by setting our own myths and legends among them we lend those stories the same immortality. There are hundreds, perhaps thousands, of such tales, far more than could ever be gathered in one place.

Orion the Hunter

One of the easiest constellations to pick out belongs to Orion, the great and handsome hunter, whose faithful dog follows close behind as the constellation Sirius. Orion was the lover of Eos, goddess of the dawn, and his place in the sky is so prominent that it was once used to mark the turning of the year. His legend reaches back to one of the oldest works of Greek literature, the tales of Hesiod in the eighth century BC. He was the son of the sea god Poseidon and Euryale, the mortal daughter of the king of Crete, a pairing of divine male and human female that recurs throughout the world's mythologies as an image of the union of Heaven and Earth, the joining of opposites. Like his father he could walk upon water. He was also fond of his drink, and after crossing the sea to the island of Chios he grew so drunk that he assaulted Merope, daughter of its king Oenopion. The outraged king blinded him, and Orion groped his way to Lemnos, where the smith god Hephaestus counseled him to travel as far east as he could so that Helios, the sun god, might restore his sight. Healed, the stubborn hunter set out for Chios to take his revenge, but the wise Oenopion had hidden himself away. Orion then went on to Crete, where his skill at the hunt made him a fit companion for Artemis, yet his bloodlust grew so fierce that he swore to kill every beast on Earth. Alarmed at the threat, Gaia the Earth Mother sent a small but deadly creature, the scorpion, to bring him down. Because he was so handsome, the goddesses begged Zeus to set him among the stars, making him perhaps the world's first pin-up, and Zeus placed the scorpion there too, as the constellation Scorpio, a lasting warning of what had become of Orion.

Shooting Stars

A shooting star is no more than a small piece of space debris, anything from a grain of sand to a boulder, whose path is briefly made visible as it burns up on meeting the Earth's atmosphere. Yet the wonder of catching so fleeting an event, and the sense of privilege at having looked at the right patch of sky at the right moment, has gone on inspiring poetry, song, legend, and myth. Tradition holds that whoever sees a shooting star should be quick enough to make a wish upon it, even if the chance of truly catching one and slipping it into a pocket is remote. Our ancestors believed these falling lights carried messages straight from the divine realms, or were the souls of fallen angels and other celestial beings, an idea used beautifully in Neil Gaiman's Stardust, where the star takes the form of a lovely and fiery girl alarmed to find herself stranded on Earth.
