Life Under the Moon

This guide explores the deep, ancient relationship between the moon and all life on Earth. From her role in shaping the planet's tides and seasons to her influence on animals, plants, and human behavior, the moon has been a guiding force since the earliest days of existence. Understanding these connections strengthens your awareness of lunar energy and how it weaves through every layer of your magickal practice.

The Moon and Earth

The moon likely formed at least 4.5 billion years ago when a massive celestial body collided with young Earth. Scientists named the impactor Theia, after the titan mother of Selene, the Greek goddess of the moon. The collision sent enough debris into space to coalesce and eventually form the moon. Analysis of basaltic rock samples has confirmed the shared composition of the Earth and moon, showing that the moon was born in part from the Earth itself.

At the time of her formation, the moon was much closer to us. Even now, she drifts roughly 1.5 inches farther from the Earth each year. Without her presence, life here would be profoundly different. The gravitational exchange between the two bodies has steadied the seasons, lengthened our days, and created the tides that shaped the world as we know it.

The moon takes approximately 27.3 days to complete one orbit around the Earth. However, because the Earth is also moving during that time, the moon must travel a bit farther to realign with the sun, resulting in a full lunar cycle of about 29.5 days from one new moon to the next. Her orbit is elliptical rather than perfectly circular, tracing a slightly elongated path.

Lunar Tides

Although the moon's gravity is far weaker than the Earth's, its effects are significant. As the moon orbits, her gravitational pull displaces ocean water, creating high tides on the sides of the Earth closest to and farthest from the moon. The water drawn toward these points is displaced from elsewhere, producing low tides at the points roughly 90 degrees out from the highs.

When the Earth, sun, and moon align during the new moon and full moon, their combined gravitational forces create especially dramatic tidal shifts known as spring tides. About seven days later, when the moon and sun form a right angle (coinciding with the first and last quarter moons), the sun's gravitational pull partially offsets the moon's, producing gentler changes called neap tides.

Billions of years ago, when the moon was closer and the Earth spun more rapidly, these tides would have been far more extreme. The churning of ancient coastlines stirred essential minerals into the water, and some scientists believe this process played a critical role in the evolution of life on Earth.

Seasons and the Length of Day

The moon's gravitational relationship with the Earth influences more than just the tides. The Earth's axis is tilted at 23.5 degrees, and as different hemispheres point toward or away from the sun throughout the year, we experience the changing seasons. Without the moon, these seasons could become far more extreme, because the Earth's tilt is not perfectly fixed. It has a slight wobble, and the moon's gravity helps stabilize it, keeping the seasons relatively consistent.

Over immense stretches of time, the moon's gravitational pull has also slowed the Earth's rotation, gradually lengthening our days. Fossil evidence shows that the Earth once had much shorter days than the 24-hour cycle we know today.

Sea Creatures and the Lunar Cycle

Marine life offers some of the most visible examples of lunar influence. The tides created by the moon shape the life cycles of countless ocean creatures. Nesting and newborn sea turtles rely on moonlight to navigate shorelines safely at night, when temperatures are cooler and predators are fewer. Oysters and other shellfish open and close in rhythm with the moon's phases and the resulting ocean currents. The moon's light and its pull on the water also guide marine creatures in their patterns of mating, birthing, feeding, and migration.

Animals and the Moon

The moon's influence extends well beyond the ocean. Ecologists studying the behavior of prey animals in relation to the lunar cycle observed that wildebeest adjusted their activity based on moon phases, staying in sheltered, safer areas during the dark moon. During the full moon, they ventured into more open territory where encounters with predators like lions were more likely, because the increased visibility allowed them to spot threats at a distance. Predators with strong night vision, on the other hand, may have an advantage under the dark moon, when shadows conceal their approach.

Birds adjust feeding and migration patterns according to the lunar cycle, and even dung beetles navigate by moonlight as they roll their cargo away from competitors and predators.

Plants and the Moon

Plant life is attuned to the moon as well. A Mediterranean plant called the joint pine produces droplets of fluid that refract moonlight, creating tiny sparkles that attract nocturnal pollinators. Many night-flowering plants depend on moonlight to draw moths and other nighttime visitors, a process essential to their reproduction and survival.

Lunar gardening is a longstanding practice in many traditions. Some practitioners maintain that the waxing-to-full moon increases the movement of sap within plants, while the waning moon slows it down, making certain phases better for planting, pruning, and harvesting.

Moonlight and Human Behavior

Humans are also attuned to the moon, even in an age of artificial lighting and digital screens. Many people who work in hospitals and emergency services report an increase in incidents during the full moon, though formal research remains inconclusive. At a minimum, the moon may influence sleep patterns by affecting levels of melatonin, the hormone that regulates the sleep-wake cycle. The emerging study of human circalunar rhythms, the body's response to the 29.5-day lunar cycle, may eventually reveal much more about how deeply the moon's presence is woven into our biology.

The Moon in Human History

Since the earliest days of human life, the moon, sun, and sky have shaped daily existence. Early humans tracked the moon's passage across the sky to measure time, and folklore about the moon persists to this day, from reading a ring around the moon as a sign of coming rain to planting and harvesting crops by her phases. The lunar cycle's correspondence with the seasons (three to four lunations per season) helped our ancestors anticipate and prepare for each turning of the year.

Early Lunar Timekeeping

The question of when humans first began tracking the moon remains a subject of ongoing research. Cave paintings of animals and the night sky, such as those found in the Lascaux caves in France, demonstrate early creativity and may reflect the moon's power to inspire. These images may also have served ritualistic and spiritual purposes, calling for blessings and luck in hunts, possibly making them some of the earliest recorded lunar rites. Research published in 2023 found that notations of lines, dots, and Y-shaped marks in certain cave paintings corresponded with animal activity and the lunar cycle, suggesting that humans may have been actively timekeeping with the moon as far back as 37,000 to 42,000 years ago.

Beyond timekeeping, the moon would have aided nighttime hunting, foraging, and travel, casting a luminous glow across the landscape. Our ancestors lived by the rhythms of the moon, the sun, and the seasons, and it was only natural for the moon to be personified as a deity.

The Moon as a Deity

With the agricultural revolution and the development of writing, records of specific moon deities emerged, connecting the moon to timekeeping and the fertility of life. To countless ancient cultures, celestial bodies were gods, and the moon was among the most revered.

In ancient Sumeria, the moon god Nanna (also known as Sin) was one of the most prominent deities. Nanna was depicted as an old man or cowherd and was closely associated with the bull and the cow, both ancient fertility symbols. Through his family, Nanna was connected to fertility, abundance, and the realm of the dead.

The phases of the moon held deep significance and guided the timing of rituals and rites. The Egyptian moon deity Khonsu, whose name means "traveler," was a god of healing, fertility, and time. He was often petitioned at the appearance of the waxing crescent for blessings of fertility for both people and animals.

Even as permanent settlements led many cultures toward sun-centered worship, the moon remained vital. Without electricity, moonlight was essential for nighttime travel. Seafaring demanded knowledge of the moon, tides, and stars. Agricultural communities relied on lunar cycles for the fertility of crops, livestock, and people alike.

Suppression and Survival

As Christianity spread and the church's influence grew in Western culture, nature-based deities, including moon gods, were suppressed in favor of a single deity. Leaders discouraged people from accessing the healing power of nature and the moon. Worship of lunar deities went underground, and healers lived on the margins of society, quietly continuing to use the moon for gardening, harvesting, travel, and healing.

The Moon in Religious Traditions Today

The moon continues to guide religious observance around the world. Ramadan spans from crescent moon to crescent moon. Easter falls on the Sunday after the first full moon following the spring equinox. Many Jewish holidays are marked by the full moon. In China, the lunar new year is a cornerstone of cultural life, and the Mid-Autumn Moon Festival honors the lunar goddess Chang'e, who drank an elixir of immortality and ascended to the moon. Harvest moon festivals, both sacred and secular, are still celebrated across many traditions.

The moon landing may have stripped away some of her mystery, but it also deepened our connection to her. India and China have both named spacecraft after their lunar deities. The moon's light continues to inspire, and she is experiencing a revival of reverence as modern witches weave her energy into their practice.

The Moon and Witchcraft Today

The moon has been intertwined with spells and rituals for as long as magick has been practiced. While the forms may have shifted over the centuries, witches today still follow her call.

How the Moon Aids Witches

Modern witches connect with the moon in countless ways:

  • Manifesting desires by casting intentions at the new and full moons
  • Hosting lunar gatherings and coven rituals under the full moon
  • Meditating to align with lunar energy
  • Charging water under the moon for use in spellcrafting
  • Letting the moon's phases guide when to cast, heal, and reflect
  • Charging crystals and conducting healing rituals by the light of the full moon
  • Planting, nurturing, and harvesting healing herbs according to the moon's phases
  • Using the 29.5-day lunar cycle from new moon to new moon to build habits, set monthly goals, and work toward long-term intentions
  • Drawing on the moon's phase, sign, and seasonality to guide reflection and focus

At its core, working with the moon allows witches to access a deeper layer of magickal power and healing. The moon is both part of us and a source of energy for spellwork. Some practitioners are so closely attuned to her that their own energy and psychic sensitivity rise and fall with her cycle.

Embracing Dark and Light

The moon's cycle reflects the polarity of dark and light that is central to witchcraft: the unknown and the known, the spiritual realm and waking reality, the subconscious and the conscious. Like the dark moon goddess Hekate, who holds the keys to secrets and mysteries, moon witches are unafraid to explore the shadows and find power in the dark. There is a cycle and a season for everything under the moon. Her phases offer a time for rest and retreat, a time for growth and change, and a time to stand fully in your power.

The Moon in Western Astrology

In Western astrology, the moon is considered feminine and represents the soul, the subconscious, early childhood, and patterns of nurturing. Her cycles often draw what is hidden in the subconscious into conscious awareness. In modern times, the moon has taken on a particularly significant role in the reawakening of goddess energy, calling practitioners to honor the power of the feminine.

An Enduring Symbol

The moon is our relative, born of Earth and stardust, just as we are. She has watched over our ancestors and will continue to watch over future generations, a steady light in the sky connecting everyone to the past, present, and future. Just as harvest moon gratitude rituals have been observed for generations, the moon can serve as a bridge to ancestors and spirits beyond the veil. Through her patient, nurturing presence, all things can come to manifest.
