Stars

The word comet comes from the Greek kometes, meaning hair of the head. Aristotle is thought to have been the first to describe these stars with hair, whose hazy haloes and streaming tails do indeed resemble tresses of light. The same comets return to our skies at regular intervals. A depiction of Halley's Comet appears on the Bayeux Tapestry of 1066, though that is a recent record beside the Babylonian tablets that noted its passage as far back as 164 BC. Halley's Comet is visible roughly every seventy-five to seventy-six years; it last graced the night sky in 1986 and is due to return in 2061.

As befits so potent a symbol, the arrival of a comet has long been believed to coincide with momentous events touching the whole Earth. The tsunami of December 2004, for instance, was linked by some to a comet called Machholz 2, though as ever with warnings of this kind, the interpretation tends to be possible only after the event.

Bright stars, comets, and other unusual cosmic phenomena were said to herald the births not only of Christ but of other holy figures, among them the Buddha and the Hindu god Agni, whose mother, like Christ's, was paradoxically a virgin. Given how consistently Halley's Comet was recorded, pinpointing such a notable celestial event ought to be straightforward. Yet for all the labor of astronomers and astrologers, no celestial event has been found to coincide with the birth of Christ at Bethlehem, leaving open the question of whether the famous Star was ever a literal object in the sky at all.
