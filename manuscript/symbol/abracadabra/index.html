Overview

Abracadabra is perhaps the most famous magical word of all. Traditionally it was written as a diminishing triangle, the full word along the top and one letter dropped from each line below until only a single A remained. As the word shrank away to nothing, so too would the illness or malevolent energy it was meant to banish.

Origins

The word may descend from the Aramaic "Avra Kedabra," meaning "I create as I speak," or words to that effect. Several other theories circulate as well:

  • A derivation from the name of the deity Abraxas.
  • The Hebrew phrase "Abreg Ad Habra," meaning "Hurl your thunderbolt unto death," which would suit its use as a charm to ward off illness.
  • The Aramaic "Abhadda Kedabhra," meaning "Disappear as this word," echoing the way the charm itself dwindles and vanishes.
  • The initials of the Hebrew words for Father (Ab), Son (Ben), and Holy Spirit (Ruach Acadsch).

Its lasting power may rest in the fact that all of these readings ring true, lending the word a universal appeal.

A Lingering Charm

Most accounts say the charm was in regular use until the Middle Ages, yet a curious trace survives in a small thirteenth-century church in a remote Welsh valley. St. Michael and All Angels Church at Cascob, on the edge of the Radnor Forest, keeps an Abracadabra charm engraved on a wall tablet. In the seventeenth century a local girl named Elizabeth Lloyd was said to be possessed by evil spirits, and the symbol was used to drive them out, together with the astrological signs carved beneath it. The tablet may even have been the work of the alchemist and astrologer John Dee, who served Queen Elizabeth I and lived nearby.
