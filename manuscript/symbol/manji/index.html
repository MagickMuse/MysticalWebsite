Manji

Notes/Warnings
Also known as the swastika. A sacred Buddhist and Hindu solar symbol of well-being, ancient and near-universal in its reach, distinct from its later twentieth-century political misuse by the Nazi regime.

Manji is a Japanese name often glossed as the "Chinese symbol for eternity." It is in essence the swastika, an ancient solar emblem, and within Buddhism it stands for the balance of opposing forces, harmony, and dharma, the practice of right conduct.

The Manji may face either way, and unlike the superstitions that surround the swastika, both orientations carry positive meaning. Turning clockwise it signifies love and mercy; turning counterclockwise it signifies intelligence and strength.

Few solar signs are older than the swastika, which traces back to the Neolithic and won reverence across a remarkable spread of cultures. It ranks among the rare symbols whose meaning and presence are very nearly universal, turning up from India and northern Europe to Central America and East Asia, and appearing as a sacred form in Navaho sand painting. Its name comes from the Sanskrit Swasti, rendered as well-being or so be it. By one suggestion the shape was first stumbled upon in basket weaving, where it forms of its own accord as the work proceeds.

With its four bent arms the sign suggests a wheel, and like a wheel it can spin in either sense. Arms swept clockwise, deosil, read as the Sun, the masculine, the overt; arms swept counterclockwise, widdershins, answer to the Moon, the feminine, the covert, in which form it is sometimes named the sauvastika. Religious practice, however, generally attaches no such split to the direction, and the notion that a left-facing swastika is wicked in itself does not hold. The Hopi, for their part, give the clockwise turn to the Earth and the reverse to the Sun.

Those four hinged arms deepen the weight of the number four: the solid stuff of matter, the ordered cosmos, the world's four corners, the four winds, the cardinal points, the seasons, and the elements. At the unmoving center sits the quintessence, that fifth element, which marks the figure as an image of the Axis Mundi. Freemasonry sees in that central hub a likeness of the Pole Star.

The wheel ranks high among the symbols of the Dharmic faiths, and so does the swastika. Across Hindu temples and households it is often drawn with a bindhu, a dot placed in the field between the arms, and it is numbered among the 108 sacred emblems of Lord Vishnu.
