Symbol Grimoire

The Secret Language of Letters and Signs

The most versatile secret symbols we will ever meet are the letters of the alphabet. Even as your eyes move across a page, you are instantly translating these curious squiggles, marks that between them hold all the secrets of the Universe. The symbols bear no real relationship to sound, yet we still "hear" the sounds they stand for and grasp their complex ideas thousands of times a minute. What follows looks at how signs, sounds, and ideas converge, and at how other signs have been used to keep information hidden.

Sound

Everything in the Universe has its own frequency, its own vibration. The human ear cannot catch some of these frequencies, but they exist all the same. The dog whistle is a simple example, heard by dogs yet seldom by their owners. Some frequencies are powerful enough to destroy physical objects, as when a soprano's note is so pure that it shatters glass. In the biblical account, the walls of Jericho fell not to physical force but to the power of sound. Certain very low frequencies can break matter apart by scrambling its molecules, and sound can even trigger avalanches. In one memorable film scene, travelers searching for a lost city are halted at an impassable ravine, until their helpless laughter sets off an avalanche that bridges the gap and lets them continue.

Every place has its own frequency, as does the Earth itself. The natural harmonic of our planet is said to be F sharp, a note recognized by ancient peoples including the Egyptians, whose Great Pyramid is said to "play" it, and the Chinese, who called it Hu. Native Americans, by the same account, tune their flutes to this note.

Does this mean our most secret and sacred symbols are inspired by something that can be heard but never seen? Sounds can only be set down by us as written squiggles, and like the wind we gauge them only by their effect on something else. Arguably the most important symbol in the world is one that can be neither touched nor seen.

This hidden power has long been tied to sacred architecture. At the Romanesque-Byzantine abbey at Souillac, built by Eleanor of Aquitaine, a carved capital on the eighth pillar shows doves dipping their beaks into an owl's ear. The owl is Athena's, a sign of access to knowledge, and the carving is said to mark a spot where the building's acoustics produce a startling second harmonic, audible only from that exact place, opening what the ancients called the "third ear." Ancient megalithic structures such as Newgrange in Ireland and Wayland's Smithy in England seem to have been raised to exploit such effects. Mogul palaces in India were designed so that guards could clap at set points, the loud echoes serving as an intruder alarm. The Whispering Gallery at St. Paul's Cathedral in London is built so that a whisper can be heard clearly on the far side, directly opposite the speaker.

Words

Many faiths agree on at least one point: that the primal resonance of a word, or sound, gave birth to the Universe, and that the source of this first sound was divine. The Bible puts it this way:

In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God. (John 1:1)

The Quran speaks of it too:

Words of Truth that have the power to express the Truth are like flourishing trees whose roots, or direct meanings, are established deep in the earth of the heart, and whose branches, or subtle meanings, reach high into the sky of mystical knowledge.

So great is the power of a word that it is held to be the very seed of creation. The old line "sticks and stones may break my bones, but words can never hurt me" takes no account of the potency of a curse; whether that power rests in superstition or not, its consequences are real enough. In the same way, a blessing carries wishes that are a strong force for good.

For the Greeks, logos, meaning "word," was not only the spoken word or phrase but was bound up with the intellect and with ideas themselves. The gift of eloquence belonged to the gods, or lent godlike standing to those who mastered it; a leader without the art of oratory will never truly hold power. Among the Druids, whose oral tradition outlasted the coming of writing, there were charms and rituals said to confer eloquence, such as placing a cinquefoil leaf under the tongue. Learning by repetition was thought the purest way to take in knowledge, since passing words from one mouth to the next kept their intention undiluted by the "middle man" of writing.

Letters and Writing

The journey from primal sound to deliberate word to written symbol is a mysterious one. Just as children learn to draw before they can write, early humanity's first "writing" was symbolic, a pictorial way of setting down ideas. The history of writing is well documented elsewhere, but the sacred weight of the alphabet shows in how widely cultures credit its invention to the divine: Thoth for the Ancient Egyptians, Hermes or Mercury in the Greco-Roman world, Odin in Norse myth, and Ogma in the Celtic pantheon. In Hinduism, Sarasvati is not only the Goddess of Speech but is also called Lipidevi, the Goddess of the Alphabet. Letters, along with numbers, are the most potent of all secret signs and sacred symbols, their meanings so ingrained in us that we take them for granted.

The letters of any alphabet carry great power; they are not merely letters but calendars, calculators, symbols, and ideas of divinity. It is worth remembering that, not so long ago, reading and writing were arts reserved for the powerful and priestly castes, for with them came the power of knowledge, even the power to transcribe the very Word of God. The first printed texts were religious. Once ordinary people could read and write, and so choose for themselves which ideas to favor, the grip of both secular and religious authorities was much loosened.

The Hebrew language is said to hold secrets known only to God, secrets dangerous enough to work miracles, even the raising of the dead. The Torah likens the vowels of the alphabet to the living soul of a person and the consonants to the world of matter. Fittingly, the vowel signs are rounded in shape while the consonants are angular: the vowels are seen as spiritual, perhaps because they are formed freely, without the tongue or teeth needing to meet, while the constructed consonants stand for the material world. It is also said that God gave Adam 32 letters, of which some 10 were lost, leaving the 22-letter alphabet we have today.

For all its power, writing in some ways "kills" ideas by fixing them in permanent form. Ideas change, and revolutionary theories harden into everyday fact. As with every sign and symbol gathered here, it is worth keeping that in mind when exploring the hidden meanings of the alphabets themselves.
