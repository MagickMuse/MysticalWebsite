Smaragdina Tablet

◇Notes/Warnings
Also known as the Emerald Tablet or the Secret of Hermes. A cornerstone of alchemy, said to hold the teaching of Hermes Trismegistus and the instructions for making gold, and likely carved from green jasper. Its central maxim is "as above, so below."

The Smaragdina Tablet stands at the foundation of alchemy, a short and ancient text held to preserve the teachings of Hermes Trismegistus, reckoned the father of the whole art. Story has it that Alexander the Great came upon the tablet inside the tomb of Hermes, said to rest somewhere near Hebron, with the formula for producing gold cut into its face in Phoenician letters; the oldest surviving renderings of that recipe come down to us in Arabic.

The Emerald

There is a glamour to the name, yet in those days the word emerald was applied to almost any green stone, and the slab was very likely worked from green jasper. The substance hardly mattered to the readers it drew, a roster that includes Roger Bacon, Albertus Magnus, Aleister Crowley, and C. G. Jung.

As Above, So Below

Alchemists wrapped their secrets in emblem, pun, and layered meaning, and the tablet's lines play the same game. The reading found among the manuscripts of Isaac Newton, who pursued the art himself, lays out the doctrine: that the lower reflects the higher and the higher the lower, so as to accomplish the wonders of a single thing; that all things issued from one by adaptation; that its father is the Sun and its mother the Moon, that the wind bore it in its belly and the earth gave it nurse. The adept is told to part the fine from the coarse gently and with great care, the work climbing from earth to sky and sinking back to draw down the powers of both. Its strength outdoes every strength, mastering all that is subtle and piercing all that is solid; in this way the world was fashioned. From it flows the three-part wisdom that names Hermes the thrice-great, Trismegistus, his solar work brought to its end.
