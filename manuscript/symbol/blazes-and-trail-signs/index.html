🧭Blazes and Trail Signs

Overview

Blazes are signs that mark trails or tracks. Some are overt and easily read, while others are hidden in the landscape so that only the initiate can follow them. The open kind is visually striking and crosses boundaries of nationality and culture, the sort of marker found at tourist sites or painted at eye level on a forest tree, made to withstand the weather. There is nothing secret about everyday signage of this kind.

Covert Signs

The hidden kind is another matter. Often used by hunters, these signs may echo the methods of Native Americans. One of the simplest is a small nick of bark cut from a tree to mark the way; the size of such axe blazes varies with the skill of the maker, the aim being to keep them as small as possible while still noticeable to anyone who knows what to look for.

Where there are no trees, the natural surroundings are used instead. A favorite device is to snap a twig on a bush and leave the broken part dangling by a thin thread of bark to mark the trail, the torn end pointing the way to go. Across open grassland a clump of grass might be twisted into a knot, and more than one knot can carry added meaning. Stones, too, may be stacked or set beside one another in a particular arrangement.

Patrin

There have always been times when people needed to cover their tracks or conceal their route. Romany and other nomadic travelers each developed their own systems of signs to share news and directions across distances. The Romany markers laid along a chosen route are called patrin, or patteran, from a Romany word meaning leaf. A patrin might be a drawn sign or one built discreetly from natural objects: a bundle of twigs, a feather, or a snapped twig.

Hobo Signs

During times of severe economic depression, people have often been forced to leave home and take to the roads in search of work. Conditions were desperate during the Depression in the United States in the early twentieth century. Many saw these destitute people as vagrants, yet most hobos were eager for any work they could find; a hobo set himself apart from a tramp, who was thought unwilling to work. Out of poverty, or perhaps because of it, a strong bond grew among fellow travelers, and from that bond came a set of symbols that those following after could read, passing along hints and warnings earned by hard experience.

The signs are hard to date precisely, but they seem to have begun in the 1880s and faded by the Second World War. They were drawn on fence posts, along the railroad, and on paths, anywhere a watchful traveler would notice them, usually in chalk or coal or whatever else came to hand. A single mark might tell the initiated whether to stop or move on, whether a house would offer food or a kind word, whether a doctor lived nearby, or whether the authorities were quick to act and a town best avoided.
