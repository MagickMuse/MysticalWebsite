Overview

Although "sigil" can mean the signet or seal of a fraternity or order, as a magickal symbol it is something subtler. A sigil belongs in particular to the Western tradition of magick. The word comes from the Latin sigillum, "seal," and a sigil is a personalized glyph or emblem charged with a specific meaning or intent. The Monad of John Dee, for instance, is a sigil.

A Hebrew word, segulah, may or may not be related, but it describes a sigil well: it means "a word, action, or item of spiritual effect," close in sense to "talisman." Sigils usually appear in drawn or written form, though they can also be rendered in sound or as a solid three-dimensional object.

A sigil may look simple or elaborate, but either way its design carries a complex web of meanings. Many are built from several different elements: the Norse binding rune is one kind of sigil, and astrological signs and their planets each have their own. Medieval magicians used sigils to call up angels, demons, and other spirits, each entity having its own sigil that captured its "essence," a kind of spiritual signature. Such marks were powerful tools in the right hands, or the wrong ones. Pages upon pages of these signatures fill the old grimoires; one famous seventeenth-century work offers an at-a-glance guide to the personal sigils of the seventy-two demons of Hell, in effect a directory for summoning entities from another realm. Command of a sigil, together with proper ritual attention, was held to give the conjurer power over the being called upon. Self-professed adepts such as Aleister Crowley and Austin Osman Spare famously used sigils, and the practice is far from dead: modern practitioners employ them still.

Sigil Magic

The aim is to cast a spell that draws the object of your desire toward you, a kind of tool for "cosmic ordering," with the sigil as its focus.

  1. Define the desire precisely. Precision is vital. It is not enough to ask simply for money, for instance: what if the fortune arrived through the death of a loved one?
  2. Write a sentence that clearly expresses the outcome you want.
  3. Strike out repeated letters so that no letter appears more than once.
  4. Reduce the remaining letters into shapes. It does not matter if some letters end up reversed, and upper or lower case is irrelevant. A single letter P, for example, already contains within it an I, a lowercase l, a C, an F, and, flipped over, a lowercase d.
  5. Arrange the letters into a design that pleases you. Better still if it can be drawn in one continuous stroke and enclosed in a circle.
  6. Inscribe the sigil on paper or parchment, with as much ceremony as you care to bring to it.
  7. Charge the sigil. Use it as a focus for meditation, then burn it or otherwise put it entirely out of mind.

As an illustration, a practitioner might begin with a desire such as "I bring understanding and peace into the world," strike out the repeated letters, and fold what remains into a single interlocking glyph that can be drawn in one movement and wrapped in a circle.
