Symbol Grimoire

💧Watermark

Type
Emblem & Device

Overview

Some terms are so familiar that we rarely pause over their origins. "Foolscap" paper is one: this once-common size, known more plainly today as A4, took its name from the fool's cap watermark that used to be set into it, a usage traceable to 1479.

There are few better ways of hiding a symbol than the watermark, and the technique was probably a happy accident. Handmade paper has changed little over the centuries: a thin layer of wet pulp is spread over a sieve and left to dry, then peeled from the mesh. If a piece of wire is laid on the sieve before the pulp is spread, the image of the wire appears when the paper is held to the light, because the sheet is very slightly thinner at that point. The method is thought to have been discovered by the papermakers of Fabriano and Bologna in late thirteenth-century Italy.

Papermakers soon saw that a mark could protect their paper from theft, vouch for its quality, and identify its maker, and that it might also carry a secret message. Henry VIII of England, for instance, commissioned a watermark for his own paper showing a hog wearing a miter, a gibe at the Pope.

Mystical uses are almost impossible to prove, yet some marks seem to speak for themselves. Paper bearing Rosicrucian symbols was used in privately published works that displayed such sympathies, including a watermark with the initials C.R., for the legendary founder of the Order, Christian Rosenkreutz. Papermaking reached Europe from the East by way of the Knights Templar and other returning crusaders, though the watermark itself was a European accident. The Cathars, a sect condemned as heretical for their dualist beliefs, were persecuted until they fled their homeland in France and scattered across Europe, making a living however they could, papermaking among their trades. They are said to have recognized one another and tracked their supporters through a watermark on their "Lombardy Paper," marks whose meaning is hard to read but whose mystical look lends some weight to the idea.
