Aesclepius Wand

A rod, staff, or wand generally surmounted with wings. Two serpents entwine about the staff, forming a figure-of-eight shape. The key elements of its construction are the serpent, the spiral, the infinity sign, the circle, wings, and the wand.

The Caduceus is an incredibly ancient symbol, and its earliest recorded appearance is on the goblet of the King of Lagash, dating back some 2,600 years BC.

Layers of Meaning

The Caduceus is the emblem of Mercury/Hermes and is incredibly rich in meaning:

  • The staff or wand is a symbol of power and authority, of magickal and supernatural forces, and is the tool of all magicians, medicine men, and shamans. It also represents the Tree of Life or World Axis.
  • The wings on top signify flight (both physical and metaphorical), intuition, the spiritual, and communication from the Heavens or the Gods. Mercury is the Messenger of the Gods.
  • The two serpents, twining in opposite directions, represent opposition and equilibrium. They signify opposites: male and female, day and night, good and evil, and so represent balance. Serpents also remind us of hidden knowledge.
  • As the serpents scroll around the wand, they form the figure-of-eight shape, or infinity symbol, which stands for completeness and perfection.
  • Part of the infinity symbol is the circle, ultimately representing the cosmos, the spirit, unity.

All these elements combined make for a powerful symbol that has altered very little over the millennia. Together, they add up to supernatural power and hidden wisdom, messages from the spiritual realms, authority, the cosmos and infinity, and the pairing of opposites in harmony and unity.

Healing and Medicine

Perhaps the most common use of the Caduceus, both today and since its earliest appearance, is as a symbol of healing and medicine. Aesclepius, the first physician and the God of medicine, had the Caduceus as one of his attributes because he had the power and the intuition (the wand and the wings) to be able to use potentially poisonous or corruptive substances (the serpents) to restore health and, reputedly, to bring the dead back to life.

The Caduceus was not only the instrument of Aesclepius but of the healing God Ningishzida of Mesopotamia (whose symbol is intertwining snakes), and of the Egyptian deities Isis and Ishtar. It is also found in India where it carries the same meaning.
