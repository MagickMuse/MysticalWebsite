Overview

Heraldry works as a kind of historical shorthand, encoding the qualities of the families to whom the crests belong. Coats of arms, still in use today, began with the need to identify opposing armies and individual combatants in the era of hand to hand combat, almost a thousand years ago. Even earlier, soldiers painted personally meaningful images on their shields as marks of both significance and identity. The blazons, escutcheons, badges, mottoes, and crests can look like an impenetrable forest of symbols, but the code is readable. What follows is a general guide to some of the most common emblems rather than an exhaustive analysis.

Shape: Shield versus Lozenge

Men's heraldic designs appear on a shield, while women's appear on a lozenge, a diamond balanced on one point. The lozenge, suggestive of the vesica piscis, is a feminine symbol. Members of the noncombative clergy also use the lozenge or an oval.

Color (Tinctures)

Heraldic colors are called tinctures, and their names keep their archaic, mainly French roots:

  • Gold = Or
  • Silver = Argent
  • Red = Gules
  • Blue = Azure
  • Purple = Purpure
  • Green = Vert
  • Black = Sable

There are also patterned fields called Furs. The most common, ermine, resembles the fur of the ermine stoat; the other, vair, comes from a gray-blue squirrel. To stay clear and visible, a color is rarely laid over another color, and the same rule applies to the metals.

Divisions

The shield or lozenge can be split in many ways:

  • Party per fess: divided horizontally
  • Party per pale: divided vertically
  • Party per bend: divided diagonally from left to right
  • Party per sinister: divided diagonally the opposite way

The field can also be split by a saltire or a regular cross, by a chevron, or into three with a Y shape. Lines may also be wavy or curved.

Charges

A charge is essentially a picture set on the field, and can be any object, symbol, animal, or plant. Exotic creatures play a large role, with unicorns and dragons standing beside boars, lions, and eagles. Each creature carries its own symbolism, though a charge may also hold a specific meaning within a particular family's history. The Fleur de Lys, for instance, stands for the French ruling classes.

Crest

The crest sits on top of the emblem, crowning it, usually above the shield. It echoes the plume of feathers knights once wore on their helmets for distinction and recognition. Since women had no occasion to wear a helmet, the lozenge generally carries no crest.

Mottoes

A motto is a phrase describing the bearer, a kind of historic mission statement. The family name is sometimes worked into it as a pun. Mottoes may be in any language, though Latin and French are the most common.

Supporters

The shield or lozenge is sometimes held up by supporters, usually animals standing upright to hold the device. These creatures also bear a relevance to the owner.

Other Heraldic Devices

Many devices are concise shorthand for the owner's qualities, with the lion signifying valor and the fox a wily intelligence. Others carry fixed meanings:

  • Mullet: not a fish but a star, denoting the third son
  • Bezant: a gold coin, meaning the owner can be entrusted with treasure
  • Escutcheon: a small shield showing a claim to or descent from royalty
  • Talbot: a hunting hound
  • Martlet: a small footless bird, the mark of the fourth son, who must rely on his own resources rather than an inheritance
  • Stirrup: signifies action
