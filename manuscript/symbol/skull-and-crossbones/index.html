Skull and Crossbones

◇Notes/Warnings
A grinning skull above two diagonally crossed bones, best known as the Jolly Roger, the universal emblem of the pirate, and the worldwide sign for poison. To the Templars and Freemasons it carried an inner meaning of death turned toward hope and rebirth.

A grinning skull sits above two bones crossed on the diagonal. At first glance it can seem one of those everyday signs taken for granted, yet a longer look reveals something far stranger. It is known above all as the Jolly Roger, the wickedly grinning skull and crossed bones that serves as the universal emblem of the pirate.

A Symbol of Death

The origins of the skull and crossbones are wrapped in mystery. It is very old, set over the entrances of Spanish cemeteries and graveyards for centuries, and as a sign of death it has no rival; it has become the universally recognized mark for poison.

Templars and Freemasons

The Knights Templar and the Freemasons may hold the key to its deeper meaning. The Jolly Roger is said to have begun as the Jolie Rouge, the name the French branch of the Templars gave to the flag flown by their warrior ships, later taken up by pirates. In Freemasonry the sign is repeated six times on the Tracing Board spread across the floor of the Temple before any ritual. There it stands for both death and life: the crossed bones form a saltire, a diagonal cross that signifies evolutionary change, so the skull of death takes on a second aspect of hope, progress, reincarnation, and life beyond death. Fittingly, the Templars are said to have buried their dead with the leg bones removed and laid in the shape of a cross.
