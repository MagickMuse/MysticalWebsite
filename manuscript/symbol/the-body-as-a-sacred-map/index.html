The Body as a Sacred Map

In trying to grasp the deeper mysteries of the Universe, our ancestors understandably began with the most accessible and easily defined thing they knew: themselves. The human body, with its head in the stars and its feet on the ground, is the most universal of all sacred symbols. Ancient peoples believed that the body held every secret of the Universe within it, both inside and out.

The opening book of the Bible offers a short statement that captures an idea which has fascinated humankind for thousands of years:

So God created man in His own image. (Genesis 1:27)

Philosophers, theologians, poets, artists, and mystics have all tried to explain this idea in their own ways. In the thirteenth century, the philosopher Albertus Magnus held that humankind was not only the image of God, the imago dei, but also the image of the Universe, the imago mundi. The Theosophist Helena Blavatsky echoed this in the early twentieth century when she described a person as a little world, a microcosm set inside a great Universe.

In what may be the most profound act of mutual adoration ever witnessed, humans in turn created their gods in their own image. Ancient peoples believed that deities ruled over each part of the body, so that every part was charged with divine qualities and its own mythology. We see this in the All Seeing Eye of God, the Eye of Horus, the feet of the Buddha or Vishnu, and the phallus of Priapus. Pythagoras taught that to disfigure the body in any way was a sacrilege against the dwelling place of the Gods.

For the Pythagoreans, the magick resonating within numbers also helped define the nature of humankind. Five is widely accepted as the number of the human being, because it counts the body's most important extremities: the head, the two hands, and the two feet. The feet correspond to the elements of earth and water, the hands to air and fire, and the head to ether, the fifth element that governs all the others.

Our modern ideas about the body are not so different from these older beliefs. Like Pythagoras, we sanctify the body by calling it a temple, and we grant powers to certain parts that have nothing to do with their mechanical roles. These associations persist despite all our advances in biology and physiology. The heart is still the symbolic seat of the emotions, and of love above all. To be "gutsy" is to be brave and forthright. To vent one's spleen is to air an irritation. To say that the head rules the heart is to say that intellect prevails over emotion.

The practical ways we use our bodies have shaped how we make sense of the wider Universe. Our ten fingers govern how we count and give their name to the base-10, or digital, way of measuring. Because a warrior typically held a weapon in the right hand and defended with a shield on the left, the right side of the body became associated with attack and the left with defense. From this, the right side came to be seen as the masculine side, linked to the Sun, fire, and light, while the left belongs to the feminine, the Moon, water, and darkness.

The physical and spiritual aspects of a person are bound up with the greater Universe as well. The body houses the spirit for as long as it survives. In another sense, the body is the clothing of the spirit, and each part of it reflects the soul, the breath of life preserved within.

The Greeks pictured the Earth itself as a giant human figure curled into a ball, with Delphi as the Omphalos, the navel of the world. Early philosophers held that all things represented the body, if not in form then in essence. The natural world is full of these analogies: mountains become the nourishing breasts of the Mother Goddess, caves her womb, and certain trees and rocks become phallic symbols. In effect, the body is a measuring stick for the Universe.

Centuries after the Greeks, Leonardo da Vinci expressed the same idea in his famous drawing of the Vitruvian Man. Vitruvius was an influential Roman architect of the first century BC, and the idealized building proportions he set down inspired Leonardo, who worked from the belief that mathematics provided the foundation for everything. The sketch shows a figure set inside both a circle and a square, with the navel at the exact center. These same proportions appear in architecture, art, music, and harmony, and we seem to recognize this "divine proportion" instinctively wherever it is applied. The drawing is regarded as the ultimate rendering of the body, its measurements a symbol of divine intention.

The Kabbalistic Tree of Life is often laid over a human figure, its proportions aligning with the parts of the body that represent the esoteric principles of that arcane system. The idea of the body as a blueprint for the Universe appears in astrology as well, where each sign of the Zodiac rules a different body part. The first sign, Aries, governs the head, and the authority of the signs flows downward through the body to the last, Pisces, which rules the feet.
