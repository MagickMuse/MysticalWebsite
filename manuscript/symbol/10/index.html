Symbolism of Ten

The first number that requires more than one digit, ten is the foundation stone of the digital age, and despite any high-tech connotations it is the number of choice simply because of the number of fingers and thumbs on the hands. That same fact gave rise to the decimal counting system, and ten is an infinitely malleable and versatile number.

Pythagoras believed ten to be the most sacred and greatest of all numbers, and the Tetraktys, a shorthand version of the name of God, was devised from it based on the premise that one plus two plus three plus four equals ten. The Decad was a holy number, the first to need a second part, and the Pythagoreans took their oaths upon it. Pythagoras also described it as the nature of number and said that it symbolized the world and heaven, since heaven has to encompass the world. The Ancient Greek philosophers decided that there were ten orders of the Heavenly Bodies.

Ten is a satisfying number, and ten of anything seems like a complete set, as with the Ten Commandments. In the Kabbalah there are ten Sephiroth, emanations from the mind of God that represent the Universe and its workings, and here ten symbolizes the unity of Creation and the synthesis of all things. In Hinduism the tenth state, Avastha, is the point at which self-consciousness, one of the three imperfections, is shed and unity with the Divine is attained, and there are ten rules of good conduct, the Yamas, just as there are Ten Commandments in the Bible.

The Mayans regarded ten as unfortunate, since it belonged to the God of Death, Thoh. In Africa, by contrast, ten is a lucky number and a symbol of fertility, since it is made up of six and four, both fortunate numbers in themselves. In the Tarot, ten is the number of the Wheel of Fortune.
