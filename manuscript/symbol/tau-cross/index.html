Tau Cross

Instantly recognizable as a capital letter T, the Tau cross forms the basis of another famous symbol, the Ankh. Its name comes from the Greek letter Tau, which is also the final letter of the Hebrew alphabet, and it is a very ancient sign.

The Tau served as the emblem of three major deities: the Sumerian Sun god Tammuz, the Roman god Mithras, and the Greek god Attis. Each of them died and was resurrected, drawing an analogy with the Sun that dies every night and rises again every morning. Followers of Tammuz daubed the T shape in ashes onto their foreheads. The symbol stands for resurrection, reincarnation, life, blood sacrifice, symbolic death, and a gateway.

The Tau is also known as the Robbers Cross, since the thieves crucified at either side of Christ are believed to have been hanged on crosses of this shape. Although it is commonly held that Christ went to his death on a cross whose upright extended beyond the crossbar (the Latin Cross), it is likely that a Tau cross was actually used.

St. Francis was especially fond of the Tau and used it almost as a personal signature. The Egyptian hermit St. Antony is said to have put its power to good use when he frightened away a horde of demons. Very few Tau crosses remain in the world, but an intact one dating back to the sixth century still stands on Tory Island in Eire, a clue that early Egyptian Coptic Christians may have landed there.
