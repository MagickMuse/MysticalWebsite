Overview

Pythagoras was a Greek polymath born between 590 and 580 BC, remembered as the Father of Philosophy, a phrase that means one who seeks knowledge, and as the Father of Numerology, though the study of numbers reaches back long before him. The Oracle at Delphi is said to have told his parents that he would make an extraordinary difference in the world, and some who knew him believed he was a divine being in human form.

Life and Travels

He journeyed widely and was initiated into the mystery traditions of his age, among them the Eleusinian, Chaldean, Egyptian, and Babylonian schools. He was familiar with the teachings of the Kabbalah and traveled to India, where he studied Hindu astrology, mathematics, and numerology. He later founded a school at Cremona and gathered students around him. Accounts of his death vary, and because he was outspoken and controversial, his end may have been a violent one.

Teachings

Pythagoras taught that a knowledge of music, geometry, and astrology was essential to understanding the nature of God, humankind, and the natural world, all of which he saw as deeply interconnected. He regarded the planets as living, conscious beings that carried the souls of great divinities, and he taught the transmigration of the soul, the passage of the spirit into a new body after death.

The Platonic Solids

He introduced the five forms later known as the Platonic Solids, which he understood as the physical shapes of sacred numbers.

Numbers as Living Things

At the heart of his work was the belief that numbers possess souls. In Pythagorean thought, each number holds its own character, personality, and magickal power, and the secrets locked within numbers are without limit.
