Sacred Geometry in Sacred Places

Sacred geometry is the study of proportion and form as an expression of divine order. The word geometry comes from the Greek for "measurement of the Earth." People have always needed ways to define and order space, and geometry answers that need. Geometry becomes sacred when its proportions are shaped to please the gods, when a structure is built with the conscious aim of resonating in harmony with the natural, divinely created world. The same reasoning that frames the solid material plane also lets us measure the movement of the stars and planets, and so understand our own place in the cosmos.

Sacred geometry was never the property of a single culture. The intricate patterns of Islamic architecture grew from the belief that certain measurements belong to a divine plan, and Gothic and Renaissance builders followed the same principles. Its influence runs from the Pyramids to modern landmarks both secular and sacred. Contemporary engineering lets fluid, organic shapes enter large structures, as in Gaudí's Sagrada Família in Barcelona, Jørn Utzon's Sydney Opera House, and Norman Foster's 30 St. Mary Axe in London, popularly called the Gherkin.

What sets these proportions apart from ordinary measurement is harmony. Spatial proportion is only one part of the picture. The same principles underpin the harmonics and frequencies of music and color as well as the patterns of natural forms. Numbers, like letters, were treated as sacred symbols in their own right, and our ancestors saw the two as deeply connected. Phi is one such symbol, known also as the golden mean, the golden section, the golden ratio, and the divine proportion.

Golden Section

Of all the sacred signs, the golden section may be the most surprising, since it surfaces throughout the natural world in the most unexpected places. Clench your hand, tuck the index finger into the base of the thumb, and wrap the thumb in tight, and you trace its form. The spirals of shells and ammonite fossils, the seed heads of sunflowers, and the stylized unfurling fern fronds of Māori art all obey its rules. The Fibonacci sequence (see the Numbers section) serves as a kind of instruction manual for building this never-ending spiral.

Technically, the golden section is any point that divides a line so that the smaller part relates to the larger part exactly as the larger part relates to the whole. This divine proportion runs through every line of the Pentagram, which helps explain why the five-pointed star feels so satisfying and intriguing a symbol.

Platonic Solids

Though named for the Greek philosopher and mathematician Plato, these shapes were known long before him, and the first three were identified by Pythagoras. Plato set out his account around 360 BC. Because each solid corresponds to an element, the group was said to encompass everything within the known universe.

The Platonic solids are the only five regular polyhedrons. Each is a multi-sided, three-dimensional figure whose corners all touch the surface of a single orb. Irregular polyhedrons number in the millions, but only five regular ones exist, and each is matched to an element:

  • Earth: the cube, or hexahedron, a fitting emblem for the solidity of the planet.
  • Fire: the tetrahedron, its pyramid form suitably flame-like.
  • Air: the octahedron.
  • Water: the icosahedron.
  • Ether, or aether: the dodecahedron. Aristotle named the fifth element, though he did not tie it to this solid, while Plato held that God used the dodecahedron to arrange the constellations across the heavens.

The five solids also shaped the model proposed by Johannes Kepler, a German astronomer of the early seventeenth century. With five planets then known, Kepler tried to fit their orbits to the shapes of the solids. He abandoned the idea, but his experiments led him to discover that planetary orbits are not circular.

At first glance the Platonic solids may not look secret or sacred. Their discovery, though, reshaped our understanding of how the universe is ordered, and the beauty of their regular forms remains a profound influence on sacred geometry and architecture. All five can be drawn out from Metatron's Cube, which is itself built from the Flower of Life.

Places of Pilgrimage

The structures that most clearly follow sacred geometry tend to be places of worship or tombs, though not always, as the hogan and the yurt show. Sometimes a natural feature of the landscape becomes part of the building itself, as with the church that once crowned Glastonbury Tor. Siting a temple on a high place adds value to the work of human hands, making it prominent in the landscape and drawing on the shared symbolism of the altar, the holy building, and the mountain, all of which express the human reach toward the divine.

The places gathered under this theme range from the famous to the obscure, the grand to the humble, including some that few would think to call a pilgrimage site at all. What unites them is that each was built with the conscious use of sacred geometry, an attempt to please God by shaping a small piece of Heaven on Earth. That single intention is what renders them holy. The outward form of this celestial reflection may change, but its meaning holds steady, and like every symbolic discipline sacred geometry keeps growing and evolving, much like the spiral traced by the golden mean. Some whole cities are revered as earthly images of the heavenly archetype, among them Varanasi, Jerusalem, and Mecca.

Temples, Cathedrals & Shrines

Built houses of worship and their sacred enclosures, where geometry shapes a fitting setting for the divine.

Borobudur

This Buddhist temple in central Java, built in the eighth century AD, is a remarkable example of sacred geometry, since its form, structure, and intention together resemble a map of the universe. Imposing from a distance, it grows more astonishing up close through the sheer detail of its ornament and the subtlety of its construction.

Like other Buddhist monuments, Borobudur takes the form of the Mandala, itself a diagram of the cosmos, with each face of the square base set squarely to a cardinal direction. Seven square terraces rise from the platform, each smaller than the last, giving the building its stepped shape. The uppermost square level carries three raised circular tiers set with stupas, seventy-two in all, crowned by a single central stupa that seems to pin the whole structure to the ground.

Each of the seventy-two stupas holds a statue of the Buddha, glimpsed only with difficulty through the latticework, the filigree stone showing how closely the worlds of spirit and matter lie. A stone staircase climbs each side toward the circular levels, so that the square earth meets the circular heavens. Seen from above the temple forms a perfect mandala, a living version of the cosmic map that reminds the pilgrims circling it that their physical lives run parallel to the tenets of their faith.

Chartres Cathedral

Among the most impressive and mysterious of the French cathedrals, Chartres was raised in the Gothic style during the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, though its ground is far older. Beneath it lies an ancient pre-Christian mound with a grotto called the Pregnant Virgin, and a Roman temple once replaced the earlier Druid temple on the site. Chartres remains an important center for the worship of Mary. If the human body and the cosmos mirror one another, Chartres belongs to that reflection, for its plan is a cross-shaped mandala representing the nine gates of the body.

Legend clings to Chartres. Some say the Knights Templar carried lost tongues such as the Language of the Birds, along with the treasures of Solomon, and hid them within the walls, and it is even rumored that the Ark of the Covenant rests in its crypt. As the cathedral has nine gates, legend gives it nine designers, the knights who excavated Solomon's temple in the eleventh century and returned with its secrets.

Notably, the cathedral holds no burials. Many such secrets could be read into the very fabric of the building, since the early masons knew the arcanum of sacred geometry and the construction methods that later inspired the fraternity of the Freemasonry Guide. These secrets work across many dimensions and include sound, shape, and color as much as material form. Echoing the Pregnant Virgin grotto below, the cathedral keeps a veil, the Sancta Camisia, said to be the one Mary wore when she gave birth to Christ. Given by Charlemagne in 876, it was first housed in an earlier wooden church that burned down, but the veil was saved, and the main body of Chartres as it stands today was finished in just twenty-six years, between 1194 and 1220.

Labyrinth

Church

No two churches are exactly alike, yet they share features that carry symbolic weight. The earliest Christians gathered quietly, often in secret, in one another's homes to share a meal and discuss their faith. Knowing they risked persecution if discovered, they recognized one another by the sign of the Ichthys, sometimes scratched quickly into the dust. Once the Roman Empire embraced Christianity in the fourth century AD, much of Western Europe adopted the basilica, a Roman public hall, for worship. The word basileus means "king," so the building honored the King of Heaven.

Aligned to the compass points, a church seen from above takes the shape of the Latin Cross of Christ, a design that renders one of the faith's central symbols in three dimensions, as if the architects wished their God to recognize them from above. The long arm of the cross is the nave, named for the Latin word for ship, casting the church as a "ship of souls" and marking the spiritual journey toward the altar. The altar sits at the short, eastern end, the place of the rising sun, so the path from west to east traces the movement from the material world to the spiritual and from ignorance to illumination. The font, emblem of life's beginning, stands at the western end, the physical and spiritual entrance and the public face of the building.

At the front of the nave stands the pulpit, where the officiant reads from Scripture. The lectern holding the Bible is often carved as an eagle whose wings bear the book, the eagle being the emblem of St. John the Evangelist. The arms of the cross form the transept, holding side chapels and space for monks, nuns, and pilgrims, and the crossing of the long and short arms is marked by the spire. This tower dominates the landscape as a reminder of the church and stands as an Axis Mundi.

As the Church grew in wealth and mystique, its design and symbolism grew more elaborate. The northern end of the transept carried the old pagan associations of that direction as a place of cold, damp evil and usually held scenes from the Old Testament, and many still call the northern entrance the "devil's door." The southern transept, by contrast, stood for goodness, sunshine, and warmth, and often held scenes from the New Testament. Paintings, sculptures, and stained glass reminded those who could not afford a Bible, or could not read, of the stories of Scripture. Churches also hold a sanctuary, meaning "holy place," where the Blessed Sacrament was kept. By custom a criminal who reached the sanctuary unapprehended was shielded from the law, and so the word also came to mean a safe place.

Delphi

The holiest site in Ancient Greece, Delphi was fixed when Zeus released two eagles from the ends of the Earth and they met on the southern slopes of the holy mountain Parnassus. As the navel of the world, the spot was marked by the Omphalos stone, a conical artifact carved with a criss-cross pattern whose meaning is uncertain but may depict the crossing of the birds in flight. The Omphalos lay at the heart of the chamber of the Delphic oracle, where the priestess known as Pythia, the Python, delivered her oblique pronouncements, never about the future but about present concerns. Scented vapors were said to rise through the cave, and it is unknown whether they were natural gases or sacred herbs that sharpened her sight. So great was her reputation that foreign monarchs traveled vast distances to consult her, and an eternal flame burned in the hearth of the oracle.

The name Delphi shares a root with the word for womb, which suggests an earlier site dedicated to the Earth Goddess Gaia. The shrine to Apollo was held to be the meeting point of the powers of Heaven and Earth, and his temple grew larger and more elaborate over the years and remains a place of pilgrimage. Only impressive ruins survive of the once-vast complex, which had been filled with statues, inscriptions, and treasures offered in honor of the oracle. Many were looted by Nero, and the temple was finally closed in AD 390 by the Emperor Theodosius as un-Christian.

Hindu Temples

Like other holy buildings, the heart of a Hindu temple is a womb-like inner sanctum, the vimana, which houses the effigy of the deity to whom the temple is dedicated. A space usually surrounds this chamber so that worshippers can circle the shrine, always clockwise, following the path of the sun. Directly above the vimana rises the main tower, the shikhara, which from outside appears as a highly decorated, colorful stepped form representing the ascent to Heaven and standing as an emblem of the holy mountain, Mount Meru. Its tiers repeat the same motifs, sometimes depicting stories of the deities, and these graduated patterns express death and rebirth, the soul climbing gradually toward the summit and ultimate union with the collective consciousness, or Brahman.

Hindu temples fall into two broad styles: the Nagara of northern India and the Dravida of the south. The chief difference is that the Nagara tower is more dome-shaped, while the Dravidian towers are pointed. The sharpest contrast with most Western places of worship lies not in sacred intention but in use. A Hindu temple may be busy at any hour, thronged with people come to make puja, or worship. Offerings might include fragrant malas, garlands of flowers, ghee, rice, fruit, and incense, all bought outside the doors amid a jumble of worshippers' shoes, since to be anything but barefoot inside is held to be an act of disrespect. The Gopura, or gateway tower, marks the main entrance, and like the Shinto Torii it marks the threshold between secular and sacred space.

Mecca

For Muslims, Mecca marks the point on Earth where the vertical axis of Heaven and space meets the horizontal axis of human existence and time. One of the most famous of all holy cities, it is the goal of the Hajj, the pilgrimage that every Muslim is called to make at least once in a lifetime, an act so central that it forms the fifth of the five Pillars of the faith. At its heart stands the Great Mosque, built to surround the Ka'aba at its center. This large Cube-shaped building is held to have been raised by the prophet Abraham after his wife Hagar found water in the desert at the Well of Zamzam. That well, revealed by an angel, saved their son Ishmael from dying of thirst and moved Abraham to build the Ka'aba, which Muslims believe mirrors a heavenly house and stands on the site of the first dwelling built by the first man, Adam.

People of other faiths are forbidden to enter Mecca. The first Western woman to do so was Lady Evelyn Cobbold, who embraced the faith and performed the Hajj in 1933 at the age of sixty-six, while the explorer and scholar Sir Richard Burton had entered the Ka'aba itself in the nineteenth century, disguising himself as a Sufi to gain access, knowing that discovery would have meant death.

The word Ka'aba shares a root with "cube," and the granite building is imposing, standing nearly fourteen meters high with sides of about eleven and thirteen meters. Its four corners point roughly to the four points of the compass, and it is the focal point of prayer: wherever they are in the world, the devout pray toward the Ka'aba, often using special compasses to align themselves. Set into the eastern corner is the sacred Black Stone, widely thought to be the remnant of a meteorite and perhaps the original source of the site's holiness. Before the coming of Mohammed, the stone was a focus for worship of the goddess Al'Lat, who had seven priestesses, and pilgrims circled it seven times in honor of the seven known planets of the ancient world.

The Ka'aba is draped entirely in a black silk cloth banded with gold embroidery bearing texts from the Quran. Its doorway sits two meters above the marble base, reached by a wheeled wooden staircase, and cleaners are admitted twice a year to sweep it with simple brooms, wash it with water from the ever-flowing Zamzam, and sprinkle it with rosewater. Few have been inside, where a marble floor, gilded silver panels, and further Quranic texts are found. Parts of the Ka'aba and the Grand Mosque are arranged so that pilgrims can re-enact key moments of Hagar's story; an elevated walkway atop the tiered building, for instance, is where the faithful run back and forth seven times in memory of her desperate search for water. Pilgrims also travel to the small town of Mina, where they throw stones at three pillars representing Satan.

Pagoda

The pagoda grew out of another piece of sacred architecture, the stupa, and like the stupa it came from India, spreading across the world as Buddhism gained influence. Originally built to house holy relics or sacred texts, it is rich in symbolism, though the details of its construction vary. A distinctive feature of Chinese pagodas is the "underground palace." Because burial in China was underground, unlike the overground methods of India, and because the pagoda served mainly as a reliquary, this underground palace folded the Chinese way of burial into an Indian Buddhist form. Known by the exotic name of the dragon cave or dragon palace, the space is circular or hexagonal, and though it may hold many rare treasures, its most important contents is a stone container, richly decorated with jewels and gold, holding relics of the Buddha or other saints. The whole cave is adorned with friezes and statues, and because no daylight reaches it, the painted decoration stays bright and vivid.

Above the underground chamber rises the base of the pagoda's distinctive storied tower. Early pagodas had low, plain bases, while later builders, wanting grander structures, gave them much larger bases, sometimes topped with a decorative pedestal. Base and pedestal are Square, reflecting the element of earth and the four directions and giving the building a solid foundation, and pagodas are raised in many materials: stone, wood, brick, even metal. The tower itself, the most recognizable part, may be hollow or solid and varies in shape, but its symbolism matters most: it represents the Axis Mundi, the conceptual pole joining the Heavens to the Earth, and expresses the human desire for elevation and enlightenment. Its several storeys are each capped with a roof whose edges flick upward to repel evil spirits.

At the very top is the steeple, a vital part of the building that represents the "country of the Buddha" and so also the country in which the pagoda stands. Like the stupa's finial, it often bears the symbols of the sun and the moon and is crowned with a shape resembling a flame or a Lotus bud, both emblems of enlightenment. The obvious hazard of so tall a building topped with metalwork is lightning, and pagodas make an open target; many of Japan's wooden pagodas were burned to the ground. Yet this very danger is felt to charge the building with even greater heavenly energy and power.

Pantheon

Tucked between other buildings in modern Rome, the Pantheon is among the most remarkable achievements of Western civilization in both sacred geometry and architecture, all the more astonishing given that it was raised almost two thousand years ago. Its name means "temple to all gods" in Ancient Greek, and it was conceived around AD 50, when the old deities had not yet been displaced by Christianity, as part of Augustus Caesar's plan to rebuild the city in his own image and to honor the gods and goddesses who had aided his rise. The temple was not finished until the following century, under the Emperor Hadrian.

Several features mark the Pantheon as a work of sacred geometry: the rectangular porch with its triangular pediment set squarely on top, the circular main temple, the domed roof representing the Vault of Heaven, the proportions of the whole, and the symbols inlaid into the floor. These rest on the basic units of measurement that Pythagoras held to be part of the harmony of the cosmos and so believed to have been devised by the Creator. That celestial harmony is reflected throughout: the main temple measures forty-four meters in diameter and forty-four meters from floor to ceiling.

The dome is a particularly magnificent feat of engineering, enclosing an internal volume with no reinforcing support; only fifteen hundred years later was its size marginally exceeded by the dome of the Church of Santa Maria del Fiore in Florence. As an emblem of the Vault of Heaven, the dome became a favored form for sacred buildings, and cities vied to raise ever bigger and bolder examples. The Pantheon's dome is open to the sky through an opening called the oculus, or "great eye," nine meters wide and symbolic of the sun. The ceiling's graduated ribs both lighten the structural load and represent the major planetary gods of Rome: Mars, Venus, Mercury, Jupiter, and Saturn.

Heavier materials were used for the foundation and base, with progressively lighter stone toward the top. The floor holds marble from across the Roman Empire, set in a mosaic of large stone slices called opus sectile, which forms a squared Circle, itself a feature of sacred geometry and a magickal symbol in its own right. The original design as a temple to all gods included apses, or niches, holding the shrines of the deities, but with the coming of Christianity the shrines were removed, the building was renamed the Church of Santa Maria Rotunda, and the niches now hold the tombs of Italian kings and of the Renaissance artist Raphael.

Rosslyn Chapel

This small chapel, also called the Collegiate College of St. Matthew, stands in the quiet hamlet of Roslin some seven miles south of Edinburgh, and has drawn worldwide curiosity since Dan Brown's novel and the film that followed, which suggested that the Holy Grail was hidden there and that the bloodline of a child born to Christ and Mary Magdalene could be traced to the building. The frenzy of speculation has made fact hard to separate from fiction, with unproven rumors placing the Ark of the Covenant, the original Stone of Destiny, and even the Grail itself within its walls, though the chapel's mystical charisma needs no media excitement to sustain it.

It was commissioned by Sir William Sinclair, or St. Clair, in 1446, apparently as the beginning of a much larger Catholic church and, like other grand religious buildings, a way for its patron to accumulate credit in Heaven. Rumor even held that Sinclair was Christian Rosenkreuz, founder of the Rosicrucian Order. The chapel is smothered in elaborate carvings of angels and demons, roses, stars, elephants, pyramids, serpents, plants not found in Britain when it was built, and many figures of the The Green Man, along with carvings that still guard their secrets and dozens of masons' marks left by the anonymous craftsmen. Its 213 carved stone cubes seem to represent Chladni patterns, the shapes that fine powder forms on a taut surface vibrated at certain frequencies.

📜
The "Apprentice Pillar" is especially notable. Legend tells that while the master mason was abroad studying designs, his apprentice dreamed he had carved the pillar himself, and his vision was so vivid that he set to work. On the master's return, consumed by jealousy at the ornate result, he struck the apprentice dead with a blow to the head. The apprentice, the master, and the grieving mother are all immortalized in stone, and the tale, which seems to have arisen in the eighteenth century, echoes the story of Hiram Abiff at the heart of Freemasonry Guide.

At the pillar's base coil eight dragons, from whose mouths vine tendrils spiral upward in a representation of the Tree of Life, and at its top a Latin inscription reads:

Wine is strong, a king is stronger, women are stronger still, but truth conquers all.

The Green Man appears 110 times throughout the chapel, an unusually pagan presence in a Christian space, the carvings youthful on the eastern side and ageing toward the west with the course of the sun. Perhaps the greatest puzzle of Rosslyn is this very mingling of pagan and Christian images, as though its designers understood that integration is the surest path to wisdom.

St. Peter's Basilica

The largest building of religious significance in the world, able to hold sixty thousand people, and effectively the mother church of Roman Catholicism, St. Peter's Basilica in the Vatican City was raised in honor of the One God. Yet in a reminder of symbolism's universality, it shares several features with a pre-Christian building in the same city raised in honor of many gods and goddesses, the Pantheon. The basilica is said to stand on the site of St. Peter's grave, following his martyrdom by crucifixion on an Inverted Cross at his own request, and excavations in the 1930s did reveal a small shrine directly beneath the main altar. As with all sacred places, it is felt to possess a numen, a presiding spirit, here associated with St. Peter.

Its shared symbolism is clearest from above, where, whatever the various architects intended, it strikingly resembles sacred buildings of other faiths: the Circle of the dome sits within the Square of the structure below, symbolizing the union of spirit and matter. Inside are reminders of the sacredness of the place, great works of art and statues honoring figures of the Catholic Church, among them Queen Christina of Sweden, who gave up her throne to convert, and a statue of St. Peter whose stone foot has been worn smooth by the kisses of millions of pilgrims.

Where the Pantheon's niches now hold the tombs of Italian kings, the basilica's thirty-nine niches hold statues of saints. The building overflows with richly symbolic art; a work by Bernini honoring Pope Alexander VII, for instance, shows a winged skeleton lifting a fold of red marble drapery and holding an hourglass, a reminder that time is short and death certain, while the praying pope is flanked by figures of Justice, Prudence, Charity, and Truth, the last shown standing on a globe with England underfoot and a thorn piercing her foot, an emblem of the trouble the English gave the Roman Catholic Church.

Stupa

If Buddhism has an archetypal example of sacred architecture, it is the stupa, or chorten. These elaborate constructions are as encrusted with symbolic meaning as with jewels, and the perfection of their form is said to represent the very mind of the Buddha. Originally the stupa, from the Sanskrit for "heap," was just that: a simple earth mound covering relics of the Buddha, whose cremated ashes were divided into eight parts that, with the urn and embers, made up the original ten stupas. The Emperor Ashoka later opened these and redistributed their contents among the thousands of stupas he built after his conversion in the third century BC, and as the buildings and their symbolism grew more complex they became objects of veneration rather than simple funerary monuments.

Nothing in a stupa is left to chance. Though not every stupa can hold actual relics of the Buddha, each generally contains soil from the eight burial places of his ashes, along with relics of other saints, sacred texts, and statues. Its base is always a Square, aligned to the four cardinal directions, representing the earth element and the four corners of the world, with four gates often guarded by deities. Thirteen steps lead to the spire atop the dome: the first ten mark the steps to enlightenment, the final three the levels of super-consciousness. The hemispherical dome resting on the square represents the World Egg and the Vault of Heaven, and resembling the rising sun or a pregnant belly, it is called the "womb," a female form balancing the male square; as both womb and tomb, it recalls the cycle of death and rebirth.

Above the dome rises the conical spire, which represents the Axis Mundi as the element of fire, the upright triangle at its peak providing the clue, energy reaching upward like flame. The parasols along the spire are emblems of protection, their varying number also significant, and the parasol is one of the Ashtamangala, the Eight Auspicious Objects of Buddhism. Next comes the Crescent Moon, symbol of the element of air and an ancient feminine sign whose waxing and waning mark the menstrual cycle, and then the circle of wholeness and completion, here signifying space and the sun. Together the sun and moon stand for Absolute and Relative Truth.

At the very summit sits a jewel-like shape carrying the same meaning as the flame of enlightenment sometimes shown rising from the Buddha's head, equating to the Crown Chakra of pure bliss. In its entirety the stupa is the body of the Buddha: the base his legs, the dome his torso, and where his eyes would be sits a second cube-shaped structure, the harmika, set between dome and spire and sometimes marked with the Wisdom Eyes of Buddha. The relics within the "womb" of the dome symbolize the germ of new life. Pilgrims walk around the stupa clockwise, following the path of the sun and "winding up" its energy, which spirals upward to the Heavens, and seen from above the whole appears as a Mandala. Though eastern in origin, stupas now stand around the world; the largest, the Phra Pathom Chedi in Nakhon Pathom, Thailand, rises 127 meters high.

Synagogue

The word "synagogue" means "assembly," and its décor adapts to the country and surroundings in which it stands. Before the synagogue, hereditary priests called kohens performed the rites while worshippers remained outside the main body of the temple, making their burnt offerings to God. The rabbis who later officiated belonged to a more democratic movement, and a key difference is that, whereas God was believed to dwell in the temple, the synagogue is an unconsecrated building. Its emphasis falls on education and the study of the Talmud, the Jewish laws and legends, yet for all this it remains a sacred building.

The focal point of any synagogue is the Ark, a box holding the scrolls of the Torah, the first five books of the Bible that God gave to Moses. Richly decorated though it may be, the ark is not an object of veneration; it matters only because it holds the Word of God. It is set into the wall facing the direction of the original Temple in Jerusalem, a sign that although the physical Temple no longer stands its idea remains close, a reminder of exile, and prayers often turn toward the coming of the Messiah, believed to coincide with the rebuilding of the Temple for the third time. God is never portrayed figuratively in the synagogue, a feature shared with Islamic places of worship, and unlike the decorated windows and Gospel scenes of Christian churches, its patterns are abstract and indeterminate.

Temple Mount

One of the holiest cities in the world, Jerusalem is a place of pilgrimage for Jews, Muslims, and Christians alike. For Jews it is the most sacred site of all; for Muslims it ranks third after Mecca and Medina; and because the Church of the Holy Sepulchre stands where Christ is said to have been buried, it is the holiest place for Christians, a sanctity that drove the medieval Crusades to secure it for Christendom. With these sometimes conflicting faiths in such close proximity, this holiest of cities has also seen some deeply unholy conflict.

King David made Jerusalem the capital of Israel around 1000 BC, and his son Solomon, reputed one of the wisest of men, was instrumental in building the famous Temple that gave a permanent home to the Ark of the Covenant and a focus for worship, a building so influential that it shaped many features of Masonic temples. The room that held the Ark was the "Holy of Holies," an adyton entered only by the High Priest, and only on Yom Kippur. Many treasures were carried off when Nebuchadnezzar II deported the Jews and destroyed the building in the sixth century BC; after Cyrus II of Persia freed the people in 538 BC the Temple was rebuilt, though more modestly, and King Herod restored it to full glory in the first century AD, only for the Romans to destroy it soon after. The third temple, it is said, will be built when the Messiah comes. The name "Temple Mount" arose because the site was believed to lie on Mount Moriah, where Abraham had been ready to sacrifice his son Isaac, and the Talmud holds that the world itself was created from the Foundation Stone on the mount. Though the Temple is gone, the whole site remains full of sacred significance.

The Western Wall. The Western Wall is all that survives of the First Temple in Jerusalem, and is also called the "Wailing Wall." Jewish people come here to mourn the loss of the Temple and to pray, and little slips of paper bearing prayers fill every available crevice of the stones.

The Court of the Priests. As with sacred symbolism the world over, the very center of the Court of the Priests held an altar, representing the world mountain, the mountain of the gods, and so the Axis Mundi.

The Dome of the Rock. In Islam the Dome of the Rock is called the "Noble Sanctuary." At the center of this building, whose golden-domed roof is the focal point of the city, lies the Foundation Stone of the World, said to be the place where Heaven and Earth meet. It is believed to be the same stone on which Abraham sought to sacrifice his son and from which Mohammed ascended to Heaven on his magickal horse, Buraq, to receive the Islamic prayers from God. The Ark of the Covenant rested on this rock in the time of the First Temple, and it was used for blood sacrifices on Yom Kippur in the time of the Second; because it lay within the Holy of Holies, only the High Priest could approach it.

The Well of the Souls. Directly beneath the Foundation Stone is the Well of the Souls, a small natural cave whose southern walls are reinforced to support steps. This is where Mohammed is said to have ascended to Heaven, and where the Last Judgment is reputed to take place. Here again is the symbolism of the cave as the womb of the Earth, a place not only of death but of rebirth.

Standing Stones, Earthworks & Land Figures

Sacred forms drawn from the landscape itself, raised in stone and earth or traced across the ground for the gods to see.

Glastonbury Tor

Few firm facts are known about Glastonbury Tor, which only deepens its mystery. What is certain is that this conical hill, the meaning of the word tor, cuts an awe-inspiring silhouette against the sky. Seen from above the mound resembles a vulva and has become a natural emblem of feminine power. The surrounding land was once watery fenland, making the tor an island and an easily defended vantage point for the Celts who lived there.

📜
Its myths fill the hill with Druids, Wiccans, fairies, and the Celtic gods of the Underworld, and name it the burial place of King Arthur. The surrounding land was known as Ynys yr Afalon, which has led many to identify it with Avalon, the Isle of Apples.

One of its most intriguing features is the spiral path that winds from the foot of the hill to its summit, forming seven terraces. Various explanations have been offered, from agricultural use or the tracks of grazing animals to defensive ramparts. Given the tor's setting and its standing as a place of pilgrimage, the likeliest reading is that the path served the same purpose as the stepped routes of other sacred sites with seven levels of ascent. People still climb the Spiral path as both a physical and a spiritual journey. The seven levels answer to the seven planets known to the ancients and to the deities bound up with them, the same reasoning that gave the ziggurat its seven levels. In parts of Wales it is still the custom to walk to the top of the nearest high place around the Lughnasadh festival at the start of August.

The tower that now crowns the tor is a stark punctuation to the sacred landscape. A fifth-century fort once stood there, replaced by a medieval church, St. Michael's, which lasted until an earthquake destroyed it in 1275, on the eleventh of September. The church rebuilt some eighty years later survived until the Dissolution of the Monasteries, after which the tower became a place of execution where the last Abbot of Glastonbury was hanged. Only a roofless, haunting ruin remains today.

Glastonbury Zodiac

Few places in the British Isles, or arguably anywhere in the world, hold the mystical charisma of Glastonbury. Steeped in legend and tied to the Arthurian tales, it is believed by many to be a place where the seen and unseen worlds meet. John Dee, the celebrated magickian, seer, and astrologer to Queen Elizabeth I, was fascinated by it and is said to have been the first to propose that an image of the celestial zodiac lies across the sacred land in and around the town.

Several hundred years later, Katharine Maltwood took up the idea. Born in 1878, she was an artist and scholar whose marriage to the wealthy advertising manager John Maltwood freed her to devote herself to art, sculpture, and above all to antiquarian study. She was drawn to the story of Joseph of Arimathea's supposed visit to Glastonbury and to the Arthurian legends, and while living in Somerset she made the discovery for which later seekers would remember her. In 1929 she claimed that the outlines of the zodiac figures could be traced in the earthworks within a ten-mile radius of the town, publishing her findings in The High History of the Holy Grail.

Working from large-scale Ordnance Survey maps, she first picked out the shape of Leo the Lion, and in the 1930s she went so far as to commission aerial photographs of the whole area, at great expense for the time. Her work caused a sensation that was soon overshadowed by the outbreak of the Second World War. Today some treat the Glastonbury Zodiac as fact and others as pure fancy, a product of imagination and the wish to wrap still greater mystery around a place already brimming with the marvelous.

Green Man Maze

Few finer examples exist of a magickal symbol hidden in the landscape than the Green Man Maze at Penpont House near Brecon in Wales. Although the term "Green Man" was not coined until the late 1930s, it captures perfectly the idea of a spirit of nature living among the leaves from which he is formed. Set in a wild and magickal stretch of countryside, the maze is surprisingly recent, built in 2000 to mark both the Millennium and the fortieth birthday of the landowner, who helped raise it with his wife and family. That the The Green Man can be seen whole only from above, the vantage of the gods, makes it more intriguing still.

David Eveleigh designed the maze according to the principles of sacred geometry, using dowsing to align the figure with the points on the horizon where the solstice sun rises and sets. The design draws on the ancient Sun Cross wheel, along with a Pentagram laid over an Elven Star to set its proportions and structure. At the very center sits the root of an upturned tree, an image that held meaning for the Celts and also appears in the Upanishads.

Medicine Wheels

The Medicine Wheel also serves as a mandala-like graphic focus for meditation, but in its original form it is built as a living part of the landscape, and like the European stone circles it resembles, the reasons behind its design and construction remain largely a mystery. The largest of these stone wheels, sometimes called the American Stonehenge, is the Bighorn Medicine Wheel at Medicine Mountain. Set at an elevation of 9,640 feet, it is an impressive sight, and the plain it rests on is sacred in its own right, a place where ceremonies marking rites of passage were held for hundreds of years by peoples including the Cheyenne, Arapaho, and Shoshone.

Made of half-buried rocks laid out like a giant wagon wheel, it has twenty-eight spokes radiating from a hollow-centered central cairn about three feet tall. Each spoke runs thirty-six feet, and the whole measures 245 feet around. Six smaller cairns surround the central wheel, each open on one side, giving it a C-shaped look from above. As at Stonehenge, certain points align with the sun, the moon, and the planets; in particular, the central cairn and another on the outer rim work just as Stonehenge's heel stone does on the summer solstice. The twenty-eight spokes likely stand for the days of the lunar cycle, while other lines mark earthly bearings for celestial bodies such as Sirius, Rigel in the constellation of Orion, and Aldebaran. The age of the Bighorn Medicine Wheel is uncertain. Like the Circle, the wheel stands for eternity, without beginning or end, an image of the endlessness of passing time.

Menhir

A menhir is a large, upright standing stone. Our ancestors clearly spent great effort to find and quarry these stones, shape their tapered tops, drag them into place, and set them upright, yet their purpose is far from clear. Unless a menhir belongs to a group, such as a stone circle, there is no consensus about what it signified. The word comes from two Breton words meaning "long stone." Menhirs are most common in Western Europe but appear as far afield as Asia and Africa. Theories about their function or symbolism include territorial markers, parts of a calendrical system, and sites of sacrifice. Even their age was until recently uncertain; long thought to belong to the Bronze Age, around 3000 BC, they may in fact be much older.

Any tall, narrow object can mark time by its shadow, much as the gnomon stands at the center of a sundial, and the menhir may have served this purpose as well as standing as a fertility symbol, given the obvious phallic shape of these mighty stones. As with many ancient artifacts, early Christians regarded menhirs with deep suspicion and toppled many of them, while others were explained away as the work of demonic forces.

📜
The Rudston monolith in Yorkshire stands twenty-six feet high and weighs at least eighty tons, and is believed to reach as deep into the ground as it rises above it. Standing right beside the tiny village church, local legend says the Devil hurled it at the church but missed through poor marksmanship, while a rival tale says God threw it to punish people desecrating the churchyard.

Rudston itself means "cross stone," from rud stan, and the monolith was at one point Christianized with a Latin Cross set on its top.

Nazca Lines

The Pampa Colorada desert near Nazca, high on a plateau between the Andes and the Pacific, is one of the driest places on Earth. Across a vast area of some 200 square miles lie more than a hundred enormous animal shapes, so large that they can be seen whole only from the sky, by the gods or by means of transport the people who made them two thousand years ago could never have imagined. Rain has rarely fallen in the valley over the last ten thousand years, and the figures, made by scraping away the topsoil much as similar shapes were created in Britain and elsewhere, have been preserved from vehicles and footprints.

Some lines run more than five miles, an astonishing feat of concentration to keep the form true without ever seeing the finished result, and all of it carried out in dusty, arid conditions. The shapes include geometric patterns as well as human figures, a Serpent, flowers, a lizard, a spider, and several kinds of bird, among them the great warrior god Huitzilopochtli in the form of a hummingbird. No one knows for certain why they were made. Theories range from a calendrical system to landmarks for visiting craft from the sky, but the most logical suggestion is that they were made to communicate with the gods, to please them as they looked down upon the Earth.

Newgrange

In the northeast of Ireland, Newgrange is an immense construction at least 5,000 years old. As a sacred space it echoes the idea of the cave as a hidden place of ritual, the womb of the Earth, a place of death and rebirth. From the outside it is imposing: a mound of earth ringed by a stone wall, encircled by standing stones and nearly a hundred roughly worked stones laid end to end, eleven meters high and almost ninety meters across. Mounds like this were later thought to be the homes of the numinous beings believed to dwell there, the earth spirits, fairies, and creatures of the Otherworld.

Before the entrance lies a large stone beautifully engraved with spiral motifs, chipped into the rock with flint tools to remarkable precision. The Spiral is associated with the passage of time and the cycle of the soul through death to rebirth, often depicted as a Labyrinth, though there is nothing labyrinthine about the heart of Newgrange. A long, narrow passage leads to the main room, a vaulted space with three flat-roofed chambers, and seen as if by X-ray from above, the passage and chambers form a cross. The main hall is roofed by artfully stacked stepped stones that resemble the shape of a bee skep.

One of Newgrange's secrets was discovered only in 1972. In the roof above the entrance is an opening that lets a shaft of light fall on the triple spiral pattern more than eighteen meters inside the chamber, and this happens just once a year, at the winter solstice. Other ancient monuments are thought to have an astronomical purpose, and the roof at Newgrange proves the point plainly. Archaeologists have found bone fragments from the era of its building, suggesting it also served as a burial chamber, and the spiral motif together with the way the chamber fills with light shows that its architects linked the cycle of time and the passage of the sun with the journey of the soul.

Serpent Mound

Some sacred features of the landscape are natural, suggesting the shapes of animals or birds, while others are made by human hands, and the labor poured into them reminds us how vital certain images were to the people who shaped them. One such structure is the Serpent Mound in the northeastern United States, built some two thousand years ago by Native Americans, either the Hopewell or the Adena. Made of yellow clay and stones, it takes the form of a snake 382 meters long, its tail coiled into a Spiral and what appears to be an egg held in its mouth.

Though no one can say precisely why it was raised, the primal importance of the Serpent as a magickal symbol is well attested, counting among the most important animal symbols for Native Americans, Celts, Hindus, Assyrians, and others. Some argue the figure represented a particular deity, and since the serpent stood for the powers of the Earth it would make sense to shape it so the gods above could see it. The creature lies at the edge of a promontory, and burnt stones at the center of the "egg" suggest fires were once lit there, visible for miles and perhaps signaling that the snake, the numen of the place, was awake and guarding her people. The Egg is a symbol of fertility and rebirth, while the spiral of the tail suggests the coiled power of the Earth's energy.

Stone Circles

Why would our ancestors quarry gargantuan blocks of rock, with only stone, fire, and water for tools, and then drag these immense slabs many miles to raise them as part of a circle? The use and intention of stone circles remains obscure despite centuries of conjecture, but their sheer scale and the difficulty of building them suggest they were among humanity's earliest attempts to please the gods. Standing stones and circles still invite endless speculation: some believe them sites of Druidic sacrifice, while others suggest burial places, temples, meeting places, or sites of Goddess worship. The early Christian Church damaged many circles, demolishing a twelve-stone circle on the sacred island of Iona as recently as 1560.

📜
When a structure is so old that its original purpose has been forgotten, myths inevitably arise to explain it. The Merry Maidens circle in Cornwall, for instance, is said to be young girls turned to stone for dancing on the Sabbath.

Whatever their first use, many of these circles scattered across northwestern Europe seem to act as a calendrical system, their stones carefully aligned to mark astronomical events, the phases of the moon, and the position of the sun at the solstices.

Stonehenge

The most impressive single stone circle of all is arguably Stonehenge in Wiltshire, England. Its scale is vast, and the impact of its first appearance against the gently rolling backdrop of Salisbury Plain is breathtaking. Built between 3000 and 2100 BC, its standing stones were quarried from bluestone found only in the Preseli mountains of Wales, some 385 kilometers away.

Stonehenge remains a primary focus for neo-pagan groups, especially at the summer solstice, when the "heel" stone aligns with the sun rising over the horizon. Pagan worship resumed there in 1905 after hundreds of years, performed by the Ancient Order of Druids founded in the nineteenth century. Long a site of peaceful worship and a month-long festival, in 1985 it became the scene of the so-called "Battle of the Beanfield," when travelers heading to the solstice festival were stopped by police, some in riot gear, in an incident that left vehicles smashed and many people hurt. Access, especially at the solstice, is now carefully controlled, yet the stones stand as a powerful symbol of the freedom to choose both one's gods and the way they are worshipped.

Towers, Pillars & Stepped Monuments

Vertical forms that reach upward, modeling the world mountain and the axis that joins Earth to Heaven.

Obelisk

An obelisk is a tall, tapering, needle-like pillar with a square base and a small pyramid at its top. The name comes from the Greek obeliscos, meaning pointed pillar. Though we tend to associate them with Ancient Egypt, they also stood in Rome, Assyria, and Ethiopia. Convention held that an obelisk should be eight to ten times as tall as it was wide at the base; Cleopatra's Needle on the bank of the Thames in London, for instance, is ninety-two feet tall and fifteen feet wide.

Obelisks symbolize the rays of the sun, which broaden as they reach the Earth, hence the tapering form. Their sides were usually engraved with inscriptions to the sun god Ra, to whom they were dedicated, and in Ancient Egypt the obelisk gained cult status as an emblem of this god, generally set in pairs with one slightly larger than the other. It is also a phallic symbol, emblematic of the earth god Geb reaching up toward the sky goddess Nut, and a symbol of the World Tree or Axis Mundi. Some thirty obelisks still stand around the world, the greatest concentration in Rome, which holds eight Egyptian and five Ancient Roman examples. These once-pagan symbols were exorcized and crowned with a Latin Cross to make them acceptable to Roman Catholic sensibilities. The Egyptian government later gave obelisks to Great Britain, France, and America, though transporting these giant stone pillars proved a considerable challenge.

Pyramid

At its most basic, the upward-pointing Triangle represents fire, the very meaning of the Greek word "pyramid." The pyramid is an immensely strong construction, both symbolically and practically, the solid base of the Square supporting the four triangles that meet at the peak, and the shape is as impregnable as it looks. The Pyramids have become a symbol of Egypt, capturing the essence of that land, together with the Sphinx, more completely than any modern logo could.

Much has been written about the Pyramids, but the symbolism of the shape should not be confused with claims of "pyramidology." In essence, the pyramid represents the axis mountain of the world, said to have risen from the waters of creation and to stand for existence itself. The cavern-like interior, where the bodies of the pharaohs were interred with elaborate rites, recalls the natural cave that drew early people both as a womb-like refuge and as a place for secret religious rites. The sloped exterior is echoed in the steeply sloping internal passages, whose angles were meant to help the souls of the interred pharaohs ascend to Heaven.

Ziggurat

Ziggurats are pyramid-like models of the cosmos that arose in Mesopotamia in the last centuries BC. Great care was taken to align their four corners exactly to the cardinal directions, and a main staircase climbed from the base to a small shrine at the summit. As with any tall building, the aim was to recreate the holy mountain; indeed, the word ziggurat shares a root with the Babylonian word for "mountain peak," and a ziggurat at Larsa, in modern Iraq, bore a name meaning "The House of the Link between Heaven and Earth." Many creation myths tell of a primeval mound existing before the Earth and sky were parted, and the builders of ziggurats, like those of the Pyramids, likely sought to echo it.

Ziggurats generally had three, five, or seven terraces, each narrowing as it rose. Where there were seven, they answered to the first seven planets and their deities, and each was painted in that body's color: black for Saturn at the base, white for Jupiter, red for Mercury, blue for Venus, yellow for Mars, silver for the Moon, and gold for the Sun at the uppermost level. People could ascend to the gods by climbing the ziggurat, and the gods could descend by the same route, as myth records when Moses received the Ten Commandments atop Mount Sinai or when the gods of Mount Olympus spoke in thunder and lightning. The ziggurat shares its meaning with the Axis Mundi and the Omphalos as the center of the world, and climbing to its summit marked the completion of a spiritual journey, the physical levels of the ascent corresponding to spiritual stages of cleansing and enlightenment.

Sacred Dwellings

Everyday homes built as small models of the cosmos, proof that the humblest shelter can hold as much meaning as the grandest temple.

Dogon Granary

For the Dogon people who live near Timbuktu in Mali, grain is so precious and sacred that the granaries holding it also store other valuables, and the building itself is rich with meaning. Crops thrive there despite the harsh, infertile climate. These distinctive structures, with smooth curved walls of baked mud, mirror the people's creation myths in both their interior and exterior.

The Dogon's indigenous faith is animistic, holding that every object carries a spirit, and the door and lock of the granary tell the story of the Nommo, ancestral beings said to have come from the sky, their carved figures guarding the contents. The granary is divided into eight partitions, standing for the eight internal organs of the body and the original eight seeds the Nommo are said to have given the people, while the granary as a whole represents the "belly of the world," an eternally fertile female figure. The Dogon hold a long tradition of astronomical observation and are said to have identified the companion of Sirius, called Sirius B, long before telescopes could detect it, describing the universe as "infinite, but measurable."

Hogan

The hogan is the traditional dwelling of the Navajo people of the southwestern United States. Built of timber and earth, it is said to recreate the home that the coyote and beaver gods made for the first man and woman. The Navajo did not draw the Western line between secular and sacred space, and the hogan itself is a model of the cosmos, about as plain an example of sacred architecture as one could find. The doorway of this circular, single-roomed house faces east to welcome the morning sun and receive the blessing of its rays, an eastern opening also favored by certain tunnel-nesting birds and wild bees.

A hearth sits at the center, the southern half of the room standing for the male element and the northern half for the female. That union is echoed in the building itself: the first stage sets a forked female log toward the north and a straight male log toward the south, resting in the cleft, a pairing that speaks of the bond between husband and wife and serves as a fertility emblem as well as a firm foundation. A third forked log is set to the west to balance the entrance, and the rest is raised from stacked logs and other materials, with gemstones tucked among them and the whole sometimes covered in mud or earth against the weather. As with any sacred building, the hogan is consecrated before use, with chanting from an ancient song called the Blessingway that recounts the making of the first hogan, and a clockwise circuit of the building honors the path of the sun.

Trulli

Trulli are fairytale-like little houses found in southern Italy, especially in the town of Alberobello in Puglia. Built of white stone with a distinctive conical roof, they were originally raised to avoid the heavy taxes levied on permanent dwellings; being dry-stone structures, they could be pulled down quickly when needed. Their most intriguing feature is the symbols painted on their conical roofs, which include alchemical signs, magickal marks, signs to ward off the Evil Eye, and symbols of the planets and gods. At the very top of each roof sits a further symbol, the pinnacoli, or pinnacle, probably a survival of ancient sun worship, a reading supported by the fact that most trulli face east. The symbols on each are the personal expression of its owners and a wonderful example of spirituality within the domestic home.

Yurts and Tipis

Just as the simplest magickal symbols often hold the most concentrated meanings, so our most basic dwellings can say as much as the grandest cathedral or temple. The yurt is the portable home of the nomadic peoples of Central Asia, though its popularity now reaches far beyond. It is circular, its walls built from a trelliswork of sticks that folds together for transport and is traditionally covered in felt or other fabric. The dome-shaped roof, or shangrak, is also made of wooden lattice, and while the rest of the yurt is replaced over the years, the shangrak is an heirloom passed down through the generations.

A central hole in the roof lets smoke escape from the hearth below, and that rising smoke is itself an image of the Axis Mundi, carrying the prayers, wishes, and food offerings of the dwellers directly to the gods. The tipi is a similarly portable, circular dwelling that mirrors the greater cosmos, though its floor is more egg-shaped than round. Three poles tied together form a tripod, standing for man, woman, and the Great Spirit; further poles, their number set by the tipi's size, are woven against this junction, and the whole is wrapped in a semicircular cloth fitted with smoke flaps that can be adjusted to the wind. Its central pillar of smoke carries the same meaning as that of the yurt.
