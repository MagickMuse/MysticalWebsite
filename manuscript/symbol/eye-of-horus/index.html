Eye of Horus

◇Notes/Warnings
Also called the wedjat or udjat. According to legend, Seth tried to blind his nephew Horus by piercing or gouging out this eye during their conflict. Either Thoth or Hathor (depending on the source) used magick to restore it. The restored eye became among Egyptian magick's most potent protective symbols, signifying healing, completeness, and the defeat of disorder. Horus's eyes became linked with the sun (right eye) and moon (left eye), making them symbols of regeneration. Paired wedjat-eyes on the sides of coffins magickally let the deceased person's mummy see through the coffin walls. Appears on the Metternich Stela protecting the Horus-on-the-crocodiles scene. Used on amulets, funerary equipment, and magickal implements throughout Egyptian history.

Symbolic Meaning

The Eye of Horus (wedjat) represents healing, restoration, wholeness, and protection. Its origin myth encapsulates the triumph of order over chaos: Seth's attack caused injury, but divine magick restored what was damaged.

The Restoration Myth

During the lengthy conflict between Horus and Seth over the throne of Egypt, Seth attacked his nephew's eye. Some versions say he pierced it; others say he gouged it out entirely. The god Thoth (or in some accounts, Hathor) used magick to restore the eye and return Horus's sight. This act of magickal healing established the wedjat as a symbol of restoration and completeness.

Solar and Lunar Associations

Horus's right eye became associated with the sun, his left with the moon. The daily journey of the sun and the monthly cycle of the moon both involve apparent damage (setting/waning) followed by restoration (rising/waxing). The wedjat thus connects to cosmic cycles of regeneration.

Funerary Use

Paired wedjat-eyes painted on coffin sides allowed the mummy to magickally see through the coffin walls. The symbol appears on canopic equipment, including gold covers for embalming incisions, often flanked by the Four Sons of Horus.

Protective Function

On the Metternich Stela, right- and left-facing wedjat-eyes protect the Horus-on-the-crocodiles scene, watching over the entire composition. The symbol's protective power made it one of the most common amulet forms in Egyptian magick.

Mathematical Fractions

The six parts of the wedjat were used to represent fractions in Egyptian mathematics, with the whole eye equaling one. This connection to wholeness reinforced its symbolic meaning of completeness and restoration.
