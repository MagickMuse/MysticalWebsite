Unicursal Hexagram

Notes/Warnings
A six-pointed star drawn in one continuous, unbroken line, unlike the standard hexagram formed from two separate interlocking triangles. Adopted by Aleister Crowley as a personal emblem, frequently shown with a five-petalled rose at its center.

Any figure that can be traced in a single continuous stroke, without the pen ever leaving the page, is described as unicursal; the five-pointed star is the familiar example. A six-pointed star is normally built from two separate interlocking triangles, but the unicursal form renders the whole shape in one unbroken line.

Crowley's Emblem

Aleister Crowley took up the unicursal hexagram as a personal sign. Magicians have long prized unbroken figures for protection, on the reasoning that a line with no gaps leaves no opening through which an unwanted spirit might reach the safe space at the center.

The Rose and the Hidden Pentagram

Crowley deepened the design by placing a five-petalled rose at its heart. The rose stands for a concealed secret, and joining the outermost point of each petal brings out a pentagram hidden inside it. In this arrangement the pentagram, emblem of the Divine Feminine, balances on a single point in the inverted position, which can suggest the precedence of matter over spirit, or, in the reading of the witch Gerald Gardner, the value of facing darkness in order to understand it. Counted together, the points of the hexagram and the pentagram total eleven, a number taken to signify divine union.
