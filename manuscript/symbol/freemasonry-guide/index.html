Symbol Grimoire

Freemasonry Guide

This guide collects the symbols and emblem language most commonly associated with Freemasonry and related traditions of allegorical instruction. Meanings are often deliberately left open to interpretation within the Craft, and many symbols can carry more than one accepted reading.

Acacia

Acacia represents initiation and is used in Masonic funerals as a symbol of rebirth. Hiram Abiff’s burial place is marked with an acacia branch.

Blazing Star

Freemasons use the name Blazing Star for the pentagram. The number 5 is treated as sacred in this framing.

Column

Freemasonry draws heavily from the Temple of Solomon as a symbolic model. Lodges commonly place two columns at either side of the main door, reflecting the temple’s pillars.

The right column is Jachin, associated with stability. The left column is Boaz, associated with strength. The two columns also carry a male and female polarity. Jachin is often painted red, tied to solar and active qualities. Boaz is often painted white, tied to lunar and receptive qualities.

In some rites, the columns can indicate grade. Apprentices stand before the red column, Masons before the white column, and Master Masons in the central space between them.

Compasses and Square

Compasses combined with the Mason’s Square form one of the foremost emblems in Freemasonry. Some depictions also incorporate the letter G and may add solar and lunar elements.

Both tools are instruments of precise measurement used in disciplines that allow little room for error, including building, construction, and architecture. Because measuring tools define and delimit form, they can symbolize judgement and definition.

In this pairing, the circle created by the compasses is framed as feminine, while the square is framed as masculine. The union can be read as spirit combined with matter, and Earth combined with Heaven. Another reading frames the circle as space and the square as time, with the mason as architect who works across these dimensions.

The compasses can also signify a divine architect of the Universe, since they draw the perfect circle. Because the compasses turn on a central pivot, they can also symbolize Axis Mundi, the circular nature of time, and the ouroboros.

The Square

The phrase on the square or on the level refers to being open, honest, proper, and above board. The square carries the symbolism of the number 4 and can represent solidity, respect, and security.

The Angle of the Compass

Accounts differ on whether the angle of the compass carries a defined meaning. Some interpret varying angles as reflecting degrees within Masonry, but there is no universal agreement.

The Triangle

The square and compasses can be read as forming a triangle. This can be interpreted as a sign of stability and a sign of the spirit world.

The Six Pointed Star

A six pointed star, also called the Seal of Solomon, can be constructed from the square and compasses design by drawing a line across the open point. The seal consists of two interlocking triangles and is one of the most ancient and widely used magickal symbols, including in alchemy. One interpretation reads it as as above, so below.

The Letter G

The letter G does not always appear within the square and compasses emblem. When it does, interpretations vary. Some read it as God, others read it as Geometry. Other readings include Generation, Government, or the Great Bear as a marker of the celestial pole or center. There is no single definitive meaning.

Hiram Abiff

Hiram Abiff is a central figure in Masonic legend. Hiram is remembered as a Master Mason skilled in metalwork and as one of the prime designers of the Temple of Solomon in Jerusalem.

After the temple’s completion, three workers tried to force Hiram to reveal the secrets that would qualify them as Master Masons. Hiram refused, stating that the knowledge could be gained only through experience. The three killed Hiram and buried him, marking the grave with an acacia tree.

The initiation of the grade of Master Mason re enacts the death of Hiram as an initiatory death and rebirth. The three blows are interpreted as three kinds of death, body, feelings, and mind. In this framing, the initiate leaves the old self behind and is reborn into higher moral values associated with Hiram, including integrity, knowledge, and detachment.

Hammer and Chisel

In lodge meetings, the hammer represents the powers of intellect that drive thought and will. It is also used as a symbol of authority for the Worshipful Master who presides.

The chisel is useful only when directed by the hammer and can represent intellect and finer discernment.

Level and Plumbline

The level and the plumbline are paired tools with practical use in building and symbolic use in Freemasonry. The level comprises a set square from whose center the plumbline hangs. Because the plumbline defines both vertical and horizontal lines, it can also take on cross symbolism.

The vertical level symbolizes the apprentice, and the horizontal level symbolizes the degree of Fellowcraft. The word level also carries the philosophical sense of steady and honest, which links directly to the tool’s function.

Rule

The rule represents correct measurement as a foundation of building work. The degrees of measurement depicted on the ruler can be read as symbolic. In imperial measures, the degrees of 12 and 24 correspond to the daily cycle of the Sun, and in this sense the rule represents the macrocosm. The rule also keeps everything in order and acts as a guide.

Triangle, Shining Delta

In Freemasonry, the triangle can symbolize the Greek capital A and is called the Shining Delta. It can also signify meanings in triplicate, such as right thinking, right speaking, and right doing. One microcosmic reading frames the base as duration, with the two sides as paired qualities such as light and darkness, and male and female.

A traditional Masonic triangle is described with a 36 degree apex angle and two 72 degree base angles. One famous symbolic use is the triangle with a blazing star and a pentagram, as seen on the Seal of the United States and on the United States Dollar.

Tracing Board, Trestle Board

The tracing board, also called the trestle board, is a symbolic form of an item of practical equipment used in masonry. It originates in a flat piece of wood or cloth used by the Master Mason as a drawing board for diagrams, schemes, and measurements.

Initially it could be represented as cloth rolled out on the floor at the beginning of a meeting. Later it is represented as a wooden board containing signs and symbols relevant to the brothers within their degrees. Tracing boards are elaborate pictorial works, weaving symbols into an allegorical whole. The tools and emblems serve to remind the initiate of more esoteric meanings tied to personal betterment and enlightenment.

Different boards are made for different degrees, and tracing boards may show scenes such as the murder of Hiram Abiff or a ritual reenactment. They can also represent temple features including pillars, a checkerboard floor, and the porch with two columns.

Masonic Apron

The Masonic apron is elevated in symbolic status. Usually made of leather, how it is worn can indicate grade, with the bib worn up for the apprentice and down for higher grades. Wearing the apron represents work and the need to be industrious. It is also linked to the fig leaves of Adam and Eve, and in this sense it preserves modesty.

Masonic aprons often display signs and symbols of the Craft. A well known example is an apron owned by George Washington, given in 1784 by the Marquis de Lafayette, with embroidery by his wife. The border colors of red, white, and blue are noted as the national colors of both France and the USA.

Symbol Cluster Found on Aprons

Some aprons gather many Masonic symbols into a single design. Examples and their stated meanings include:

  • All Seeing Eye, watchfulness and the Supreme Being
  • Rays, the power of the Supreme Being to reach inside the hearts of men
  • Rainbow, the arch of Solomon’s Temple supported by Jachin and Boaz
  • Moon, the female principle
  • Globes on top of pillars, peace and plenty
  • Three tapers, stages of the Sun, rising in the East, at noon in the Southern sky, and setting in the West
  • Trowel, spreading love and affection, the cement that binds the brothers of Freemasonry
  • Five pointed star, friendship
  • Checkered pavement, duality of opposites, including male and female
  • Steps, degrees of masonry
  • Coffin, death and therefore rebirth
  • Skull and cross bones, mortality and also rebirth
  • Ark, safety and refuge
  • Tassel and knot, ties that bind the brothers
  • Sun, the Light of God and the male principle
  • Sword and heart, justice being done and that nothing can be hidden from the eyes of the Great Architect
  • Seven six pointed stars, seven as the seven liberal arts and sciences
  • Beehive, industry and a reminder that man should be rational and industrious

47th Problem of Euclid, Bride’s Chair

The 47th Problem of Euclid is a mathematical theorem called one of the foundations of Freemasonry. It is numbered simply because it is theorem 47 in Euclid’s published book. Its significance is described as somewhat nebulous, but it appears in regalia and lodge decorations.

It is also called the Egyptian string trick. A demonstration uses a loop of string with 12 evenly spaced knots and three stakes to create a 3, 4, 5 triangle. The same lines can be extended to form squares of 9, 16, and 25 parts.

This device is described as a way the Egyptians could remeasure fields after Nile floods. It creates a perfect right angle used in building. Pythagoras is said to have traveled to Egypt and may have learned it there, or discovered it independently.

Ashlar

In material terms, ashlar is rough stone taken straight from the quarry. In philosophical terms, ashlar symbolizes the rough and imperfect state of a person before they are rendered smooth and perfected in an ideally realized state. It is linked to the alchemical idea of base matter that can be perfected through intellectual and spiritual realization.

Point Within the Circle

A circle with a dot at its center is a sign of birth and resurrection. It dates back to Egyptian use as an emblem of Ra, the Sun god.

This symbol is also associated with the feast days of St John the Baptist and St John the Evangelist, which fall on the summer and winter solstices.

Temple Floor, Checkered Pavement

The temple floor is described as checkered tiles of black and white. These colors represent the duality of opposites, including night and day, dark and light, male and female, fire and water, Earth and air, and other expressions of the same polarity.

In Ancient Egypt, these colors were used as a reminder of the need to unify spirit and matter.

GAOTU, Great Architect of the Universe

Abbreviations form a large part of Masonic ritual, with the idea that pronouncing certain words can dilute their power, so initials are used instead.

GAOTU stands for Great Architect of the Universe, referring to God. It parallels the idea of God as builder or designer of the macrocosm and the Freemason as designer of the microcosm.

The Masonic System of Elements

Within Freemasonry the elements double as a map of the initiate's symbolic journey. Each is paired with a human faculty, a guiding quality, and a level of attainment.

  • Fire answers to the spirit, carried by ardor and enthusiasm, and marks the level of initiation.
  • Water answers to the soul, carried by sensitivity and emotion, and marks the level of religion.
  • Air answers to the mind, carried by intellectual power, and marks the level of philosophy.
  • Earth answers to the body, carried by materialism, and marks the level of physical life.
