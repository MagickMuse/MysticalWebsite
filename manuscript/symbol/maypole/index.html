Overview

Once a common sight across northern Europe, in city as well as countryside, the maypole stood for the phallus of the May King and for the World Axis or World Tree. Hung with flowers and trailing ribbons, it was the focus of pre-Christian Beltane rites.

The Dance

Girls each took a ribbon and danced widdershins while boys danced deosil, the interweaving of counterclockwise and clockwise movement representing solar male and lunar female powers braided together, a celebration of cosmic harmony that was also a potent fertility rite.

Suppression and Revival

Evangelical Protestants damned the maypole as a lewd, anti-Christian object, and it was banned in England, with many cut down and burned, though some communities refused to give up theirs. The Restoration of 1660 brought a defiant revival, with over a hundred raised in London alone. In time its pagan roots were forgotten and it dwindled into a quaint emblem of a vanished Merrie England.
