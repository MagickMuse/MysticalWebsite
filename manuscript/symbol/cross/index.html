The earliest known example of the cross comes from Crete and dates to the fifteenth century BC, although the symbol is ancient beyond proper reckoning. As the convergence of the vertical and horizontal lines, the cross symbolizes the union of the material and the spiritual.

As a geometric tool, the cross has no equal. Place it inside a circle and it divides the circle equally. The cross is said to "give birth to" the square.

Because of its four cardinal points, the cross represents the elements and the directions. In the West, the cross equates with the number 4. In China, however, it is associated with the number 5, since the "dot" in the middle, where the two arms intersect, is also counted.

The cross is sometimes disguised as another symbol, such as a four-petaled flower. All over the world, the cross is a symbol of protection.

Context and Meaning

The meanings of the cross are not always straightforward. To a practicing Catholic, the inverted cross is a reminder of St. Peter, who elected to be crucified on a cross of this type. To others, it is a popular emblem of Satanism, a tradition that favors inversions and reversals. The context in which a symbol is set is of paramount importance.

Symbolic Meaning

The cross represents intersection, balance, and protection. This ancient shape embodies the meeting of spiritual and earthly planes, the four directions, and the balance between opposing forces.

Magickal Properties

  • Sacred intersection - represents the meeting point of heaven and earth, spirit and matter
  • Four directions - symbolizes north, south, east, and west
  • Balance - brings equilibrium between opposing energies
  • Protection - wards against harmful influences and marks sacred boundaries

Associated Concepts

  • The four cardinal directions
  • The meeting of divine and human realms
  • Equal-armed cross (pre-Christian sacred geometry)
  • Crossroads magick and liminal spaces
  • Balance and protection

During the witch-hunt era, the cross stood as a central Christian symbol even as authorities conducted brutal executions. The fear of death curses profoundly shaped execution protocols.

Condemned witches were considered especially dangerous in their final moments. Authorities believed dying people possessed supernatural access to curse power, and witches could use their final breath to unleash devastating harm.

Execution protocols included:

  • Gagging condemned witches or cutting out their tongues before execution
  • Working quickly to prevent final words from being spoken
  • Strangling or drowning victims to prevent them from cursing while burning
  • Forcing the condemned to face away from crowds to prevent the evil eye

These brutal measures reveal the genuine fear that even those who condemned witches to death held for curse power. The same authorities who claimed to act in the name of the cross believed deeply in the witch's ability to harm through spoken words and dying curses.
