Overview

The SATOR square is an ingenious palindromic word square that can be read in any direction: left to right, right to left, top to bottom, and bottom to top. The oldest known example was found in the ruins of Herculaneum, the ancient Roman city destroyed by the same eruption that buried Pompeii in the first century AD. Since then it has surfaced in all sorts of places: the cathedral at Siena, a small chapel near Rennes-le-Château in France, and sites in Malta, Syria, and the United Kingdom. It also appears on the cover of a famous magickal grimoire long regarded as a foremost guide to the Kabbalah.

A Hidden Cross

Both the meaning and the origin of the square remain a mystery, a literary labyrinth that does not give up its secrets easily. It has been claimed as a Christian device, since its letters can be rearranged into the form of a cross spelling PATER NOSTER, "Our Father," running both vertically and horizontally and sharing the central letter N. The two leftover pairs of A and O are read as Alpha and Omega, the first and last letters of the Greek alphabet.

Meaning

The words of the square translate literally as:

  • SATOR: sower, planter
  • AREPO: possibly a proper name
  • TENET: to hold
  • OPERA: work, effort
  • ROTAS: wheels

This yields readings such as "Sator, the sower, holds the wheels by his work," or "The sower, Arepo, holds the wheels with effort." The sense can be bent toward almost any ideology, though the suggestion of sowing and harvesting, of death and rebirth, is hard to ignore. Scholars have puzzled most over Arepo, which appears nowhere else; its inventor may simply have chosen it because it fitted and assigned a meaning afterward, though it is close to a Celtic word for "plow," which sits neatly with the agricultural imagery.

A Protective Charm

Many hold that the SATOR square carries protective power, its elegantly interlocking words reaching a depth of meaning beyond the literal. Dutch settlers in Pennsylvania used it as an invocation to guard their cattle. Evil entities are said to be unable to remain in a room where the square is displayed.
