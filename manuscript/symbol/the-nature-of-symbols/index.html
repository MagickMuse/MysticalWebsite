Symbol Grimoire

The Nature of Symbols

Symbols exist everywhere. They live and breathe as part of the landscape of human understanding, present in religions and belief systems across the world, from the lavish iconography of the Catholic Church to the elaborate meanings behind the seemingly simple mudras of the East. They appear chiseled into rock, woven into coded alphabets said to contain secrets given by angels or demons, and hidden within arcane scripts of languages long forgotten.

Etymology

Secret comes from the Latin secretus, meaning "separate" or "set apart."

Sacred also derives from Latin, sacrere, meaning "holy" or "consecrated."

Symbol comes from the Greek verb symballein, meaning to "throw together," literally a "coincidence." As an object, the symballon (meaning "mark" or "token") appeared on ancient Egyptian artifacts used as signs of identification, like a ticket. As a verb, symballein was used by the Greeks to describe a gathering that was occluded or veiled, understood only by the initiate and camouflaged to the casual observer.

In this sense, all symbols are, by their very nature, secrets of one sort or another.

The Symbolate

Any object that also carries symbolic meaning is called a symbolate. A wine glass might be a handy vessel for drinking, but as the chalice or goblet it becomes a significant and universal symbol of the feminine, used as such in ritual. As the Holy Grail, it transforms further into a symbol of the deepest spiritual quest, the search for the Goddess within, the marriage of opposites, and one of the ultimate aims of alchemy.

Mercia Eliade defined the sacred as that which manifests itself as a reality different from "normal" realities. The sacred is present in everything. When a particular tree, mountain, or stone is recognized as sacred, that object becomes greater than the mere sum of its parts. If something miraculous occurs at a certain place, that place becomes sacred, and the person associated with the event may be venerated as a saint or equivalent.

Hidden Languages

Throughout history, there have been occasions when keeping certain information hidden was essential. Knowledge can be dangerous, yet recognizing other members of a secret cult or society could be a matter of survival.

In the early days of Christianity, when allegiance to the faith could result in death, followers used a simple symbol of a fish as a secret code of recognition. Gypsies, Romanies, and other traveling people developed an extensive symbolic language to keep track of one another, communicating which farmers were friendly, where companions were headed, and which towns might best be avoided. Groups like the Cathars and the Knights Templar left visual clues along the course of their wanderings, the true meaning of which we are still trying to unravel centuries later.

Native American trackers and hunters used signs and symbols invisible to anyone not attuned to them, yet carrying vital information to those who understood.

Symbolic Literacy

Symbolism concerns humanity's very knowledge of itself. Understanding symbols provides an elemental way of explaining the Universe, a method of interpreting reality that transcends time, race, culture, and everyday concerns.

J.C. Cooper expressed it this way: "The symbol goes beyond the individual to the Universal and is innate in the life of the spirit. It is the external expression of the higher truth which is symbolized."

Pictorial symbols override literacy. The messages they carry have nothing to do with the ability to read or write. The alphabetical symbols we juggle so readily are themselves among the most potent and magickal symbols ever devised, yet pictorial signs predate them all.

At the heart of symbolic study are constantly repeating themes that go to the core of human concerns: fertility, protection from the elements and the evil eye, communication with spirits and the Gods, good health and good luck, and a fine time in the Afterlife.

The Paradox of Analysis

If mystique is inherent to a symbol's nature, does explaining it strip something away? How can words describe something designed to transcend words in the first place?

To understand a symbol properly requires pinning it down for a moment, examining it from all angles, climbing into the heads of its creators and understanding their time, their beliefs, their daily concerns. Yet there is a danger in this process. Like a butterfly killed with chloroform, the outward appearance may be preserved while the essential nature is destroyed.

The Moon illustrates the counterargument. Along with the Sun, she is arguably one of humanity's most important symbols, visible to everyone, universally acknowledged. Her constantly changing face mirrors the menstrual cycle, allegedly governs tides and growth patterns, and has inspired myths, legends, and romances across every culture. The term "lunatic" derives from Luna, whose swollen fullness was believed to draw out underlying madness. When humans physically walked on her surface, fears arose that the act would somehow anger the Moon Goddess or render her symbolic meaning obsolete.

It did not. The Moon is no more a simple lump of rock than a mother is merely a member of the species with functioning reproductive organs. Instead, the "giant leap" yielded a new perspective. For the first time, humans saw their home planet from above: tiny, jewel-like, and frighteningly alone. Earth itself became a symbol, and the sorcery of a full Moon remained intact. A different perspective increased understanding without diminishing mystery.

The Mutable Nature of Symbols

A simple symbol in a film, a book, or an advertisement says far more than any wordy explanation could, acting as a visual shortcut to the part of our minds that understands them. The eye as the "mirror of the soul," the butterfly's metamorphosis as a sign of transformation, the number of the Beast, all function as compressed meaning that bypasses literacy entirely.

The meanings of symbols can change, just as a caterpillar changes into a butterfly. The number 666, personified in some cultures as the number of the Beast, sends a frisson of terror through the soul. Yet scholarship has suggested the original number may have been 665 or even 616, the subject of a scribal error thousands of years ago. It does not matter. The symbol 666, imprinted on collective consciousness after centuries of association with evil, is what carries the weight. The symbol itself has become the reality.

No Beginning, No End

Rodin said, "Man never invented anything new, only discovered things." While some symbols have been constructed for specific purposes, everything is inspired in some way by the natural world. Sometimes the revelation is immediate. Other discoveries are the result of years of painstaking observation.

The pentagram is one such discovery. The shape made in the sky by the planet Venus as she orbits the Sun describes a definite five-pointed star over the course of eight years and one day. The ancient Akkadians first charted this design and realized that the Morning Star and the Evening Star, previously considered separate celestial bodies, were one and the same. The Greeks called the morning star Eosphoros, "bringer of the dawn" (later known as Lucifer, the brightest angel cast out of the Heavens). As the Evening Star, it was Hesperos, "star of the evening," giving us the name of evening prayers, vespers.

The Goddess known to the Akkadians as Ishtar/Inanna presided over this planet, divinity of both love and war. Venus remains the only major planet aside from Earth designated a feminine spirit. The Mayans determined their calendrical system from her movements and chose propitious positions to time their wars. The five-pointed star still used as a military symbol, stenciled onto tanks and woven into insignia, derives from the stately movement of this great astral Goddess.

The apple given by Eve to Adam contained a hidden symbol within it: the pentagram created by the pattern of the seeds. Eve offered not only knowledge of the divine feminine but a symbol of the true marriage of opposites, the feminine number two wedding to the masculine number three. Eve personifies Ishtar, Venus, Aphrodite as the Goddess of sensual love, and the word "venereal" derives from Venus herself. Ishtar was later demonized as the Whore of Babylon.

A seemingly simple shape made in the sky by a planet's path, full of complexities and contradictions, clarifying some aspects while posing further questions. The quest to understand a symbol's meaning is as much a personal voyage of discovery as a collective one.
