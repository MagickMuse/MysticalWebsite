Labarum

◇Notes/Warnings
Also known as the Chi Rho or the Monogram of Christ.
◇Type
Cross
◇Culture
Gnostic/Early Christian
↗Other Symbols5

The Labarum was the earliest Christian symbol, used before the Latin Cross was adopted. Its provenance is very old, for it is thought to be an adaptation of the Egyptian Ankh and also served as a symbol of the Sun god Mithras, in which case it appears enclosed in a Wheel. The Labarum proved a convenient device for bridging the gap between Mithraism, the favored religion of Roman soldiers, and Christianity.

The symbol contains the first two Greek letters of Christ's name, which is why it is also called the Chi Rho or the Monogram of Christ. It rose to prominence when the Emperor Constantine saw it in a dream. Constantine, the first imperial emperor to adopt Christianity, was a major figure in its spread; some accounts say his whole army saw the sign hanging in the sky, an echo of the Crescent Moon and star that Osman, founder of the Ottoman Empire, later saw in a dream of his own.

Like Osman, Constantine was told in his dream that the sign would bring victory, so he had it painted on his soldiers' shields. It may have been coincidence that his forces soon won the Battle of the Milvian Bridge, but the Labarum's importance was secured, and it was adopted as the emblem of the Empire in AD 324, twelve years after that decisive victory.
