Botanical Source

Commiphora myrrha of the Burseraceae family, also classified as Balsamodendrom myrrha and traded as gum myrrh, common myrrh, hirabol myrrh, or simply myrrha. The yielding Commiphora species are shrubs or small trees up to 10 metres high, bearing sturdy knotted branches, trifoliate aromatic leaves, and small white flowers. The trunk exudes a natural oleoresin: a pale yellow liquid that hardens into reddish-brown tears, known as myrrh. Native collectors deepen the yield by making incisions in the bark.

Distribution

Native to north-east Africa and south-west Asia, particularly the Red Sea region of Somalia, Yemen, and Ethiopia.

Other Species

Several Commiphora species yield myrrh oleoresin, including African or Somali myrrh (C. molmol) and Arabian or Yemen myrrh (C. abyssinica). Bisabol myrrh or opopanax (C. erthraea) belongs to the same family.

Folk and Historical Use

Myrrh has served Eastern and Western medicine since the earliest times, with use documented some 3,700 years ago. The ancient Egyptians employed it in embalming, perfumes, and cosmetics. In Chinese medicine it addresses arthritis, menstrual problems, sores, and haemorrhoids. Western tradition (per Joseph Miller) attributes to it an "opening, heating, drying nature," suited to asthma, coughs, common cold, catarrh, sore throat, weak gums and teeth, ulcers and sores. It has also been used to treat leprosy. Myrrh appears in the British Herbal Pharmacopoeia as a specific for mouth ulcers, gingivitis, and pharyngitis.

Therapeutic Actions

Anticatarrhal, anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, antiphlogistic, antiseptic, astringent, balsamic, carminative, cicatrisant, emmenagogue, expectorant, fungicidal, revitalizing, sedative, stimulant (digestive and pulmonary), stomachic, tonic, uterine, and vulnerary.

Extraction & Characteristics

Two main commercial forms exist. The resinoid (and resin absolute) is produced by solvent extraction of the crude myrrh and presents as a dark reddish-brown viscous mass with a warm, rich, spicy-balsamic odour; because it does not pour at room temperature, a solvent such as diethyl phthalate is sometimes added. The essential oil is steam-distilled from the crude myrrh and presents as a pale yellow to amber oily liquid with a warm, sweet-balsamic, slightly spicy-medicinal odour.

Principal Constituents

The crude contains resins, gum, and about 8 percent essential oil. Major constituents include heerabolene, limonene, dipentene, pinene, eugenol, cinnamaldehyde, cuminaldehyde, and cadinene, among others.

Safety

Non-irritant and non-sensitizing in normal use, but possibly toxic in high concentration. Not to be used during pregnancy.

Other Uses

The oil, resinoid, and tincture appear in pharmaceutical products, including mouthwashes, gargles, and toothpaste, and are used in dentistry. Oil and resinoid serve as fixatives and fragrance components in soaps, detergents, cosmetics, and perfumes, particularly oriental types and heavy florals. They also feature as flavour ingredients across most major food categories and in alcoholic and soft drinks.
