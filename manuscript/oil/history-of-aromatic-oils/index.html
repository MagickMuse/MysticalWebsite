Oil Grimoire

History of Aromatic Oils

Across cultures and centuries, aromatic oils have moved between the temple, the apothecary, and the kitchen. This guide follows that long arc: how essential oils form within plants, why they smell the way they do, and how the earliest civilisations discovered, refined, and ritualised their use, all the way through to the modern practice of aromatherapy and the safety principles that guide it today.

Natural Plant Origins

The distinctive scent and flavour of plants comes from essential oils, the volatile aromatic compounds held within their tissue. These oils are concentrated in tiny pockets and glandular cells, often near the surface, and evaporate easily when exposed to air, infusing the surrounding atmosphere with their characteristic aroma. Citrus is the clearest example. Pressing the peel of an orange releases a visible burst of fragrance, since the oil sits in such profusion just beneath the skin.

Other plants are far more reluctant. The flowers of the rose hold so little aromatic oil that roughly one ton of petals is needed to produce 300g of rose oil. Why some plants generate volatile oils in abundance and others scarcely at all is not fully understood. The aromas appear to draw or repel certain insects and animals, support transpiration and other internal processes, and offer a measure of protection against disease. For these reasons the oils are sometimes described as the hormone or lifeblood of a plant, language that reflects how concentrated and central they are to its living function.

Aromatic oils can be drawn from nearly any part of a plant, including the seeds, bark, root, leaves, flowers, wood, balsam, and resin. A single species often yields several distinct oils depending on the part processed. The bitter orange tree gives three at once: orange oil from the fruit peel, petitgrain from the leaves and twigs, and neroli from the blossoms. The clove tree produces different oils from its buds, stalks, and leaves. The Scotch pine offers separate oils from its needles, wood, and resin. The breadth of aromatic material available from natural sources, and the slow development of techniques for extracting it, reaches back to the heart of the earliest civilisations.

Ancient Civilizations

Aromatic plants and oils have been used for millennia as incense, perfume, cosmetic, medicine, and culinary flavouring. In most early cultures their religious and therapeutic uses were inseparable, and traces of that intertwining remain visible today. Juniper sprigs are still burnt for purification in Tibetan temples, and frankincense continues to be offered during Roman Catholic services.

In the ancient world, perfumes were inseparable from animist and cosmic worldviews. They emerged from ritual practice as fumigations, libations, and ablutions, taking shape first as offerings made during invocations and theophanies before they were treated as instruments of healing.

India

Vedic literature dating to around 2000 BC records over 700 aromatic substances, including cinnamon, spikenard, ginger, myrrh, coriander, and sandalwood. The Indo-Aryan word atar carried multiple meanings at once: smoke, wind, odour, and essence. The Rig Veda treats these substances as both liturgical and therapeutic, and frames the handling of herbs as sacred work performed in partnership with the plants themselves. This body of knowledge developed into the Ayurvedic system of medicine, which has been transmitted in unbroken succession into the present day.

China

Chinese herbalism accompanies the practice of acupuncture and is recorded in the Yellow Emperor's Book of Internal Medicine, which dates to more than 2,000 years BC. Aromatics such as opium and ginger appear among its remedies. The same substances also served religious functions in early ceremonies, including the Li-ki and Tcheou-Li rites. Borneo camphor remains in heavy ritual use across China to this day.

Egypt

Egyptian civilisation carries the richest associations with the earliest aromatic materials. Papyri from the reign of Khufu around 2800 BC list numerous medicinal herbs, and a manuscript from around 2000 BC speaks of fine oils, choice perfumes, and the incense of temples by which every god is gladdened. Cedar and myrrh were used in the embalming process, and traces of scented unguents and oils such as styrax and frankincense have been recovered from ornate jars and cosmetic pots within the tombs. Stone inscriptions along the Nile valley preserve a complete iconography of how these oils, balsams, and fermented liqueurs were prepared.

The Egyptians were renowned cosmetologists. One celebrated remedy, kyphi, combined sixteen ingredients and could be burnt as incense, worn as perfume, or taken internally as medicine. It was considered antiseptic, balsamic, and soothing, regarded as an antidote to poison, and credited with the power to ease anxiety, lull sleepers into rest, and brighten dreams.

Treasures from the East

Natural aromatics and perfume materials were among the earliest trade goods of the ancient world, prized for their rarity and held as treasures. When the Jewish people departed Egypt for Israel around 1240 BC, they carried precious gums and oils with them, along with the knowledge of how to use them. The Book of Exodus records that Moses received the formula for a holy anointing oil during the journey, blended from myrrh, cinnamon, calamus, cassia, and olive oil. This oil was used to consecrate Aaron and his sons into priesthood, a rite passed from one generation to the next. Frankincense and myrrh, both counted among the great treasures of the East, were the gifts offered to the infant Jesus at his birth.

Phoenician merchants moved scented oils and gums outward from the Levant, first across the Arabian peninsula and gradually throughout the Mediterranean, particularly into Greece and Rome. They opened the West to the riches of the Orient, carrying camphor from China, cinnamon from India, gums from Arabia, and rose from Syria, while guarding their trading routes as a closely held secret.

Greeks

The Greeks learnt a great deal from the Egyptians. Herodotus and Democrates, who visited Egypt during the fifth century BC, later transmitted what they had gathered about perfumery and natural therapeutics back to Greek soil. Around 425 BC Herodotus became the first to record a method for distilling turpentine, alongside the earliest detailed accounts of perfumes and the materials that produced them. Dioscorides afterwards compiled a five-volume materia medica known as the Herbarius, which catalogued the sources and uses of the plants and aromatics employed by both the Greeks and the Romans.

Hippocrates, born in Greece about 460 BC and revered as the father of medicine, prescribed perfumed fumigations and fomentations as part of his practice. From Greek medical tradition comes the term iatralypte, naming a physician who healed through aromatic unctions. One celebrated Greek preparation, megaleion, was named for its creator Megallus and combined myrrh, cinnamon, and cassia. Like Egyptian kyphi, it served as both a perfume and a remedy, particularly for skin inflammation and battle wounds.

Romans

The Romans surpassed even the Greeks in their use of perfumes and aromatic oils. Three classes of perfume were in common use: ladysmata, solid unguents; stymmata, scented oils; and diapasmata, powdered perfumes. These were applied to hair, body, clothing, and bedding, and large quantities of scented oil were used for massage after bathing.

Transmission to the Arab World

With the fall of the Roman Empire and the rise of Christianity, many Roman physicians fled to Constantinople, carrying with them the books of Galen, Hippocrates, and Dioscorides. These Graeco-Roman works were translated into Persian, Arabic, and other languages, and by the close of the Byzantine Empire that knowledge had passed into the Arab world. Europe, meanwhile, entered the long stretch known as the Dark Ages.

Alchemy

Between the seventh and thirteenth centuries the Arab world produced a remarkable lineage of scientists, among them Avicenna (AD 980 to 1037). This physician and scholar wrote more than a hundred books in his lifetime, one of which was devoted entirely to the flower most beloved within Islam, the rose. He is credited with the invention of the refrigerated coil, a breakthrough in distillation that allowed the production of pure essential oils and aromatic waters.

The honour of inventing distillation, however, may belong much further back. In 1975 Dr Paolo Rovesti led an archaeological expedition to Pakistan to study the ancient Indus Valley civilisation. In the museum of Taxila, at the foot of the Himalayas, he documented a perfectly preserved distillation apparatus made of terracotta, alongside perfume containers from the same period of around 3000 BC. The presence of both items together suggests that the Arabs were not the originators of the technique but the inheritors of a process already practised more than four thousand years earlier.

Distillation Returns to Europe

Rose water, carried back to the West at the time of the Crusades along with other exotic essences, became one of the most popular scents in Europe. The technique of distillation arrived with it. By the thirteenth century the perfumes of Arabia were famous across the continent. Throughout the Middle Ages, floors were strewn with aromatic plants, and small herbal bouquets were carried as protection against plague and other infectious diseases. Lacking the gum-yielding trees of the Orient, Europeans gradually turned to their native herbs: lavender, sage, and rosemary. By the sixteenth century lavender water and the so-called chymical oils could be purchased from the apothecary. After the invention of printing, the period from 1470 to 1670 saw the publication of many herbals, among them the Grete Herball of 1526, which included illustrations of the retorts and stills used to extract volatile oils.

The Hermetic Pursuit

In the hands of the philosophers, the art of distillation became inseparable from alchemy, the hermetic pursuit dedicated to the transformation of base metals into gold and the gross into the subtle. The work was first and foremost a religious one. The stages of distillation were equated with stages of inner psychic transmutation, a process of dissolution and coagulation: separation (black, lead), extraction (white, quicksilver), fusion (red, sulphur), and finally sublimation (gold, or lapis). Just as aromatic material could be distilled to yield a pure and potent essence, the human emotions could be refined and concentrated to reveal their true nature. Within this framework, volatile oils were understood as the purified psyche, the quintessence of the alchemists, an emanation of matter and a manifestation of spirit, mediating between the two realms.

Alchemy served as the bridge across which the symbolism of the ancient Arab, Greek, and Gnostic worlds was carried into the modern era. As that symbolic language crossed the bridge, it began to be tested in continuous, dynamic interaction with the findings of chemistry.

The Birth of Aromatherapy

The term aromatherapy was first coined in 1928 by the French chemist Gattefossé, who worked in his family's perfumery business. His interest in the therapeutic potential of essential oils began with a workplace accident. After badly burning his hand, he plunged it into the nearest cool liquid, which happened to be lavender oil. The burn healed rapidly and left no scar. Through subsequent investigation he found that essential oils were often more effective in their whole, natural form than as synthetic substitutes or isolated active constituents. Cuthbert Hall had reached a similar conclusion as early as 1904, demonstrating that the antiseptic strength of eucalyptus oil was greater than that of its main isolated component, eucalyptol, also called cineol.

The French physician Dr Jean Valnet incorporated essential oils into his medical and psychiatric practice, treating specific disorders with them. His findings appeared in 1964 under the title Aromatherapie. Valnet's work was studied in turn by Madame Marguerite Maury, who applied his research to beauty therapy. Maury sought to revitalise her clients by formulating a strictly personal aromatic complex tailored to each person's temperament and particular health concerns, treating perfumed essences as medicinal agents rather than purely aesthetic ones.

Beyond Scent

The word aromatherapy can mislead, since it implies a healing approach working only through smell and emotion. In practice, each essential oil carries an individual combination of constituents that interacts directly with the body's chemistry, affecting specific organs and systems as a whole. When applied externally during massage, oils are absorbed through the skin and transported throughout the body. A simple demonstration is to rub a clove of garlic on the soles of the feet. Its volatile oil enters the bloodstream and a short time later the odour appears on the breath.

Three Modes of Action

Essential oils interact with the human body in three distinct ways.

The pharmacological mode covers the chemical changes that occur when an oil enters the bloodstream and reacts with hormones, enzymes, and other biological agents.

The physiological mode addresses how an oil affects the body's systems, whether sedating, stimulating, or otherwise altering function.

The psychological mode involves the response to a scent on inhalation, the emotional and mental reaction to odour itself.

The first two modes place aromatherapy in close company with medical herbalism, or phytotherapy. The aroma matters, but so does the chemical interaction between the oils and the body and the physical changes that result.

Herbal Medicine

The practice of aromatherapy can be understood as one branch of the larger field of herbal medicine, since an essential oil is only one of many forms in which a plant can be prepared as a remedy. Because every essential oil is derived directly from a plant, it benefits from being seen within a broader botanical context rather than treated as an isolated product. Aromatherapy and herbal medicine are not synonymous, but complementary.

Most plants that yield essential oils are also used in medical herbalism, but the therapeutic qualities of an oil must be distinguished from those of the whole herb or its other preparations.

German Chamomile

German chamomile illustrates this clearly. The herb is widely prepared as an infusion, tincture, or decoction, in addition to being processed for its volatile oil. Chamazulene, a major constituent of the oil, accounts for chamomile's longstanding reputation as a relaxant and soothing skin remedy, owing to its pain-relieving, antispasmodic, wound-healing, and anti-inflammatory activity. The essential oil is well suited to the treatment of nervous conditions, insomnia, and dermal irritation or disease. The whole herb, however, also contains a bitter principle (anthemic acid), tannins (tannic acid), mucilage, and glycosides. Its overall effect emerges from the combined action of all these pharmacologically active constituents, which in chamomile or Matricaria includes the astringency of the tannins and the stimulation of the bitters. In an infusion, tincture, or decoction the volatile oil is less concentrated, the potency of the oil is reduced, and the safety margin is increased, making the herbal preparation better suited to internal use.

Peppermint

Peppermint shows the same pattern. The oil is excellent for respiratory conditions when used as an inhalant, particularly because of its antispasmodic and antiseptic action. For longer term treatment of digestive disorders, however, extracts from the whole herb are preferable, since the volatile oil's action is supported by the presence of bitters and tannins. In herbal medicine the effect of any single herb is also typically reinforced by combining it with others.

Meadowsweet

It is also incorrect to assume that the essential oil is always the most active or therapeutically valuable part of a plant. Meadowsweet contains an essential oil of remarkable antiseptic strength: 3.3cc of meadowsweet essence renders infertile 1000cc of microbic cultures in sewage, compared to 5.6cc of phenol for the same volume. Even so, the plant also yields salicylic glycosides, valued for their pain-relieving and anti-inflammatory qualities. Aspirin, derived from salicylic acid, takes its name from the herb's old country name, spiraea.

Almond

The almond tree offers another example. The kernels of both the bitter and sweet almond trees are used to produce a fixed oil, commonly known as sweet almond oil, which has many cosmetic applications. The kernels of the bitter almond tree are also used to produce the essential oil that gives marzipan its characteristic flavour, but in unrefined form they contain cyanide. The properties of a plant, even the same part of a plant, can differ greatly depending on how it is prepared.

Therapeutic Guidelines

The accepted code of practice in modern aromatherapy is to use essential oils as external remedies only. This guidance reflects the high concentration of the oils and the irritation or damage they can cause to the mucous membranes and the delicate lining of the stomach when applied undiluted. There appears to be a kind of natural fit in this approach. Volatile oils mix readily with the carrier oils and ointments suited to external application, are absorbed easily through the skin, and vaporise readily for inhalation. When inhaled, they affect mood and feeling while at the same time producing physiological changes throughout the body. A Japanese experiment carried out in 1963 found that the effects of essential oils on the digestive system were likely to be stronger when the oils were inhaled than when they were ingested.

Herbs, by contrast, yield up many of their qualities to water and alcohol, which makes them appropriate for internal use. They lack the concentrated aromatic element, however, and so do not produce the same subtle effects on the mind and emotions.

Exceptions and Edge Cases

These are only general guidelines, and there are exceptions. Plantain is an excellent wound-healing herb valuable for external use, yet it contains no essential oil. A great many aromatic oils are also used to flavour food and drink and are consumed daily in minute amounts. Peppermint oil appears in a wide range of alcoholic and non-alcoholic beverages, confectionery, and prepared savoury foods, with the highest average use staying at or below 0.104 per cent. The mint oils, including spearmint and cornmint, are used extensively in the pharmaceutical and cosmetic industries, appearing in toothpaste, cough and cold remedies, soaps, creams, lotions, colognes, and perfumes. Cornmint also serves as the starting material for the production of menthol used in the drug industry.

A Wide and Varied Spectrum

The use of essential oils therefore covers a wide and varied spectrum. They share the holistic qualities of natural plant remedies, although some herbalists view them in much the same light as synthetic drugs, treating an essential oil as a part of the whole rather than the whole herb itself. At the same time they play an active role in modern pharmaceutical manufacture, both in their full form and as isolated constituents such as phenol and menthol.

Safety Precautions

This section provides general safety guidance for the use of essential oils. It is not a substitute for the specific safety profile of any individual oil.

Safety Data

Always consult the safety profile of a new oil before use, with particular attention to toxicity, phototoxicity, dermal irritation, and sensitisation.

Contra-indications

Note all contra-indications listed for a given oil. Common examples:

  • Fennel, hyssop, and sage should be avoided by anyone with epilepsy
  • Clary sage should not be used while drinking alcohol
  • Hops should not be used by anyone suffering from depression

High Blood Pressure

Avoid the following oils in cases of high blood pressure: hyssop, rosemary, sage (all types), and thyme.

Homoeopathy

Homoeopathic treatment is not compatible with the following oils: black pepper, camphor, eucalyptus, and the mint oils.

Pregnancy

During pregnancy, use essential oils at half the usual stated amount, and observe all oils that are contra-indicated for pregnancy.

Babies and Children

Use with care, in accordance with age.

Infants (1 to 5 years)2 to 3 drops of safe essential oils (non-toxic and non-irritant to the skin) diluted in 1 tsp of base oil for massage or bathing
Children (6 to 12 years)As for adults, but in half the stated amount
