Oil Grimoire

How Essential Oils Work

The therapeutic potential of essential oils, like other plant-derived remedies, is still being uncovered. Many medicinal herbs have been used since antiquity, and a great number have yielded the active compounds that underpin modern drugs, including quinine and cocaine, yet much of their precise pharmacology has yet to be mapped. This is especially true of aromatic oils, whose nature is concentrated and multifaceted by design. Only a small fraction of the world's flora has ever been examined for pharmacologically active compounds, and as more plant species become endangered, important sources may be lost before they are ever studied.

Modern research has largely confirmed the traditional understanding of medicinal plants, even as the language has changed. A herb such as basil, once described as a protection against evil or as good for the heart, with a scent that takes away sorrowfulness, would today be called an excellent prophylactic, nerve tonic, and antidepressant. Essential oils, like herbal remedies, can cover a wide field of activity. The same herb or oil (lemon balm being a clear example) can stimulate certain systems of the body while sedating or relaxing others. To understand how essential oils actually work, it helps to look at them through the lens of the body's major systems.

The Skin

Skin issues are often the surface expression of a deeper imbalance, whether a build-up of toxins in the blood, hormonal disruption, or nervous and emotional strain. Essential oils are well suited to this terrain because they can address such complaints on several levels at once. They dissolve readily in oil and alcohol, lend their scent to water, and so make excellent ingredients for cosmetics and everyday skincare as well as for the treatment of specific conditions. The activities listed below are particularly useful in this context.

  • Antiseptics for cuts, insect bites, and blemishes: thyme, sage, eucalyptus, tea tree, clove, lavender, lemon
  • Anti-inflammatory oils for eczema, infected wounds, bumps, and bruises: German and Roman chamomile, lavender, yarrow
  • Fungicidal oils for athlete's foot, candida, and ringworm: lavender, tea tree, myrrh, patchouli, sweet marjoram
  • Granulation-stimulating (cicatrising) agents for burns, cuts, scars, and stretch marks: lavender, chamomile, rose, neroli, frankincense, geranium
  • Deodorants for excessive perspiration and wound cleansing: bergamot, lavender, thyme, juniper, cypress, Spanish sage, lemongrass
  • Insect repellents and parasiticides for lice, fleas, scabies, ticks, mosquitos, ants, and moths: spike lavender, garlic, geranium, citronella, eucalyptus, clove, camphor, Atlas cedarwood

The Circulation, Muscles and Joints

Essential oils are absorbed easily through the skin and mucosa into the bloodstream, where they shape the character of circulation overall. Oils with a rubefacient or warming effect not only improve local blood flow but also reach the inner organs. They draw warmth and colour to the surface of the skin and can offer significant pain relief through their analgesic or numbing action. Some oils ease local inflammation by triggering mediators in the body that dilate the blood vessels, allowing blood to move more freely and reducing swelling. Others, such as hyssop, tend to balance the circulatory system as a whole, lowering blood pressure when it is too high and stimulating circulation when it is sluggish.

  • Hypotensives for high blood pressure, palpitations, and stress: sweet marjoram, ylang ylang, lavender, lemon
  • Hypertensives for poor circulation, chilblains, and listlessness: rosemary, spike lavender, eucalyptus, peppermint, thyme
  • Rubefacients for rheumatism of the joints, muscular stiffness, sciatica, and lumbago: black pepper, juniper, rosemary, camphor, sweet marjoram
  • Depurative or antitoxic agents for arthritis, gout, congestion, and skin eruptions: juniper, lemon, fennel, lovage
  • Lymphatic stimulants for cellulitis, water retention, and weight management: grapefruit, lime, fennel, lemon, mandarin, white birch
  • Circulatory tonics and astringents for swellings, inflammation, and varicose veins: cypress, yarrow, lemon

The Respiratory System

Nose, throat, and lung infections respond very well to treatment with essential oils. Inhalation is a particularly effective route. Once the oils reach the bronchi the larger part is exhaled directly through the lungs, and at the same time they prompt an increase in bronchial secretion, a protective response that benefits many respiratory ailments. Inhalation also carries the oils into the bloodstream more quickly than oral application. Most essential oils absorbed through the stomach are likewise excreted through the lungs, with only a small portion leaving through the urine.

  • Expectorants for catarrh, sinusitis, coughs, and bronchitis: eucalyptus, pine, thyme, myrrh, sandalwood, fennel
  • Antispasmodics for colic, asthma, dry cough, and whooping cough: hyssop, cypress, Atlas cedarwood, bergamot, chamomile, cajeput
  • Balsamic agents for colds, chills, and congestion: benzoin, frankincense, Tolu balsam, Peru balsam, myrrh
  • Antiseptics for flu, colds, sore throat, tonsillitis, and gingivitis: thyme, sage, eucalyptus, hyssop, pine, cajeput, tea tree, borneol

The Digestive System

External application of essential oils can influence digestive processes, even though the oils themselves are not taken orally. Herbal medicine, by contrast, draws on a wide range of remedies for stomach, gall bladder, and liver complaints, including dandelion, marshmallow, chamomile, and meadowsweet. Much of the effect of those remedies comes from the combined action of aromatic components, bitters, tannins, and mucilage, none of which travel with the volatile oil on its own. External use of essential oils on digestive issues is therefore useful but more limited in scope than internal herbal preparations.

  • Antispasmodics for spasm, pain, and indigestion: chamomile, caraway, fennel, orange, peppermint, lemon balm, aniseed, cinnamon
  • Carminatives and stomachics for flatulent dyspepsia, aerophagia, and nausea: angelica, basil, fennel, chamomile, peppermint, mandarin
  • Cholagogues for increasing the flow of bile and stimulating the gall bladder: caraway, lavender, peppermint, borneol
  • Hepatics for liver congestion and jaundice: lemon, lime, rosemary, peppermint
  • Aperitifs for loss of appetite and anorexia: aniseed, angelica, orange, ginger, garlic

The Genito-urinary and Endocrine Systems

As with the digestive system, the reproductive organs can be reached through absorption from the skin into the bloodstream, and also through hormonal influence. Rose and jasmine have a particular affinity for the reproductive system, offering a general strengthening effect as well as support for menstrual problems, genital infections, and sexual difficulties. Other oils carry plant hormones that mimic their human counterparts. Hops, sage, and fennel have been shown to contain a form of oestrogen that influences the menstrual cycle, lactation, and secondary sexual characteristics. Oestrogen also supports healthy circulation, good muscle and skin tone, and strong bones in both men and women.

Several essential oils also affect hormone secretion in other glands, including the thyroid (which governs growth and metabolism), the adrenal medulla (which manages the stress response), and the adrenal cortex (which oversees a number of processes including the production of oestrogen and androgen, the male sex hormone).

  • Antispasmodics for menstrual cramp (dysmenorrhoea) and labour pains: sweet marjoram, chamomile, clary sage, jasmine, lavender
  • Emmenagogues for scanty periods or lack of periods (amenorrhoea): chamomile, fennel, hyssop, juniper, sweet marjoram, peppermint
  • Uterine tonics and regulators for pregnancy, excess menstruation (menorrhagia), and PMT: clary sage, jasmine, rose, myrrh, frankincense, lemon balm
  • Antiseptic and bactericidal agents for leucorrhoea, vaginal pruritis, and thrush: bergamot, chamomile, myrrh, rose, tea tree
  • Galactagogues for increasing milk flow: fennel, jasmine, anise, lemongrass (sage, mint, and parsley have the opposite effect and reduce it)
  • Aphrodisiacs for impotence and frigidity: black pepper, cardamon, clary sage, neroli, jasmine, rose, sandalwood, patchouli, ylang ylang
  • Anaphrodisiacs for reducing sexual desire: sweet marjoram, camphor
  • Adrenal stimulants for anxiety and stress-related conditions: basil, geranium, rosemary, borneol, sage, pine, savory

The kidneys, bladder, and urinary system as a whole are harder to address through essential oils alone. Recent research suggests that the diuretic effects of essential oils are effectively non-existent. Traditional diuretic agents such as juniper, lovage, and parsley seed are also considered unsuitable as essential oils for internal use because of their toxicity and the risk of kidney damage. Herb teas of fennel, dandelion, or chamomile offer a gentler alternative. Bathing and the use of a douche can help control urinary infections, especially those tied to nervous or stress-related symptoms.

  • Urinary antiseptics for cystitis and urethritis: bergamot, chamomile, tea tree, sandalwood

The Immune System

Almost all essential oils carry bactericidal properties, and by encouraging the production of white blood cells they help the body resist and recover from infection. These qualities are why aromatic herbs and oils held such standing against tropical infections like malaria and typhoid, and during the plague epidemics of the Middle Ages. People who use essential oils regularly tend to maintain a higher than average resistance to illness, picking up fewer colds and recovering more quickly when they do.

  • Bactericidal and antiviral agents (prophylactics) for protection against colds and flu: tea tree, cajeput, niaouli, basil, lavender, eucalyptus, bergamot, camphor, clove, rosemary
  • Febrifuges for reducing fever and temperature: angelica, basil, peppermint, thyme, sage, lemon, eucalyptus, tea tree
  • Sudorifics and diaphoretics for promoting sweating and eliminating toxins: rosemary, thyme, hyssop, chamomile

The Nervous System

Recent research aligns closely with the traditional view of how essential oils act on the nervous system. Chamomile, bergamot, sandalwood, lavender, and sweet marjoram have been shown to sedate the central nervous system, while jasmine, peppermint, basil, clove, and ylang ylang stimulate it. A few results run counter to popular assumption: neroli proved stimulating and lemon proved sedative. Some oils are adaptogens, meaning they have a balancing or normalising effect on the body's systems. Geranium and rosewood, for example, can be sedative or stimulating depending on the situation and the individual.

Words like relaxing and uplifting often describe an odour and emotional response rather than a physiological effect, although the two are connected. Bergamot, lemon balm, and lemon can sedate the nervous system while reviving the spirit. Jasmine, ylang ylang, and neroli can stimulate the nerves and yet feel soothing and relaxing on a more subtle emotional level.

  • Sedatives for nervous tension, stress, and insomnia: chamomile, bergamot, sandalwood, lavender, sweet marjoram, lemon balm, hops, valerian, lemon
  • Stimulants for convalescence, lack of strength, and nervous fatigue: basil, jasmine, peppermint, ylang ylang, neroli, angelica, rosemary
  • Nerve tonics (nervines) for strengthening the nervous system as a whole: chamomile, clary sage, juniper, lavender, marjoram, rosemary

The Mind

The mind is perhaps the most discussed and least understood territory of essential oil work. There is no question that aromatic oils have been used throughout history to influence emotion and state of mind, and this is the basis for their long employment as incense in religious and ritual settings. Two olfactory nerve tracts run directly into the limbic system, the part of the brain concerned with memory and emotion, which is why scents can produce an immediate and powerful response that resists rational analysis.

Research at Warwick University in England and Toho University in Japan has set out to place these traditional understandings within a scientific frame. The findings describe two kinds of reaction to odours, called hard-wired and soft-wired responses. The hard-wired response is ingrained from before birth and operates purely on instinct, such as a baby's recognition of the scent of its mother's skin, or a sexual signal between adults. The soft-wired response is learned or acquired later, such as the fragrance of honeysuckle recalling a particular childhood garden.

This raises a real question. To what extent does the effect of an oil rest on its chemistry and physiology, and to what extent does it depend on belief and association? Once the conversation moves into psychological and emotional response, neat classification becomes much harder, and it makes more sense to weigh the temperament of the individual within a given context than to predict a fixed reaction.

The conclusion drawn at the 1991 Psychology of Perfumery Conference was that pharmacological effects tend to be similar from one person to the next, while psychological effects are bound to differ. The effect of an odour on a person depends on a number of factors.

  1. How the odour was applied
  2. How much was applied
  3. The circumstances in which it was applied
  4. The person to whom it was applied, including age, sex, and personality
  5. The mood they were in to begin with
  6. Any prior associations they hold with the odour
  7. Anosmia, or the inability to smell certain scents

The aim, then, is to seek out odoriferous substances that have an affinity with the person being treated, that compensate for what is lacking, and that allow particular faculties to flourish. This is the principle behind the so-called individual prescription, a blend tailored on every point to the identity of the person it serves.

Once individual need enters the picture, essential oils begin to show the full range of their nature. The rose is a clear example. In folklore and religious writing, especially within the Sufi tradition, the rose is associated with beauty, love, and spiritual depth, and it has an equally long history in physical care, used for skin problems, regulating the female cycle, supporting circulation, purifying the blood, and as a heart tonic. When the fragrance reaches the nose it carries all of these associations with it, working on mind and body at once, with the precise effect shaped by personal experience.

The dominant trend in modern thought is strictly dualistic, treating psychic and somatic events as mutually exclusive rather than inseparable. Trying to disentangle spirit from matter leads nowhere. Mind and matter are better understood as enfolded projections of a higher reality that is neither matter nor consciousness alone.
