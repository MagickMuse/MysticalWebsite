Hops Essential Oil is steam-distilled from the dried cones (strobiles) of Humulus lupulus, the climbing perennial whose volatile oil "lupulin" forms within the glandular hairs of the cones. The oil is best known as a sedative and nerve remedy, with strong oestrogenic and soporific qualities; it carries the same heavy aromatic principle responsible for the relaxant effect of the traditional hop pillow.

⚠️
Important safety notice: Hops oil is narcotic in excessive amounts and may cause sensitisation in some individuals. It must be avoided by those suffering from depression. Avoid during pregnancy due to its oestrogenic action.

Botanical Information

  • Scientific name: Humulus lupulus
  • Family: Moraceae
  • Synonyms: Common hop, European hop, lupulus
  • Plant: Perennial creeping, twining herb growing up to eight metres high. Bears male and female flowers on separate plants. Dark green, heart-shaped leaves and greeny-yellow flowers.
  • Source of oil: The dried cones, also called catkins or "strobiles." Lupulin, a volatile oil, forms in the glandular hairs of these cones.
  • Evaporation: Middle to base note (rich, spicy-sweet aroma)

Distribution

Native to Europe and North America. Cultivated worldwide, especially in the USA (California and Washington), Yugoslavia, and Germany. The essential oil is mainly produced in France, the UK, and Germany.

Other Species and Relations

Humulus lupulus is botanically related to the common stinging nettle (Urtica dioica) and to the fig (Ficus carica).

Extraction & Characteristics

Method: Steam distillation from the dried cones or catkins ("strobiles"). An absolute is also produced by solvent extraction for perfumery use.

Appearance and scent: A pale yellow to reddish-amber liquid carrying a rich, spicy-sweet odour.

Principal Constituents

Mainly humulene, myrcene, caryophyllene, and farnesene, alongside more than one hundred other trace components.

Safety

Generally non-toxic and non-irritant in normal aromatic dilution. Narcotic in excessive amounts. May cause sensitisation in some individuals. Should be avoided by those suffering from depression.

Therapeutic Actions

Anodyne, aphrodisiac, antimicrobial, antiseptic, antispasmodic, astringent, bactericidal, carminative, diuretic, emollient, hypnotic, nervine, sedative, soporific, and possessing oestrogenic properties.

Folk and Historical Use

Best known as a nerve remedy for insomnia, nervous tension, and neuralgia, and also for sexual neurosis in both sexes. The oil supports the female oestrogens and is useful for amenorrhoea (heavy periods). The hop pillow, whose heavy aromatic odour has been shown to relax through direct action at the olfactory centres, is the classic delivery method, and the volatile aromatic component appears to be the most active fraction. Hops have additionally been used for heart disease and for stomach and liver complaints including bacterial dysentery. In China the herb is used for pulmonary tuberculosis and cystitis. The plant is the defining bittering and flavouring agent in beer, and is current in the British Herbal Pharmacopoeia as a specific for restlessness with nervous headaches and indigestion.

Aromatherapy Uses

  • Skin care: dermatitis, rashes, rough skin, ulcers.
  • Respiratory system: asthma, spasmodic cough.
  • Digestive system: indigestion, nervous dyspepsia.
  • Genito-urinary and endocrine systems: amenorrhoea, menstrual cramp, support of female oestrogens, promotion of feminine characteristics, reduction of sexual overactivity.
  • Nervous system: headaches, insomnia, nervous tension, neuralgia, stress-related conditions.

Blending Partners

Pine Essential Oil, Hyacinth Absolute, Nutmeg Essential Oil, Copaiba Balsam Essential Oil, plus citrus oils such as Bergamot Essential Oil and Lemon Essential Oil, and spice oils such as Cardamom Essential Oil and Black Pepper Essential Oil.

Other Uses

Employed as a fragrance ingredient in perfumes, especially spicy or oriental compositions. Hops oil also appears in flavour work for tobacco, sauces, and spice products, but its most extensive flavouring use is in alcoholic drinks, especially beer.
