Violet Leaf Absolute is solvent-extracted from the fresh leaves of Violet. The result is an intense dark green viscous liquid with a strong green-leaf odour and a delicate floral undertone, prized in fine perfumery and for skin and respiratory work.

Other Names & Related Species

Known as English violet, garden violet, blue violet, and sweet-scented violet. There are over 200 species of violet; the main types cultivated for aromatic extraction are the Parma and Victoria violets. Not to be confused with the unrelated African Violet, which belongs to a different family entirely. Pansy is a related Viola.

Origin & Distribution

Native to Europe and parts of Asia, and cultivated in gardens worldwide. The aromatic crop is mainly grown in southern France around Grasse, and to a lesser extent in Italy and China, for perfumery use. The plant itself is small and tender, with dark green heart-shaped leaves, fragrant violet-blue flowers, and an oblique underground rhizome.

Folk Tradition

Both the leaf and flowers have a long tradition in herbal medicine, mainly for congestive pulmonary conditions and sensitive skin conditions, including capillary fragility. The leaf has also been used to treat cystitis and as a mouthwash for infections of the mouth and throat. It is reported to have mild pain-killing properties, probably due to the presence of salicylic acid, as in aspirin. The flowers are still used to make a syrup of violet that serves as a laxative and colouring agent. The dried leaf and flowers are current in the British Herbal Pharmacopoeia as a specific for eczema and skin eruptions with serous exudate, particularly when associated with rheumatic symptoms.

Extraction & Characteristics

A concrete and absolute are produced by solvent extraction from the fresh leaves. The leaf absolute is an intense dark green viscous liquid with a strong green-leaf odour and a delicate floral undertone. Both leaves and petals contain nonadienal, parmone, hexyl alcohol, benzyl alcohol, ionone, and viola quercitin, among others. Reported actions include mild analgesic, anti-inflammatory, antirheumatic, antiseptic, liver decongestant, diuretic, expectorant, laxative, soporific, and circulatory stimulant.

Therapeutic Uses

  • Skin care: acne, eczema, refines the pores, thread veins, wounds.
  • Circulation, muscles, and joints: fibrosis, poor circulation, rheumatism.
  • Respiratory system: bronchitis, catarrh, mouth and throat infections.
  • Nervous system: dizziness, headaches, insomnia, nervous exhaustion. The scent was traditionally believed to comfort and strengthen the heart.

Blending

Violet leaf blends well with Tuberose Oil, Clary Sage Essential Oil, Boronia Absolute, Tarragon Essential Oil, Cumin Essential Oil, Hops Essential Oil, Basil Essential Oil, Hyacinth Absolute, and other florals.

Safety

Non-toxic and non-irritant, but possible sensitization in some individuals. Patch test before topical use, and avoid on broken or sensitive skin.

Where You Will Encounter It

Used in high-class perfumery work, and occasionally in flavouring, mainly confectionery.

Therapeutic Applications

  • Headache: Apply via massage, compress, or vaporization.
  • Nervous Exhaustion: Apply via massage, bath, or vaporization.
  • Nervous Tension and Stress: Apply via massage, bath, or vaporization.
  • Acne: Apply via massage, skin oil/lotion, flower water, bath, inhalation (steam), or neat application.
  • Dry & Sensitive Skin: Apply via massage, skin oil/lotion, flower water, or bath.
  • Vertigo: Apply via vaporization or steam inhalation.
  • Sore Throat: Apply via vaporization or steam inhalation.
  • Insomnia: Apply via massage, bath, or vaporization.

Traditional Grimoire Notes

Said to be sacred to the Fairy Queen. Deeply healing when added to ritual baths. A protective oil that wards off evil, promotes good health, and brings peace — particularly helpful in marital difficulties. Can encourage reunions and dissolve the barriers of indifference. Also draws wealth and good fortune.
