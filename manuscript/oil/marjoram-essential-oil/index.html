Botanical Source

Sweet marjoram (Origanum majorana) of the Lamiaceae family, also referenced as Marjorana hortensis or knotted marjoram. The plant is a tender bushy perennial (grown as an annual in colder climates), reaching about 60 cm with a hairy stem, dark green oval leaves, and small greyish-white flowers gathered into clusters or "knots." The whole plant is strongly aromatic.

Distribution

Native to the Mediterranean region, Egypt, and North Africa. Major oil-producing countries include France, Tunisia, Morocco, Egypt, Bulgaria, Hungary, and Germany.

Other Species

Considerable taxonomic confusion exists across the marjorams and oreganos. The most common related types are pot or French marjoram (Origanum onites, also Marjorana onites), a hardier and more spreading plant; Spanish marjoram or oregano (Thymus mastichina); and wild or common marjoram (Origanum vulgare), the source of so-called "oregano oil." See separate entries for common oregano and Spanish oregano.

Folk and Historical Use

A traditional culinary herb and household folk remedy. The ancient Greeks worked marjoram into fragrances, cosmetics, and medicines; the name oregano descends from a Greek phrase meaning "joy of the mountains." Marjoram carries a soothing, fortifying, and warming character and has been called upon for digestive and menstrual problems as well as nervous and respiratory complaints. Older herbal authorities describe it as "comforting in cold diseases of the head, stomach, sinews and other parts, taken inwardly or outwardly applied... helps diseases of the chest, obstructions of the liver and spleen." It also serves muscular and rheumatic pain, sprains, strains, stiff joints, and bruises.

Therapeutic Actions

Analgesic, anaphrodisiac, anti-oxidant, antiseptic, antispasmodic, antiviral, bactericidal, carminative, cephalic, cordial, diaphoretic, digestive, diuretic, emmenagogue, expectorant, fungicidal, hypotensive, laxative, nervine, sedative, stomachic, tonic, vasodilator, and vulnerary.

Extraction & Characteristics

The essential oil is steam-distilled from the dried flowering herb, with an oleoresin produced in smaller quantities. The oil presents as a pale yellow or amber-coloured mobile liquid carrying a warm, woody, spicy-camphoraceous odour.

Principal Constituents

Terpinenes, terpineol, sabinenes, linalol, carvacrol, linalyl acetate, ocimene, cadinene, geranyl acetate, citral, and eugenol, among others.

Safety

Non-toxic, non-irritant, and non-sensitizing. Not to be used during pregnancy.

Aromatherapy Uses

For skin care, marjoram is useful for chilblains, bruises, and ticks. For circulation, muscles, and joints it addresses arthritis, lumbago, muscular aches and stiffness, rheumatism, sprains, and strains. For the respiratory system it supports asthma, bronchitis, and coughs. For the digestive system it eases colic, constipation, dyspepsia, and flatulence. For the genito-urinary system it is used for amenorrhoea, dysmenorrhoea, leucorrhoea, and PMT. For the immune system it supports recovery from colds. For the nervous system it addresses headache, hypertension, insomnia, migraine, nervous tension, and stress-related conditions.

Other Uses

Fragrance component in soaps, detergents, cosmetics, and perfumery. Employed across most food categories, especially meats, seasonings, and sauces, as well as soft drinks and alcoholic beverages such as vermouths and bitters.
