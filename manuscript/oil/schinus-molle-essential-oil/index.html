Schinus molle yields a warm, woody-peppery essential oil that has long served as a substitute for black pepper in flavouring and perfumery work.

Other Names & Related Species

Known as Peruvian pepper, Peruvian mastic, and Californian pepper tree. Closely related to the mastic tree (Mastic).

Origin & Distribution

Native to South America. The tree grows wild in Mexico, Peru, Guatemala, and other tropical regions, including California, and has been introduced into North and South Africa and the Mediterranean. The fruits are collected for essential oil production in Spain, Guatemala, and Mexico.

Folk Tradition

In Greece and other Mediterranean countries, an intoxicating beverage is made from the fruits of the tree. The fruit is also used as a substitute for black pepper in the regions where it grows. During World War II, when black pepper oil was unavailable, schinus molle replaced it.

Extraction & Characteristics

The essential oil is produced by steam distillation from the fruit or berries. A small quantity of oil is also produced from the leaves. The liquid is pale green or olive and oily, with a warm, woody-peppery scent and a smoky undertone. Principal constituents are phellandrene, caryophyllene, pinene, and carvacrol. Reported actions include antiseptic, antiviral, bactericidal, carminative, stimulant, and stomachic.

Therapeutic Uses

Applications follow those of Black Pepper Essential Oil, for which schinus molle has historically substituted.

Blending

Schinus molle blends well with oakmoss, clove, nutmeg, cinnamon, black pepper, and eucalyptus.

Safety

Non-toxic, non-irritant, and non-sensitizing.

Where You Will Encounter It

Used as a substitute for black pepper in perfumery and flavouring work.
