Botanical Information

Zingiber officinale of the Zingiberaceae family, originating from China. The essential oil is extracted from the roots and is classified as a base note.

Warnings

Use caution when taking anticoagulant drugs like aspirin and warfarin. Possible skin sensitizer. Avoid burning ginger root indoors, as it can cause respiratory irritation.

Ginger helps regulate muscle contractions and soothes irritations in muscle fibers, and herbalists and doctors have prescribed ginger capsules for menstrual difficulties for this reason.

Since ginger is a thick, strong root with a particularly strong smell, this essential oil is well suited for spells involving magickal and physical strength and courage. True hexes are rarer than folks think, but the jealousy of the evil eye and small jinxes are common, and ginger easily dispatches these petty attacks.

Botanical Source Detail

Zingiber officinale is an erect perennial herb of the Zingiberaceae family, growing up to one metre high. The plant produces a thick, spreading, tuberous rhizome with a very pungent character, sending up a green reed-like stalk each year that bears narrow spear-shaped leaves and white or yellow flowers on a spike rising directly from the root. Also known as common ginger and Jamaica ginger.

Distribution

Native to southern Asia and extensively cultivated throughout the tropics, including Nigeria, the West Indies, India, China, Jamaica, and Japan. Most of the oil is distilled in the United Kingdom, China, and India.

Other Species

Several regional varieties of Z. officinale are all used for oil production, with slight variations in their constituents; the African oil, for example, is generally darker than other types. Galangal (Alpinia officinarum), another member of the Zingiberaceae family, is also known as ginger root or Chinese ginger.

Folk Medicine

Ginger has served as a domestic spice and remedy for thousands of years, especially in the East. Fresh ginger is used in China for rheumatism, bacterial dysentery, toothache, malaria, and for cold and moist conditions such as excess mucus and diarrhoea. In the West it is best known as a digestive aid; the British Herbal Pharmacopoeia specifically indicates it for flatulent intestinal colic. Preserved and crystallised ginger remains a popular sweet across both East and West.

Therapeutic Actions

Analgesic, anti-oxidant, antiseptic, antispasmodic, antitussive, aperitif, aphrodisiac, bactericidal, carminative, cephalic, diaphoretic, expectorant, febrifuge, laxative, rubefacient, stimulant, stomachic, and tonic.

Extraction & Characteristics

Steam distillation from the unpeeled, dried, ground root; an absolute and an oleoresin are also produced for use in perfumery. The resulting liquid is pale yellow, amber, or greenish, carrying a warm, slightly green, fresh, woody-spicy aroma.

Principal Constituents

Gingerin, gingenol, gingerone, zingiberine, linalol, camphene, phellandrene, citral, cineol, borneol, and other minor components.

Safety

Non-toxic, non-irritant (except in high concentration), slightly phototoxic, and may cause sensitisation in some individuals.

Aromatherapy Uses

For circulation, muscles, and joints, ginger oil addresses arthritis, fatigue, muscular aches and pains, poor circulation, rheumatism, sprains, and strains. For the respiratory system it is used for catarrh, congestion, coughs, sinusitis, and sore throat. For the digestive system it addresses diarrhoea, colic, cramp, flatulence, indigestion, loss of appetite, nausea, and travel sickness. For the immune system it is useful for chills, colds, flu, fever, and infectious disease. For the nervous system it addresses debility and nervous exhaustion.

Blending Partners

Sandalwood Essential Oil, Vetivert Essential Oil, Patchouli Essential Oil, Frankincense Essential Oil, Rosewood Essential Oil, Atlas Cedarwood Essential Oil, Coriander Seed Essential Oil, Rose Oil, Lime Essential Oil, Neroli Essential Oil, Orange Essential Oil, and other citrus oils.

Other Uses

The oleoresin appears in digestive, carminative, and laxative preparations. Ginger is widely used as a fragrance component in cosmetics and perfumes, especially oriental and men's fragrances, and is extensively employed across all major food categories, in alcoholic and soft drinks.
