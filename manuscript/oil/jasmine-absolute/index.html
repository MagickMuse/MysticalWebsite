Botanical Information

Jasminum officinale from the Oleaceae family, extracted from flowers through solvent extraction. Originates from India, Morocco, France, and Italy. Middle note with average absorption. Labor-intensive harvest requires 3,600,000 hand-picked night-blooming flowers per pound of absolute.

Scent Profile

Heady, thick floral with rich, sweet character. Relieves anxiety and improves concentration.

Herbal Lore

One of the most recognized florals in aromatic practice. Pale blossoms open at night, picked by hand to flavor tea and rice. Jasmine green tea preserves the delicate taste through drying leaves in night-blooming jasmine gardens. Traditional use in love spells and "Come to Me" blends spans centuries. The night-blooming habit connects jasmine to moon goddesses, darkness, and nocturnal energies while preserving fragrant oils from daytime heat.

Due to extraordinary cost (nearly $5,000 per pound), jasmine became associated with wealth, royalty, and clergy. This history makes it powerful for prosperity work and connecting with lunar goddesses. Companies often dilute this precious oil; ethical sellers clearly label "jasmine absolute in jojoba." Dark glass bottles protect quality, as UV light degrades oils rapidly.

Botanical Source Detail

  • Scientific name: Jasminum officinale
  • Family: Oleaceae
  • Synonyms: Jasmin, jessamine, common jasmine, poet's jessamine
  • Plant: An evergreen shrub or vine reaching up to ten metres in height, bearing delicate bright green leaves and star-shaped, intensely fragrant white flowers.
  • Source of oil: The flowers themselves, processed by solvent extraction.
  • Evaporation: Base note (intensely rich, warm floral with tealike undertone)

Distribution

Native to China, northern India, and west Asia. Cultivated through the Mediterranean region, China, and India depending on the exact species. The concrete is produced in Italy, France, Morocco, Egypt, China, Japan, Algeria, and Turkey; the absolute is mainly produced in France.

Other Species

Many species of jasmine are used in medicine and perfumery work. Aside from the common jasmine, the most widespread varieties are the royal or Italian jasmine (J. grandiflorum), grown in the Mediterranean region, and its Eastern counterpart J. officinale var. grandiflorum or J. auriculatum. J. sambac yields a distinct oil and is the variety dried for jasmine tea.

Extraction & Characteristics

Method: A concrete is produced by solvent extraction from the flowers. The absolute is then obtained from the concrete by separation with alcohol. An essential oil can also be produced by steam distillation of the absolute.

Appearance and scent: A dark orange-brown viscous liquid carrying an intensely rich, warm floral scent with a tealike undertone. The absolute rounds off rough notes and blends with virtually anything.

Principal Constituents

More than one hundred constituents have been identified, including benzyl acetate, linalol, phenylacetic acid, benzyl alcohol, farnesol, methyl anthranilate, cis-jasmone, and methyl jasmonate, among many others.

Safety

Non-toxic, non-irritant, and generally non-sensitising; allergic reactions have been known to occur in some individuals. Avoid during pregnancy due to its parturient, galactagogue, and uterine-tonic actions, and reserve for use during labour itself only with care-team guidance.

Therapeutic Actions

Mild analgesic, antidepressant, anti-inflammatory, antiseptic, antispasmodic, aphrodisiac, carminative, cicatrisant, expectorant, galactagogue, parturient, sedative, and uterine tonic.

Folk and Historical Use

In China the flowers of J. officinale var. grandiflorum are used to treat hepatitis, liver cirrhosis, and dysentery, while the flowers of J. sambac address conjunctivitis, dysentery, skin ulcers, and tumours. The root is used for headaches, insomnia, dislocation pain, and rheumatism. In Western herbal tradition the common jasmine was held to warm the womb and facilitate birth, to ease cough and difficulty of breathing, to disperse crude humours, and to suit cold and catarrhous constitutions but not hot ones. It was also used for hard, contracted limbs and for problems of the nervous and reproductive systems.

Aromatherapy Uses

  • Skin care: dry, greasy, irritated, and sensitive skin.
  • Circulation, muscles, and joints: muscular spasm and sprains.
  • Respiratory system: catarrh, coughs, hoarseness, and laryngitis.
  • Genito-urinary system: dysmenorrhoea, frigidity, labour pains, and uterine disorders.
  • Nervous system: depression, nervous exhaustion, and stress-related conditions. Jasmine produces a feeling of optimism, confidence, and euphoria; it is most useful where there is apathy, indifference, or listlessness.

Source-Named Blending Partners

Rose Oil, Sandalwood Essential Oil, Clary Sage Essential Oil, plus all citrus oils, including Bergamot Essential Oil, Lemon Essential Oil, Lime Essential Oil, Orange Essential Oil, Neroli Essential Oil, and Grapefruit Essential Oil.

Other Uses

Used extensively in soaps, toiletries, cosmetics, and perfumes, especially high-class floral and oriental compositions. The oil and absolute also appear in a wide range of food products and in alcoholic and soft drinks. The dried flowers of J. sambac are used in jasmine tea.
