Testing Essential Oils at Home

Knowing what you're working with matters. Before incorporating an essential oil into your magickal practice, it's worth verifying its purity. Adulterated oils may still carry some energetic properties, but watered-down or chemically altered products won't perform as expected in spellwork or blending.

These two simple at-home tests can reveal whether your essential oils have been diluted, watered down, or treated with additives.

Method 1: The Paper Test

You'll Need: A watercolor paper notebook (available at craft or art supply stores). The thick, absorbent pages prevent oil from bleeding through to subsequent sheets.

Steps

  1. Draw a small shape (circle, square, etc.) to mark your testing area
  2. Record all label information: brand name, botanical (Latin) name, common name, distillation method, lot numbers, batch codes, and any other details
  3. Place a single drop of oil within your marked shape
  4. Allow the oil to air dry naturally without fans or other assistance
  5. Check the paper at one hour, several hours, and again at twenty-four hours
  6. By the final check, the oil should have largely evaporated
  7. Document your observations: Do you see water spots, oily residue, or color stains?

Interpreting Results

Scent: After twenty-four hours, minimal fragrance should remain. Deeper base notes (like vetiver) will linger longer than bright top notes (like orange), but the scent should be faint.

Color: Results vary by source material and extraction method:

  • Distilled citrus oils are typically colorless
  • Expressed citrus oils may leave colored residue (orange oil will stain orange)
  • Root-derived oils like vetiver can be naturally dark
  • Grass-derived oils like lemongrass should be colorless despite the green plant material
⚠️
Red Flag: If your lemongrass or other naturally colorless oil leaves a colored stain, the company has added dyes for aesthetic purposes.

Water spots (tear-stained appearance): The oil has been diluted with water. There's no ethical justification for this practice.

Greasy residue (french-fry-bag appearance): The oil has been cut with carrier oil. Reputable companies disclose dilution and specify which carrier was used, both for allergy safety and budget transparency.

💡
Why Dilution Happens: Some essential oils are extraordinarily expensive due to the time and resources required to produce them. Frankincense, for instance, comes from tree sap that takes roughly twenty years before the tree begins producing. This makes pure frankincense oil cost hundreds of dollars per milliliter. Companies often dilute such oils with carriers like Jojoba Oil, Coconut Oil, or sunflower oil to make them accessible. Ethical vendors clearly state this on the label.

Method 2: The Water Test

You'll Need: A clear container with a lid (a plastic water bottle works well)

Steps

  1. Add approximately 2 oz. of water to the bottle
  2. Add one drop of the essential oil you're testing
  3. Secure the lid tightly and shake vigorously
  4. Observe the contents

Interpreting Results

Cloudy liquid: The oil has been treated with an emulsifier, allowing water to be mixed in.

Oil coating the interior walls: The essential oil has been diluted with carrier oil, whether for cost reduction, profit margins, or other reasons.

Final Thoughts

These tests won't reveal every form of adulteration, but they catch the most common issues. When sourcing oils for magickal work, building relationships with transparent vendors pays dividends. Quality oils carry stronger, cleaner energy and perform more reliably in your practice.
