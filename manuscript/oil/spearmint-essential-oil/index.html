Botanical Source

Spearmint (Mentha spicata) of the Lamiaceae family, also classified as M. viridis. The plant is a hardy branched perennial herb with bright green, lance-shaped, sharply toothed leaves, quickly spreading underground runners, and pink or lilac-coloured flowers gathered into slender cylindrical spikes.

Distribution

Native to the Mediterranean region and now common across Europe, western Asia, and the Middle East. Introduced to the USA, where it has become a very popular flavouring. Oil production centres on the midwest USA, Hungary, Spain, the former Yugoslavia, the former USSR, and China.

Other Species

Several spearmint types are recognised, particularly in the USA, including curly mint (M. spicata var. crispa). In Russia the oil from M. verticellata is also traded as spearmint oil.

Folk and Historical Use

Valued worldwide as a culinary herb, a fact reflected in its many folk names. The ancient Greeks employed it as a restorative and to scent their bathwater. Distilled spearmint water is used to ease hiccough, colic, nausea, indigestion, and flatulence. Older herbal authorities describe it: "Applied to the forehead and temples, it eases the pains in the head, and is good to wash the heads of young children with, against all manner of breakings out, sores or scabs... being smelled unto, it is comforting to the head."

Therapeutic Actions

Local anaesthetic, antiseptic, antispasmodic, astringent, carminative, cephalic, cholagogue, decongestant, digestive, diuretic, expectorant, febrifuge, hepatic, nervine, stimulant, stomachic, and tonic.

Extraction & Characteristics

The essential oil is steam-distilled from the flowering tops. It presents as a pale yellow or olive mobile liquid carrying a warm, spicy-herbaceous, minty odour.

Principal Constituents

L-carvone dominates at 50 to 70 percent, alongside dihydrocarvone, phellandrene, limonene, menthone, menthol, pulegone, cineol, linalol, and pinenes, among others.

Safety

Non-toxic, non-irritant, and non-sensitizing.

Aromatherapy Uses

The oil's profile resembles peppermint but acts more gently, making it better suited to children's complaints.

Skin care: Acne, dermatitis, and congested skin.

Respiratory system: Asthma, bronchitis, catarrhal conditions, and sinusitis.

Digestive system: Colic, dyspepsia, flatulence, hepatobiliary disorders, nausea, and vomiting.

Immune system: Colds, fevers, and flu.

Nervous system: Fatigue, headache, migraine, nervous strain, neurasthenia, and stress-related conditions.

Blending Partners

Lavender Essential Oil, Lavandin Essential Oil, Jasmine Absolute, Eucalyptus Essential Oil, Basil Essential Oil, and Rosemary Essential Oil. Frequently combined with Peppermint Essential Oil.

Other Uses

A fragrance component in soaps and colognes. Used primarily as a flavour ingredient across a wide range of products, including toothpaste, chewing gum, sweets, alcoholic drinks, and soft drinks.
