Botanical Source

Onion (Allium cepa) of the Liliaceae family, also called common onion or Strasburg onion. The plant is a perennial or biennial herb growing up to 1.2 metres high, carrying hollow leaves and flowering stems above a globelike fleshy bulb.

Distribution

Native to western Asia and the Middle East, with a long history of cultivation around the world, primarily for culinary use. The essential oil is mainly produced in France, Germany, and Egypt from the "red" onion.

Other Species

Many cultivated forms of onion exist, including the Spanish or silver-skinned onion, the Tripoli, and the red onion.

Folk and Historical Use

Onion holds an ancient reputation as a curative agent, praised by the schools of Galen and Hippocrates. It is high in vitamins A, B, and C and shares many properties with garlic, its close relative. Raw onion is credited with warding off colds and infections, supporting strong bones, promoting tissue circulation, and acting as a blood cleanser; combined with its sulphur content, this is said to keep the skin clear and well-conditioned.

A solid reputation also follows it for correcting glandular imbalance and weight problems and for improving lymphatic drainage often implicated in oedema and puffiness.

Long used as a home "simple" across many conditions: as a poultice for the removal of hard tumours, for relief in suppressed gout or obstructed circulation, to soothe the nerves and induce sleep, to stimulate the action of the skin and clear obstructions of the viscera, applied bruised to burns and scalds when no other remedy was at hand, and laid on the sting or bite of poisonous insects.

Therapeutic Actions

Anthelmintic, antimicrobial, antirheumatic, antiseptic, antisclerotic, antispasmodic, antiviral, bactericidal, carminative, depurative, digestive, diuretic, expectorant, fungicidal, hypocholesterolaemic, hypoglycaemic, hypotensive, stomachic, tonic, and vermifuge.

Extraction & Characteristics

The essential oil is steam-distilled from the bulb. (An oleoresin is also produced in small quantities for flavouring use.) The oil presents as a pale yellow or brownish-yellow mobile liquid carrying a strong, unpleasant, sulphuraceous odour with a lachrymatory (tear-producing) effect.

Principal Constituents

Mainly dipropyl disulphide, with methylpropyl disulphide, dipropyl trisulphide, methylpropyl trisulphide, and allylpropyl disulphide, among others.

Safety

⚠️
Specific safety data is unavailable at present. The profile is presumed similar to garlic: generally non-toxic, non-irritant, with possible sensitization.

Aromatherapy Uses

None. The oil is not employed in aromatherapy or home use due to its offensive odour.

Other Uses

Featured in some pharmaceutical preparations for colds and coughs. The oil is used extensively across major food categories, especially meats, savouries, salad dressings, alcoholic drinks, and soft drinks. It is not used in perfumery work.

Related Oils

Closely related to Garlic Essential Oil; the two oils share many therapeutic properties.
