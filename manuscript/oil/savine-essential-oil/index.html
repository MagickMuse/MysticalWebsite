Savine

Savine essential oil is highly toxic and is not for home aromatherapy, internal use, or external use. It is included here for botanical and historical reference only.

Other Names & Related Species

Known as savin, with the botanical synonym Sabina cacumina. Savine is closely related to common juniper (Juniper) and other members of the Cupressaceae family.

Origin & Distribution

Native to North America and middle and southern Europe. The oil is produced mainly in Austria (the Tirol), with smaller production in France and the western Balkans.

Folk Tradition

Savine was once used as an ointment or dressing for blisters to promote discharge, and for syphilitic warts and other skin problems. It is rarely administered today because of its possible toxic effects.

Extraction & Characteristics

The essential oil is produced by steam distillation from the twigs and leaves. The liquid is pale yellow or olive and oily, with a disagreeable, bitter, turpentine-like odour. Principal constituents include sabinol, sabinyl acetate, terpinene, pinene, sabinene, decyl aldehyde, citronellol, geraniol, cadinene, and dihydrocuminyl alcohol. Reported actions include powerful emmenagogue, rubefacient, and stimulant, but the oil is not safe to apply for any of these.

Safety

Do not use savine essential oil in home aromatherapy, internally or externally. The oil is an oral toxin, a dermal irritant, and an abortifacient. It acts as a nerve poison and a blood circulation stimulant, and it is banned from sale to the public in many countries. Pregnant and nursing people, children, and anyone with sensitive skin or compromised health must avoid it entirely.

Where You Will Encounter It

Occasional perfumery use only. Little employed today.
