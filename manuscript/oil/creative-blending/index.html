Oil Grimoire

Creative Blending

Blending essential oils sits between two impulses: the medical and the aesthetic. In practice these are not separate categories but two ends of the same scale. At one end is therapeutic action on a physical condition like backache. At the other is the emotional or aesthetic response to a particular scent. The person nursing lumbago also carries a temperament and an emotional state, conscious or not, that responds in its own subtle way to a chosen blend. The reverse is also true: a personal perfume that captures someone's character through fragrance has a generally remedial effect on the whole person.

This is why fragrance is worth keeping in mind even when blending principally for medicinal effect. A remedy that smells lovely to the person using it is simply more pleasing to apply, and certain combinations clash for personal reasons (a heavily floral blend, for instance, often does not appeal to men). Choosing and combining oils remains a matter of personal taste, but the guidelines below cover the most useful ground.

Correct Proportions

For therapeutic use, essential oils are diluted before application to the skin. To make a massage or body oil, mix the chosen essential oil or oils into a light base such as grapeseed or sweet almond oil. The dilution table for common blend sizes lives in How to Use Essential Oils at Home. Other suitable bases include sunflower, hazelnut, safflower, peanut, soya, and corn oil. Mineral oils are best avoided.

Heavier, more nourishing oils can be added in small amounts (up to about 10 per cent) when treating specific conditions. These include jojoba, avocado, peach or apricot kernel, borage, olive, sesame, evening primrose, and infused oils such as calendula or St John's wort. A small quantity of wheatgerm oil, around 5 per cent, helps preserve the blend.

The essential oil content in a finished blend usually sits between 1 and 3 per cent, depending on what is being addressed. Physical complaints generally call for a stronger concentration than emotional or nervous conditions. A few oils, the high-quality florals such as rose and jasmine in particular, have unusually strong diffusive power, so a very small percentage is enough to have real effect or to set the character of an entire blend.

Synergies

The proportions of each oil in a blend matter as much as the oils themselves. Some oils, when combined, enhance each other so that the whole is greater than the sum of the parts. The anti-inflammatory action of chamomile, for example, is supported by lavender. A combination working in this kind of harmony is called a synergy. A good synergy takes into account not only the symptom but also the underlying cause of the disorder, the biological terrain of the person, and the psychological or emotional factors at play.

This was the principle behind the Individual Prescription that Marguerite Maury developed for her clients, in which the blend was matched both to physical needs and to circumstance and temperament.

In general, oils from the same botanical family blend well, as do oils that share common constituents. The camphoraceous oils that contain a good proportion of cineol, for instance, work easily together. This group includes the entire Myrtaceae family (eucalyptus, tea tree, cajeput, myrtle, and others) along with many herbs such as spike lavender, rosemary, and Spanish sage. Most floral fragrances pair well with each other, as do the woods, balsams, citruses, and spices. Rosewood and linaloe combine beautifully despite belonging to different families, because both carry a high proportion of linalol and linalyl acetate.

A handful of oils, among them rose, jasmine, oakmoss, and lavender, seem to enhance almost any blend, which is why they appear (often in adulterated form) in most commercial perfumes. The old saying among perfumers is no perfume without rose.

Other combinations work against each other. Oils dominated by aldehydes, such as citronella with its citronellal, those dominated by ketones, such as sage with its thujone, and those rich in phenols, such as clove with its eugenol, tend to pull in different directions when paired together. Knowing the precise chemistry of each oil is not necessary for building a good synergy. Much of it comes down to learning the character of each oil and trusting intuition.

Fragrant Harmony

In the nineteenth century, the Frenchman Piesse introduced a new approach to perfumery by classifying scents according to the notes of a musical scale. He carried the idea of musical harmony into fragrance, treating each scent as a note that, when properly combined with others, formed a balanced chord. The strict version of his theory has long since fallen away, but it still informs perfumery in the form of top, middle, and base notes.

A top note is fresh and light, immediately apparent because of its rapid evaporation rate.

A middle note is the heart of the fragrance and usually forms the bulk of the blend. Its scent emerges shortly after the first impression.

A base note is rich and heavy, emerging slowly and lingering on the skin. It also acts as a fixative, slowing the dispersal of the lighter oils.

Ylang ylang is often described as a well-balanced perfume oil in its own right, with a powerful sweet-floral top, a creamy middle, and a soft, slightly spicy base.

For the sake of simplicity, each essential oil is usually placed into one of these three categories according to its dominant character. Opinions differ, and the list below is meant only as a general orientation.

A well-balanced perfume contains elements from all three categories, with the proportions deciding whether the result reads as a heavy oriental scent or a light floral. The same principle applies to aromatherapy work and to personalised remedies.
