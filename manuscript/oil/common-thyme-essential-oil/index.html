Common Thyme Essential Oil is steam-distilled from the fresh or partially dried leaves and flowering tops of Thyme (Thymus vulgaris), the perennial Mediterranean evergreen subshrub of the Lamiaceae family. The oil is one of the most ancient and versatile aromatherapy oils, used since the time of Hippocrates and Dioscorides for respiratory, digestive, and infectious complaints.

Extraction & Characteristics

The essential oil is produced by water or steam distillation. Red thyme oil is the crude distillate, a red, brown, or orange liquid with a warm, spicy-herbaceous, powerful odour. White thyme oil is produced by further redistillation or rectification, yielding a clear, pale yellow liquid with a sweet, green-fresh, milder scent. An absolute is also produced in France by solvent extraction for perfumery use. Principal constituents are thymol and carvacrol (up to 60 per cent), cymene, terpinene, camphene, borneol, and linalol, with geraniol, citral, and thuyanol also present depending on chemotype.

Many chemotypes of thyme oil exist: the thymol and carvacrol types are warming and active; the thuyanol type is penetrating and antiviral; and the milder linalol or citral types are sweet-scented and non-irritant.

Therapeutic Uses

In aromatherapy and home use, thyme oil is applied to skin care for abscess, acne, bruises, burns, cuts, dermatitis, eczema, insect bites, lice, gum infections, oily skin, and scabies. For circulation, muscles, and joints it covers arthritis, cellulitis, gout, muscular aches and pains, obesity, oedema, poor circulation, rheumatism, sprains, and sports injuries. Respiratory applications include asthma, bronchitis, catarrh, coughs, laryngitis, sinusitis, sore throat, and tonsillitis. Digestive applications include diarrhoea, dyspepsia, and flatulence. Genito-urinary applications include cystitis and urethritis. For the immune system it is used for chills, colds, flu, and infectious diseases. For the nervous system it addresses headaches, insomnia, nervous debility, and stress-related complaints, helping to revive and strengthen both body and mind. Recorded actions include anthelmintic, antimicrobial, anti-oxidant, antiputrescent, antirheumatic, antiseptic (intestinal, pulmonary, genito-urinary), antispasmodic, antitussive, antitoxic, aperitif, astringent, aphrodisiac, bactericidal, balsamic, carminative, cicatrisant, diuretic, emmenagogue, expectorant, fungicidal, hypertensive, nervine, revulsive, rubefacient, parasiticide, stimulant (immune system and circulation), sudorific, tonic, and vermifuge.

Blending

The oil blends well with Bergamot Essential Oil, Lemon Essential Oil, Rosemary Essential Oil, Lemon Balm Essential Oil, Lavender Essential Oil, Lavandin Essential Oil, Marjoram Essential Oil, Peru Balsam Essential Oil, and Pine Essential Oil.

Safety

Red thyme oil, serpolet (from wild thyme), and the thymol and carvacrol chemotypes all contain quite large amounts of toxic phenols (carvacrol and thymol). They can irritate mucous membranes, cause dermal irritation, and may cause sensitization in some individuals. Use in moderation, in low dilution only, and avoid during pregnancy. White thyme is not a complete oil and is often adulterated. Lemon thyme and the linalol chemotypes are in general less toxic, non-irritant, with less possibility of sensitization, and are safe for use on the skin and with children.

Where You Will Encounter It

Thyme oil is used in mouthwashes, gargles, toothpastes, and cough lozenges. Thymol is isolated for pharmaceutical use in surgical dressings and disinfectants. The oil is used as a fragrance component in soaps, toiletries, aftershaves, perfumes, and colognes, and is extensively employed by the food and drink industry, especially in meat products.
