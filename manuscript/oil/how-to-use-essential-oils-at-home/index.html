Oil Grimoire

How to Use Essential Oils at Home

Essential oils can be used simply and effectively at home in a wide range of ways, both for their scent and for their cosmetic and medicinal qualities. They work as perfumes and as refreshers for pot pourris, they can be added to the bath, and they form the basis for personal beauty preparations. They are also useful in minor first aid and in the prevention and relief of common complaints such as headaches, colds, period pains, and aching muscles.

Storage matters. Essential oils should always be kept in a cool place, in dark bottles, with as little contact with air as possible to protect them from photo-oxidation. They should also be kept well out of reach of children.

The methods below cover the most useful ways to apply essential oils at home, with notes on the conditions and oils each method suits best.

Massage

Massage is the method favoured by professional aromatherapists, who usually carry out a full body treatment. Specific essential oils are chosen to suit the condition and temperament of the person being treated, then blended with a base oil such as sweet almond oil or grapeseed oil.

The essential oil content in a blend is usually between 1 and 3 per cent, depending on what is being addressed. As a rough guide, physical complaints such as rheumatism or indigestion call for a stronger concentration than emotional or nervous conditions. A useful conversion is that 20 drops of essential oil equals roughly one millilitre, so the proportions below cover the common blend sizes.

Massage is a relaxing and nourishing experience in its own right, in part because of the unspoken communication that touch carries, and in part because it ensures the oils are effectively absorbed through the skin and into the bloodstream. For general well-being it is worth practising self-massage on specific areas of the body, focusing especially on the feet and hands. Targeted rubbing can also help with discomfort. Peppermint, in dilution, can be rubbed on the stomach in a clockwise direction to ease indigestion. Marjoram can help relax a stiff neck and shoulders.

Skin Oils and Lotions

For skin oils and lotions, the preparation follows the same approach as a massage blend, except that the base should include more nourishing oils such as jojoba, avocado, or apricot kernel oil. The emphasis here is on treating the skin itself and tackling specific complaints. Gentle circular finger movements are usually enough for the oils to be absorbed. It is important not to drag the skin, especially around the neck and the eyes. Rose and neroli suit dry or mature complexions. Geranium, bergamot, and lemon help with acne and greasy skin.

A few drops of essential oil can also be mixed into a bland cream or lotion, or added to a face mask built around oatmeal, honey, or clay with the pulp of various fruits. For some conditions, such as cold sores (herpes) and athlete's foot, an alcohol-based lotion works better than an oil or cream. This can be made by adding 6 drops of essential oil to 5 ml of isopropyl alcohol or vodka. The same mixture can be diluted further into a litre of boiled and cooled water for use on open cuts or sores, including those from chickenpox or genital herpes.

Hot and Cold Compresses

Compresses are a simple and very effective way to use essential oils for pain relief and to reduce inflammation.

A hot compress is made by filling a bowl with very hot water and adding 4 or 5 drops of essential oil. Fold a piece of cotton cloth, cotton wool, or a flannel, dip it into the bowl, squeeze out the excess water, and place it on the affected area until it has cooled to blood heat, then repeat. Hot compresses are particularly useful for backache, rheumatism, arthritis, abscesses, earache, and toothache.

A cold compress is made the same way, using ice-cold water in place of hot. This type works well for headaches (apply to the forehead or the back of the neck), sprains, strains, and other hot, swollen conditions.

Hair Care

A few drops of essential oil added to a final hair rinse, or stirred into a mild shampoo, can support hair and scalp health. An alcohol-based scalp rub can be made by adding 5 ml of essential oil to 100 ml of vodka, useful both as a conditioning treatment and for clearing parasites such as lice and fleas.

A deeper conditioning treatment can be made by combining about 3 per cent (around 60 drops) of essential oil with a nourishing base such as olive oil mixed with jojoba or sweet almond oil. Massage this into the scalp and wrap the hair in warm towels for an hour or two. Rosemary, West Indian bay, and chamomile help condition the hair and encourage healthy growth. Lavender repels lice and fleas. Bergamot and tea tree are useful for controlling dandruff.

Flower Waters

A toilet or flower water can be made at home by adding about 20 to 30 drops of essential oil to a 100 ml bottle of spring or de-ionised water. Leave the bottle in the dark for a few days, then filter the water through coffee filter paper. Essential oils do not dissolve in water, but they readily lend it their scent and their properties.

Flower waters are particularly helpful in the prevention and treatment of skin conditions such as acne, dermatitis, and eczema, as well as for general toning and cleansing. Almost any oil can be used. Traditional choices include rose, orange blossom, lavender, and petitgrain. Blended flower waters can also be tailored to suit specific complexions.

Baths

One of the simplest and most pleasurable ways to use essential oils is to add 5 to 10 drops to the bath when the tub is full. Aromatic bathing has a long tradition as a sensual and enjoyable experience, with the Romans among its great enthusiasts, and as a treatment for a wide range of complaints including irritating skin conditions, muscular aches and pains, rheumatism, and arthritis. Ylang ylang offers a euphoric aromatic experience in its own right. Chamomile and lavender ease stress-related complaints such as anxiety and insomnia. Rosemary and pine soothe aching limbs. Take care to avoid oils that may irritate the skin.

Vaporization

A burner or aromatic diffuser is a clean way to scent a room, with none of the dust or smoke that incense produces. A few drops of oil placed on a light bulb ring, or added to a small bowl of water on a radiator, work just as well. Specific oils can be chosen to set different atmospheres. Frankincense and cedarwood have a long ritual history and create a peaceful, relaxed mood. Vaporised citronella or lemongrass keep insects at bay and clear the air of unwanted smells like cigarette smoke.

In earlier times, juniper and rosemary leaves were burned to help control epidemics and purify the air. The same oils can keep an environment free of germs and inhibit the spread of infections like colds and flu. Myrtle or eucalyptus can be vaporised in the bedroom at night to ease breathing difficulties or children's coughs. A few drops on a pillow or a handkerchief carry the same effect through the day.

Always place the burner in a safe spot, well out of reach of children and pets.

Steam Inhalation

Steam inhalation is particularly suited to sinus, throat, and chest infections. Add around 5 drops of an oil such as peppermint or thyme to a bowl of hot water, cover the head and bowl with a towel, and breathe deeply for a minute, then repeat. Sitting in a steaming hot bath is another way to take in a small amount of essential oil through the air, though the dose is far less concentrated. The same approach can serve as a facial sauna: oils like lemon or tea tree help unclog the pores and clear the complexion.

Douche

A douche can help with common genito-urinary infections such as thrush, cystitis, and pruritis. For candida or thrush, add between 5 and 10 drops of tea tree to a litre of warm water and shake well. The mixture can be used in a sitz bath or bidet, or placed in an enema or douche pot, which can be bought from some chemists. Lavender and cypress can also support healing after childbirth.

Neat Application

As a general rule, essential oils are not applied to the skin undiluted, but there are a few notable exceptions. Lavender can be applied neat to burns, cuts, and insect bites. Tea tree can be dabbed on spots. Lemon can be used directly on warts. Sandalwood, jasmine, and rose make excellent perfumes when dabbed neat onto the skin.

Take care with oils that are known to cause skin reactions: phototoxic oils such as bergamot, which discolour the skin when exposed to direct sunlight; irritants such as red thyme; and skin sensitisers such as cinnamon bark.

Blending a selection of oils into a personal fragrance is also worth exploring. Certain oils make excellent additions to linen and clothes, or for refreshing pot pourris. Patchouli, for example, has been used for centuries in India to scent cloth.

Internal Use

Essential oils should not be taken internally at home. They are highly concentrated, and a handful of essences are genuinely toxic. The International Federation of Aromatherapists advises against oral use as well.

This is rarely a practical limitation, because essential oils are absorbed readily through the skin and so reach the internal organs and systems through external application. In a condition like arthritis, which reflects a build-up of toxins in the joints, dietary changes and herbal remedies can be supported externally by oils such as juniper and white birch, which help purify the system while easing pain and inflammation at the site of the swelling.

⚠️
Essential oils should never be used at home to treat serious medical or psychological problems.

Herbal Preparations

These methods apply to herb-based preparations made at home using dried or fresh plant material, without essential oils.

Creams

An unscented, hypoallergenic cream base serves as the carrier. Empty the jar into the top of a double boiler and heat over boiling water. Add the chosen herbs (rose, chamomile, violet, or others) and simmer gently for two hours with the lid on. Use roughly one ounce of dried herbs or two and a half ounces of fresh herbs per ten ounces of cream base. Remove from heat, strain through cheesecloth, and pack into jars with tight-fitting lids. Cool to room temperature before sealing. Store in a cool, dark place.

Decoction

Decoction draws constituents from tough plant materials such as roots and bark. Combine herbs and cold water in a pan, using one ounce of dried herbs or two ounces of fresh herbs per two cups of water. Bring to a boil and simmer gently for twenty to forty minutes, or until the liquid reduces by half to one-third. Strain into a covered container. Store in a cool place or refrigerate. Best used freshly made.

Infusion

An infusion is a strong herbal steep. Use one tablespoon of fresh herbs or two tablespoons of dried herbs per cup of water. For a cold infusion, steep herbs in cold water overnight in a sealed container. For a hot infusion, remove boiling water from heat, add herbs, push them down into the water, cover, and steep for ten minutes. For a sun infusion, place the sealed jar in sunlight and allow the herbs to steep for three to four weeks, shaking daily. Strain and bottle.

Hot Oil Infusion

Combine fresh herbs and a light carrier oil (sunflower, almond, or light olive) in the top of a double boiler. Heat over boiling water for two hours with the lid on. Use one cup of fresh herbs per two cups of oil, or half a cup of dried herbs. Do not allow the oil to boil. Strain through cheesecloth and store in bottles in a cool, dark place. Infused oils serve well for massage, as the base for ointments, or as culinary additions.

Creating Magickal & Perfumed Oils

These instructions apply to perfumed oils, anointing oils, and massage oils blended for magickal use.

Yield Sizes

  • Small batch — 1 to 2 oz · ideal for personal use and anointing candles
  • Large batch — 6 to 8 oz · ideal for massage

How to Blend

  1. Fill your bottle three-quarters full with the carrier oil of your choice — almond, jojoba, or similar.
  2. Add the required drops of essential oil one at a time, using an eye dropper, swirling gently after each addition.
  3. Once all oils are combined, hold the bottle between your hands and focus clearly on the intent you desire, charging the blend with your will.
  4. Cap and label each bottle.
  5. Gently swirl again just before every use to re-integrate the blend.

Dropper Care

Unless the bottle has its own built-in dropper, flush your dropper with rubbing alcohol between each oil — draw it in and push it out several times — to prevent cross-contamination of scents.

Blending for Aromatherapy

When blending for a diffuser rather than for topical use, omit the carrier oil entirely. Drop the essential oils directly into the water in the diffuser container that sits over the heat.

Fixative & Preservation

💧
Add 1 drop of benzoin to every bottle of personal magickal oil — at least 2 drops in massage oils — to act as a fixative and prevent the blend turning rancid. A tiny chip of an appropriate crystal added to the bottle will also amplify and align its energy.

Double-Charging Candle Spells

To add an extra layer of herbal power to candle magick, choose two or three appropriate herbs, grind them finely, spread them on a paper towel, and roll the dressed candle over them so the herbs adhere to the oiled surface.
