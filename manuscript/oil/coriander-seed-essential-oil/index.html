Therapeutic Actions

Coriander seed oil is recognized as analgesic, aperitif, aphrodisiac, anti-oxidant, anti-rheumatic, antispasmodic, bactericidal, depurative, digestive, carminative, cytotoxic, fungicidal, larvicidal, lipolytic, revitalizing, stomachic, and a stimulant of the cardiac, circulatory, and nervous systems.

Constituents

A colourless to pale yellow liquid with a sweet, woody-spicy, slightly musky fragrance. Linalol dominates at 55 to 75 percent, alongside decyl aldehyde, borneol, geraniol, carvone, and anethole; the exact composition varies by chemotype and source. A distinct essential oil is also produced by steam distillation from the fresh and dried leaves and contains a high proportion of decyl aldehyde.

Other Uses

Coriander seed oil features as a flavouring agent in pharmaceutical preparations, particularly digestive remedies, and as a fragrance component in soaps, toiletries, and perfumes. The food industry employs it heavily in meat products and to flavour liqueurs such as Chartreuse and Benedictine; it is also used to flavour tobacco.

Herbal Lore

Coriander is the seed of cilantro and a well-known addition to many styles of food, with Italian and Mexican being the most notable.

The lore of coriander frequently centers on its reputation in the realm of love, lust, and sex. Coriander seeds were frequently crushed and added to wine to act as a lustful potion, designed to inflame the loins of anyone who drank it. The seeds were not only to arouse desire in the intended target but also had a reputation for making sex last longer, and helping the coupling be healthy and fertile.

Pregnant women were told to eat coriander seeds so that they would bear intelligent children.

Wives would place coriander seeds near the hearth to release their scent, thereby increasing the peace in the home, protecting it from harm, and arousing the man of the house.
