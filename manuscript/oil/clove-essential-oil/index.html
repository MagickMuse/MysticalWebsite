Notes/Warnings
Clove bud is the least toxic of the three clove oils due to its lower eugenol content. Use only in low dilution under 1 percent and avoid in those with sensitive skin or mucous membranes. Leaf and stem oils have higher eugenol content and should not be used in home aromatherapy.

Herbal Lore

Clove can be used for both fiery workings and ones designed for thought and intellect. Learn all you can about both sets of information, and use clove when intuition calls for it.

Clove buds have several uses medically, emotionally, and magickally. Medical research has turned up over thirty-five papers from studies on the effects of clove. It is used in many blends because of the varied use and history of the plant and oil. Many people only know clove from its reputation for soothing and numbing the pain of toothaches. The essential oil also combats aging, relieves arthritis pain, soothes lung infections, and is antiparasitic.

Historical lore suggests that cloves are so protective, they were named after nails. Stories suggest placing a small bag filled with cloves near a sleeping baby to protect it, and stringing cloves on red thread and hanging it near the baby's crib to protect them from sibling rivalry. Orange and clove pomander balls are commonly seen during the winter months to protect homes and families by warding off sickness.

Magickal Uses

To stop gossip, place diluted clove oil on a taper candle with the word "gossip" carved into it. Burn the candle to dispel gossip in your name and let the truth be heard. You may also substitute the name of the gossip in question, if known.

For home protection, use diluted clove oil to anoint doors, windows, and the backs of mirrors to protect the occupants of the home from maliciousness or astral spying.

The flower meaning of clove is dignity. Place a drop of clove essential oil on a tissue and inhale the aroma before making an apology.

Clove can help turn aside nasty weather. If damaging storms are expected, buy a small jar of whole cloves and masonry nails from the hardware store. Place the cloves at the four corners of your lawn and along the border. Then anchor the four corners with masonry nails. Anoint the nails with diluted clove oil before sinking them into the dirt to protect your property. If you live in an apartment or condo, place cloves in the four corners of your apartment.

Bud, Leaf, and Stem Oils

The clove tree yields three commercially distinct essential oils. Bud oil is water-distilled from the dried flower buds and is the only form recommended for home aromatherapy. It is a pale yellow liquid with a sweet-spicy odour and a fruity-fresh top note, favoured in perfumery work. Leaf oil is water-distilled from the leaves and presents as a dark brown liquid with a crude, burnt-woody odour. Stem oil is steam-distilled from the stalks and is a pale yellow liquid with a strong spicy-woody character. Bud oil contains 60 to 90 percent eugenol; leaf oil 82 to 88 percent eugenol with little or no eugenyl acetate; stem oil 90 to 95 percent eugenol. Higher eugenol content increases skin and mucous membrane irritation, which is why Clove Leaf Essential Oil and Clove Stem Essential Oil are not used in home aromatherapy.

Aromatherapy Uses (Bud)

Skin care applications include acne, athlete's foot, bruises, burns, cuts, insect repellent against mosquitoes, toothache, ulcers, and wounds. For circulation, muscles, and joints, the bud oil is applied to arthritis, rheumatism, and sprains. Respiratory uses include asthma and bronchitis. Digestive applications cover colic, dyspepsia, and nausea. Immune-system uses cover colds, flu, and minor infections.

Blending Partners (Bud)

Clove bud blends well with Rose Oil, Lavender Essential Oil, vanillin, Clary Sage Essential Oil, Bergamot Essential Oil, Bay Laurel Essential Oil (bay leaf), Lavandin Essential Oil, Allspice Essential Oil, Ylang Ylang Essential Oil, and Cananga Essential Oil.

Industrial and Commercial Uses

Clove oil is used in dental preparations and as a fragrance component in toothpastes, soaps, toiletries, cosmetics, and perfumes. It is widely employed as a flavour ingredient in major food categories and in alcoholic and soft drinks. Clove leaf oil is the starting material for the isolation of eugenol, and clove oil also features in the production of printing ink, glue, and varnish.
