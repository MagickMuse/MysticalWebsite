◇Notes/Warnings
Non-toxic, non-sensitizing, and non-irritant in standard topical use; may be irritating in high concentration. Culpeper noted that the oil of spike is of a fierce and piercing quality and should be used carefully, with very few drops being sufficient.
◇Drug Interactions
High camphor and 1,8-cineole — caution with epilepsy and seizure disorders. Do not use on or around infants and young children (1,8-cineole respiratory risk). Stimulant; not interchangeable with true lavender (Lavandula angustifolia) for calming use.

A water-white or pale yellow liquid produced by water or steam distillation from the flowering tops of Spike Lavender (Lavandula latifolia). The oil carries a penetrating, fresh-herbaceous, camphoraceous odour.

Extraction & Characteristics

Produced by water or steam distillation from the flowering tops. Principal constituents are mainly cineol and camphor (40 to 60 percent), with linalol and linalyl acetate among others.

Therapeutic Uses

Spike lavender is current in the British Herbal Pharmacopoeia, indicated for flatulent dyspepsia, colic, depressive headaches, and the oil topically for rheumatic pain. The historical preparation oleum spicae (one part spike oil to three parts turpentine) was used for paralysed limbs, old sprains, and stiff joints, and was also said to encourage hair growth. For broader aromatherapy applications, see Lavender Essential Oil, which the source defers to for actions and home use.

Blending

Blends well with Rosemary Essential Oil, Sage Essential Oil, Lavandin Essential Oil, Eucalyptus Essential Oil, Rosewood Essential Oil, Lavender Essential Oil, Petitgrain Essential Oil, Pine Essential Oil, Cedar Essential Oil, Atlas Cedarwood Essential Oil, Texas Cedarwood Essential Oil, Virginian Cedarwood Essential Oil, Oakmoss Absolute, Patchouli Essential Oil, and spice oils, particularly Clove Essential Oil.

Safety

Non-toxic, non-sensitizing, and non-irritant in standard topical use; may be irritating in high concentration.

Where You Will Encounter It

Used in some pharmaceutical preparations and especially in veterinary practice as a prophylactic, for incipient paralysis, rheumatism and arthritis, and to get rid of lice. Extensively employed as a fragrance component in soaps and industrial perfumes such as deodorants, disinfectants, and cleaning agents, as well as insecticides and room sprays. Also used in the food industry and in the production of fine varnishes and lacquers.
