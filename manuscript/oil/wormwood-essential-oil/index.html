Plant1
Wormwood

Do not use Wormwood Essential Oil in home aromatherapy. This is a toxic, abortifacient oil with a thujone content as high as seventy-one percent. The source is explicit: it should not be used in therapy, either internally or externally. The page below documents the plant and its history for reference only.

Other Names & Related Species

Known as common wormwood, green ginger, armoise, and absinthium oil. There are many other Artemisia species, including davana and the Roman wormwood. See also Mugwort, which is also commonly called armoise. Other relatives in the workspace include Tarragon, Sagebrush, and Southernwood.

Origin & Distribution

Native to Europe, North Africa, and western Asia, and naturalized in North America. Wormwood is extensively cultivated in central and southern Europe, the former USSR, North Africa, and the USA, where the oil is mainly produced. The plant is a perennial herb up to one and a half metres high, with a whitish stem, silvery-green divided leaves covered in silky fine hairs, and pale yellow flowers.

Folk Tradition

Used historically as an aromatic-bitter for anorexia, as a digestive tonic, and as a choleretic for liver and gall bladder disorders, usually in the form of a dilute extract rather than the essential oil. It was also used to promote menstruation, reduce fever, and expel worms. It was once a remedy for epilepsy, and an aromatic strewing herb to banish fleas. Reported actions of the plant include anthelmintic, choleretic, deodorant, emmenagogue, febrifuge, insect repellent, narcotic, digestive stimulant, tonic, and vermifuge.

Extraction & Characteristics

The essential oil is produced by steam distillation from the leaves and flowering tops. An absolute is occasionally produced by solvent extraction. The oil is dark green or bluish, with a spicy, warm, bitter-green odour and a sharp, fresh top note. Principal constituents are thujone at up to seventy-one percent, with azulenes and terpenes. A de-thujonized variant exists for industrial fragrance use, and is reported to blend well with oakmoss, jasmine, orange blossom, lavender, and hyacinth, but the unmodified essential oil is not safe for home or therapeutic use.

Safety

Toxic. Abortifacient. Should not be used in therapy, either internally or externally. Habitual use can cause restlessness, nightmares, convulsions, vomiting, and, in extreme cases, brain damage. In 1915, the French banned the production of the drink Absinthe made with this plant, due to its narcotic and habit-forming properties. Avoid in pregnancy and nursing without exception. Keep out of reach of children and pets. Do not ingest, do not apply to skin, and do not diffuse around vulnerable people.

Where You Will Encounter It

Occasionally used in rubefacient pharmaceutical preparations, and as a fragrance component in toiletries, cosmetics, and perfumes. Widely employed at minute levels as a flavouring agent in alcoholic bitters and vermouths, and to a lesser extent in soft drinks and some foods, especially confectionery and desserts.
