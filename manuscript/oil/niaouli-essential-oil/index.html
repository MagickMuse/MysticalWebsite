Botanical Source

Niaouli (Melaleuca viridiflora) of the Myrtaceae family, also classified as M. quinquenervia and traded under the name "gomenol." The plant is an evergreen tree carrying a flexible trunk and spongy bark, with pointed linear leaves and spikes of sessile yellowish flowers. The leaves release a strong aromatic scent when crushed.

Distribution

Native to Australia, New Caledonia, and the French Pacific Islands. The majority of niaouli oil is produced in Australia and Tasmania.

Other Species

A typical member of the "tea tree" group within Melaleuca, niaouli is closely related to cajeput. A separate physiological form of M. viridiflora, designated "Variety A," was developed as a natural source of nerolidol, the main constituent of its essential oil.

Folk and Historical Use

Used locally for a wide range of ailments: aches and pains, respiratory conditions, cuts, and infections. Also employed to purify water. The trade name "gomenol" derives from the port of Gomen in the French East Indies, from which the oil was once shipped.

Therapeutic Actions

Analgesic, anthelmintic, anticatarrhal, antirheumatic, antiseptic, antispasmodic, bactericidal, balsamic, cicatrisant, diaphoretic, expectorant, regulator, stimulant, and vermifuge.

Extraction & Characteristics

The essential oil is steam-distilled from the leaves and young twigs and is usually rectified to remove irritant aldehydes. It presents as a colourless, pale yellow, or greenish liquid carrying a sweet, fresh, camphoraceous odour.

Principal Constituents

Cineol dominates at 50 to 65 percent, alongside terpineol, pinene, limonene, citrene, terebenthene, and valeric, acetic, and butyric esters.

Safety

Non-toxic, non-irritant, and non-sensitizing. The oil is often subject to adulteration on the open market.

Aromatherapy Uses

Skin care: Acne, boils, burns, cuts, insect bites, oily skin, spots, ulcers, and wounds.

Circulation, muscles, and joints: Muscular aches and pains, poor circulation, and rheumatism.

Respiratory system: Asthma, bronchitis, catarrhal conditions, coughs, sinusitis, sore throat, and whooping cough.

Genito-urinary system: Cystitis and urinary infection.

Immune system: Colds, fever, and flu.

Related Oils

The source identifies niaouli as a member of the "tea tree" group and notes the oil is similar to cajeput. Natural blending partners include Cajeput Essential Oil and Tea Tree Essential Oil.

Other Uses

Featured in pharmaceutical preparations including gargles, cough drops, toothpastes, and mouth sprays.
