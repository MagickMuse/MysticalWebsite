Oil Grimoire

A Guide to Aromatic Materials

Aromatic plants are not interchangeable. The same species grown in two different climates can produce essential oils with quite different characters, and the way an oil is pulled from the plant changes what ends up in the bottle. This guide walks through where these plants grow, the chemistry that gives each oil its action and aroma, and the main extraction methods used to capture them.

Habitat

Roughly thirty plant families and ninety species supply most of the world's essential oils. Spices like allspice, cardamom, clove, nutmeg, and ginger come from tropical regions, while most herbs, including bay, cumin, dill, marjoram, fennel, lavender, rosemary, and thyme, come from temperate climates.

The same plant grown in different soils, altitudes, or weather can yield essential oils of strikingly different makeup. These regional variants are called chemotypes. Common thyme (Thymus vulgaris) is the classic example. Depending on where it grows and which constituent dominates, it may produce a citral or linalol type, a thuyanol type, or a thymol or carvacrol type. Each of these behaves differently on the skin, in the body, and in the nose.

For this reason, the botanical name alone is not enough when sourcing an oil. Place of origin and dominant constituents matter just as much, and reading the chemical profile is one of the main ways quality and purity are confirmed.

Chemistry

Essential oils are built from compounds whose backbone is hydrogen, carbon, and oxygen. They fall into two broad groups: hydrocarbons, made up almost entirely of terpenes (monoterpenes, sesquiterpenes, and diterpenes), and oxygenated compounds, which include esters, aldehydes, ketones, alcohols, phenols, and oxides. Acids, lactones, and traces of sulphur or nitrogen compounds sometimes appear as well.

Terpenes

Common terpene hydrocarbons include limonene, an antiviral that makes up roughly 90 per cent of citrus oils, and pinene, an antiseptic found in high proportions in pine and turpentine oils. Camphene, cadinene, caryophyllene, cedrene, dipentene, phellandrene, terpinene, sabinene, and myrcene also belong to this group. Sesquiterpenes such as chamazulene and farnesol, both present in chamomile, have drawn recent attention for their anti-inflammatory and bactericidal action.

Esters

Esters are probably the most widespread group across essential oils. Linalyl acetate appears in bergamot, clary sage, and lavender, while geranyl acetate appears in sweet marjoram. They tend to be fungicidal and sedative, and often carry a fruity aroma. Bornyl acetate, eugenyl acetate, and lavendulyl acetate are other common esters.

Aldehydes

Citral, citronellal, and neral are the aldehydes most associated with the lemon-scented oils, including melissa, lemongrass, lemon verbena, lemon-scented eucalyptus, and citronella. Aldehydes generally have a sedative effect, and citral in particular has been shown to be antiseptic. Benzaldehyde, cinnamic aldehyde, cuminic aldehyde, and perillaldehyde also belong here.

Ketones

Some of the more notably toxic oil constituents are ketones: thujone in mugwort, tansy, sage, and wormwood, and pulegone in pennyroyal and buchu. That does not make all ketones dangerous. Jasmone in jasmine and fenchone in fennel are non-toxic. Ketones tend to ease congestion and help mucus flow, which is why they often appear in plants used for upper respiratory complaints, such as hyssop and sage. Camphor, carvone, menthone, methyl nonyl ketone, and pinocamphone round out the group.

Alcohols

Alcohols are among the most useful compounds in essential oils. They tend to be antiseptic and antiviral, carry an uplifting quality, and are generally non-toxic. Common terpene alcohols include linalol (in rosewood, linaloe, and lavender), citronellol (in rose, lemon eucalyptus, and geranium), and geraniol (in palmarosa). Borneol, menthol, nerol, terpineol, farnesol, vetiverol, benzyl alcohol, and cedrol fall into this group as well.

Phenols

Phenols are bactericidal and strongly stimulating, but they can also irritate the skin. Eugenol appears in clove and West Indian bay, thymol in thyme, and carvacrol in oregano and savory. Methyl eugenol, methyl chavicol, anethole, safrole, myristicin, and apiol are other common phenols.

Oxides

The most important oxide by far is cineol, also known as eucalyptol, which sits almost in a category of its own. It is expectorant and is the principal constituent of eucalyptus oil. It also appears in many camphoraceous oils such as rosemary, bay laurel, tea tree, and cajeput. Linalol oxide (in decumbent hyssop), ascaridol, bisabolol oxide, and bisabolone oxide are other oxides found in essential oils.

Methods of Extraction

The phrase essential oil is often used loosely to cover anything aromatic pulled from a plant, concretes, resinoids, and absolutes included. Strictly speaking, those products contain only some essential oil along with non-volatile parts like wax or resin, and they are made by different methods, including solvent and carbon dioxide extraction. The aromatic quality of any of these products comes from its essential oil content.

Some plant materials, flowers especially, deteriorate quickly and need to be processed soon after harvest. Others, such as seeds and roots, can be stored or shipped, often to Europe or America, before extraction. The method chosen depends on the plant material itself and the kind of aromatic product being made.

Essential Oils

True essential oils are extracted in two main ways. Citrus oils such as lemon and bergamot are usually obtained by simple expression or pressure. Most others, including lavender, myrrh, sandalwood, and cinnamon, are produced by steam, water, or dry distillation.

Distillation isolates only the volatile, water-insoluble parts of a plant, which means many other valuable constituents like tannins, mucilage, and bitters are left behind. The resulting oil is sometimes redistilled or rectified to remove non-volatile matter. A few oils are redistilled at different temperatures to capture specific constituents and exclude others. Camphor, for example, is split into white, yellow, and brown fractions this way.

Most essential oils are liquid, but some are solid (orris) or semi-solid depending on temperature (rose). They dissolve in pure alcohol, fats, and oils, but not in water. Unlike fixed plant oils such as olive oil, essential oils evaporate when exposed to air and leave no oily residue.

Hydrosols

The water that comes off a steam distillation is not waste. It carries roughly 0.02 to 0.03 per cent dissolved essential oil, which gives it a gentler version of the parent oil's aromatic and energetic character. These aromatic waters are called hydrosols, and they hold the same magickal properties as their corresponding essential oil at a lower price point and a much milder strength.

Hydrosols work well as liquid smudge blends, gentle skin applications, bath additives, room sprays, yoga mat fresheners, and a softer alternative to water diffusers. To build a quick water-based room spray, place 1 teaspoon of table salt or witch hazel in a glass bowl, add 30 drops of essential oil, top with 2 tablespoons of water, and stir well. Transfer to a spray bottle and fill the rest with warm water. Shake before each use.

💧
Note

Always use an intermediary like salt or witch hazel when mixing essential oils with water. Without it the oil will separate and leave stains on whatever the spray lands on.

Concretes

Concretes come almost entirely from plant material such as bark, flower, leaf, herb, or root. Instead of distillation or expression, the material is treated with hydrocarbon-type solvents. This is necessary when the essential oil is harmed by hot water or steam, as with jasmine, and it also yields a more true-to-life fragrance. Some plants, lavender and clary sage among them, are made into either an essential oil by steam distillation or a concrete by solvent extraction. The residue is usually solid and waxy, with no crystalline structure.

Most concretes are about half wax and half volatile oil. Jasmine concrete is typical. In a few cases, like ylang ylang, the concrete is liquid and runs closer to 80 per cent essential oil and 20 per cent wax. Their advantage over pure essential oils is that they are more stable and more concentrated.

Resinoids

Resinoids are made from natural resinous material treated with a hydrocarbon solvent such as petroleum ether or hexane. The key difference from concretes is the source: resinoids come from dead organic material, while concretes come from previously living tissue. Typical sources include balsams (Peru balsam, benzoin), resins (mastic, amber), oleoresins (copaiba balsam, turpentine), and oleo gum resins (frankincense, myrrh).

Resinoids can be viscous liquids, semi-solid, or solid, and are usually homogeneous and non-crystalline. The alcohol-soluble portion of a resinoid is sometimes called an absolute. Frankincense and myrrh can be turned into either an essential oil by steam distillation or a resin absolute by alcohol extraction directly from the crude oleo gum resin. Benzoin is too non-volatile to distill into an essential oil. Liquid benzoin is usually a benzoin resinoid dissolved in a suitable solvent. In perfumery, both concretes and resinoids work as fixatives, prolonging the effect of a fragrance.

Absolutes

An absolute is obtained from a concrete by a second round of solvent extraction, this time using pure ethanol. Wax dissolves only slightly in ethanol, so repeated treatment leaves most of it behind. Even so, some waxes hold on. Orange flower absolute, for example, retains a small amount no matter how thorough the process. Absolutes can then be put through molecular distillation to strip away every remaining trace of non-volatile matter. The alcohol is recovered by evaporation, with a gentle vacuum applied toward the end.

Some absolutes still hold traces of ethanol, around 2 per cent or less, and are not recommended for therapeutic work because of those impurities. Absolutes are usually highly concentrated viscous liquids, though some are solid or semi-solid (clary sage absolute is one). In recent decades, much research has gone into extracting essential oils with liquid carbon dioxide, which produces oils of excellent odour quality with no solvent residues or non-volatile matter to remove.

Pomades

True pomades are the products of enfleurage, a process now nearly obsolete. It was once the main way to capture aromatic material from flowers that kept producing perfume long after they were cut. A glass plate was coated in a thin layer of specially prepared, odourless fat called a chassis. Freshly cut flowers, jasmine or tuberose for example, were laid into the fat one by one, and the fat absorbed their volatile oils. The chassis was renewed with new flowers throughout the harvest. The fragrance-saturated fat that resulted was the pomade, and it was treated with alcohol to produce the pure absolute or perfume.

Natural versus Nature Identical

Many perfumes and oils that were once pulled from flowers like carnation, gardenia, and lilac are now produced almost entirely from synthetics. In the pharmaceutical industry, these chemically constructed products are called nature identical. The perfume and flavouring industries depend on consistency, and naturally grown plants are subject to seasonal variation, which is part of the appeal of a synthetic stand-in. The price tag reflects the difference: synthetic versions are far cheaper than the real thing.

Aromatic oils such as lavender or geranium tend to contain a small number of major constituents, several minor ones, and a very large number of trace elements. Reconstructing that level of complexity, including all the trace elements, is essentially impossible. Most nature identical oils are said to be about 96 per cent accurate, yet it is the remaining 4 per cent, the trace elements, that often define a fragrance's character. Galbanum is a clear case. The pyrazines that give it its powerful green note are present at less than 0.1 per cent of the total oil.

The same principle applies to therapeutic value. The specific blend of constituents in a real essential oil, trace elements included, is what gives it its action. These minute amounts may have a synergistic or controlling effect on the main constituents. Rose contains over 300 different constituents, some still unidentified, which is why synthetic rose oil never quite convinces. Nature identical oils cannot stand in for the natural ones in therapeutic or magickal work. The subtle balance of constituents is lost, and so is the living energy that the plant itself carries.

Recognizing a Good Oil

Price is not a reliable indicator of potency. The most useful approach is to try several brands and learn what a real version of each oil smells, feels, and looks like over time. Ethical suppliers clearly label diluted oils, which is common and expected for the rarer or more expensive varieties.

Some oils are diluted more often than others, either because the pure form is hard to source or because the unblended price would be unreasonable on its own. Frankincense, blue chamomile, jasmine, melissa (lemon balm), myrrh, neroli, and rose are the names to watch most carefully.

Each oil also has tells in its physical character. Vetiver, for instance, should be dark and syrupy. If a bottle is clear and runs thin, it has either been cut or is not really vetiver. Building this kind of recognition takes time and a few side-by-side comparisons, but it is the most reliable shortcut to spotting a fake.
