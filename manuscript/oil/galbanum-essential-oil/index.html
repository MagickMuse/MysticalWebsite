Galbanum Essential Oil is steam- or water-distilled from the oleoresin of Ferula galbaniflua, a large perennial herb of the Mediterranean and Middle East whose stems exude a milky natural oleoresin. Only the Levant or soft type of galbanum is used for oil production; the harder Persian variety is reserved for resinoid and pharmaceutical work.

Botanical Information

  • Scientific name: Ferula galbaniflua (also classified as F. gummosa)
  • Family: Apiaceae (Umbelliferae)
  • Plant: Large perennial herb with a smooth stem, shiny leaflets, and small flowers. Resin ducts within the stem exude a milky oleoresin.
  • Source of oil: Dried oleoresinous exudation, collected by incising the base of the stem
  • Evaporation: Top to middle note (fresh green topnote with a balsamic undertone)

Distribution

Native to the Middle East and western Asia. Cultivated in Iran, Turkey, Afghanistan, and Lebanon. Distillation usually takes place in Europe or the USA.

Other Species and Forms

Galbanum exists in two distinct commercial types: Levant galbanum, which is liquid or soft and used for essential oil production, and Persian galbanum, which is solid or hard and reserved for resinoid and pharmaceutical preparation. Other Ferula species also yield galbanum-like gum, including the muskroot. The closely related Zalou root (F. hermonic) is used in Beirut as an aphrodisiac.

Extraction & Characteristics

Method: Water or steam distillation from the oleoresin or gum (Levant type only). A partially deterpenised oil known as "galbanol" is also produced, and a resinoid is prepared mainly for use as a fixative.

Crude oleoresin: A dark amber or brown viscous liquid with a green-woody scent and soft balsamic undertone.

Distilled oil: A colourless, pale yellow, or olive liquid carrying a fresh green topnote and woody-dry balsamic undertone.

Principal Constituents

Pinene, cadinol, cadinene, and myrcene, among others.

Safety

Non-toxic, non-irritant, and non-sensitising.

Therapeutic Actions

Analgesic, anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, antiseptic, antispasmodic, aphrodisiac, balsamic, carminative, cicatrisant, digestive, diuretic, emmenagogue, expectorant, hypotensive, restorative, and tonic.

Folk and Historical Use

Used by the ancient civilisations as a ceremonial incense, in Egyptian cosmetics, and in the embalming process. Eastern herbalism has long employed galbanum in much the same way as asafetida: for wounds, inflammations, and skin disorders, and for respiratory, digestive, and nervous complaints.

Aromatherapy Uses

  • Skin care: abscesses, acne, boils, cuts, scar tissue, inflammations, skin toning, mature skin, wrinkles, wounds; the oil "signifies drying and preservation."
  • Circulation, muscles, and joints: poor circulation, muscular aches and pains, rheumatism.
  • Respiratory system: asthma, bronchitis, catarrh, chronic coughs.
  • Digestive system: cramp, flatulence, indigestion.
  • Nervous system: nervous tension and stress-related complaints.

Source-Named Blending Partners

Hyacinth Absolute, Violet Leaf Absolute, Narcissus Oil, Lavender Essential Oil, Geranium Essential Oil, Oakmoss Absolute, opopanax, Pine Essential Oil, Fir Needle Essential Oil, Levant Storax Essential Oil, and oriental bases.

Other Uses

The Persian gum was historically employed in pharmaceutical products. Both oil and resinoid serve as fixatives and fragrance components in soaps, detergents, creams, lotions, and perfumes. Galbanum also appears as a flavour ingredient across most major food categories and in alcoholic and soft drinks.

Traditional Grimoire Notes

Galbanum has a gorgeously bittersweet aroma and was used by the ancient Egyptians in ceremonial incense and cosmetics. As an oleo-resin, it must be combined with an oil for best results in perfumery work, and fumed rather than burned directly on charcoal for incense use.
