Incense Guide

Incense has been part of magickal practice for over 5,000 years. Ancient practitioners burned it to carry prayers skyward, create sacred space, and commune with divine forces.

Why Burn Incense?

Ritual Consciousness

Burning incense helps shift awareness into the focused state needed for magickal work. The rising smoke, the fragrance, and the act of preparation all signal to the mind that sacred work is beginning.

Purification

Smoke cleanses a space of stagnant or unwanted energies before ritual. This clearing establishes the right atmosphere for effective magick.

Directing Energy

Blends formulated for specific purposes help attract and channel particular energies. A healing incense draws healing forces; a prosperity blend attracts abundance.

Offerings

Some incenses honor deities or spirits. Juniper burned for Inanna in ancient Sumer is one of the oldest recorded examples.

How Incense Works

Every substance carries its own energetic signature. When incense burns, these energies release from physical form into the environment. The smoke carries the vibrations outward, where they blend with the practitioner's focused intent and move toward manifestation.

Select incense based on its correspondences. For healing work, burn herbs associated with health and restoration.

Scent and Magick

Scent matters less than you might expect in magickal incense. The general rule:

  • Pleasant scents suit positive workings
  • Harsh scents suit banishing and exorcism

Many effective magickal incenses smell strange, bitter, or intensely resinous. Ritual blends prioritize energetic alignment over pleasant fragrance. If you want something that simply smells nice, make a separate household blend for that purpose.

⚠️
Reserve Magickal Incense for Magickal Work

Burning empowered incense casually wastes its energy. Keep magickal blends for ritual use.

Two Forms of Incense

Noncombustible (Loose/Granular)

Sprinkled onto hot charcoal to release fragrance. Contains no burning agents.

Most magickal incense is noncombustible because:

  • Simpler to make
  • Allows control over smoke quantity
  • Produces the billowing smoke some rituals require

Combustible (Cones, Sticks, Blocks)

Contains potassium nitrate (saltpeter) so it burns independently. More convenient but more complex to create.

Making Noncombustible Incense

Gather all ingredients before starting. Substitutions are fine if needed.

Step 1: Grind Everything

Reduce all ingredients to fine powder using mortar and pestle or electric grinder. Resins can be stubborn. Work patiently and they will cooperate.

Step 2: Blend Resins First

In a wooden or ceramic bowl, combine resins and gums by hand. Focus on your intention throughout. Visualize energy flowing from your hands into the mixture. This personal charge distinguishes handmade incense from commercial products.

Step 3: Add Remaining Dry Ingredients

Mix in powdered leaves, barks, flowers, and roots while maintaining focus on the blend's purpose.

Step 4: Add Liquids

Include any oils, wine, honey, or other liquids the recipe calls for. Use sparingly. If substituting oil for a missing herb, use only true essential oils. Synthetic fragrances produce acrid smoke.

Step 5: Add Boosters (Optional)

Some recipes call for powdered gemstones. Crush the stone with a hammer, then grind to powder. Add only a tiny pinch. Amber works as a general power amplifier.

Step 6: Empower and Store

Charge the completed blend with your intention. Store in labeled, airtight jars. Include the name and date.

Making Combustible Incense

Combustible incense requires more effort and specific ingredients. The process can be messy. Results vary with practice.

💭
On Saltpeter

Some practitioners avoid combustible incense because potassium nitrate has Mars associations. However, charcoal blocks also contain saltpeter. Consider your own practice and preferences.

Making Tragacanth Glue

This binding agent holds molded incense together. Available at herb shops and some pharmacies.

  1. Add one teaspoon ground gum tragacanth to one glass warm water
  2. Whisk until particles disperse
  3. Skim off foam
  4. Let sit until it becomes thick paste

One ounce of tragacanth absorbs up to a gallon of water over a week. Adjust thickness by adding water. Keep covered with damp cloth.

Gum arabic substitutes well if tragacanth is unavailable.

IngredientAmount

  2. Add essential oil and mix by hand
  3. Add empowered incense blend
  4. Weigh total mixture
  5. Add potassium nitrate at exactly 10% of total weight
  6. Mix thoroughly

Cone Incense Base #2 (Without Charcoal)

Charcoal requires frequent hand-washing and adds an off-note to the scent. This version uses wood instead:

IngredientAmount
Powdered sandalwood, cedar, pine, or juniper6 parts
Powdered benzoin, frankincense, or myrrh2 parts
Ground orris root1 part
Essential oil6 drops
Empowered incense blend3-5 parts

Match the wood to your incense recipe when possible. Sandalwood works as a neutral default.

Follow the same process: combine dry ingredients, add oil, add incense blend, weigh, add 10% saltpeter.

Shaping

Cones: Add tragacanth glue gradually until mixture reaches stiff dough consistency. Shape into cones on waxed paper. Dry 2-7 days.

Blocks: Roll dough ⅓ inch thick. Cut into 1-inch squares. Dry completely.

Sticks: Thin the mixture with extra glue. Dip bamboo skewers, wooden splints, or broom straws repeatedly, drying between coats. Or roll thin dough around sticks. Stand upright to dry.

Critical Rules for Combustible Incense

🚫
Saltpeter must be exactly 10% of total weight.

More burns too fast. Less may not burn at all.

Wood to Resin Ratio: Maintain at least 2:1 powdered wood to gum resins. Higher resin content prevents proper burning. Resins should never exceed one-third of the final mixture.

Sourcing Potassium Nitrate

  • Pharmacies (ask the pharmacist directly)
  • Chemical supply companies
  • Garden centers (sold as fertilizer)

Incense Papers

An alternative combustible form using tinctures and paper instead of molded dough.

Making the Papers

  1. Cut white blotter paper into strips (6 inches by 1 inch)
  2. Dissolve 1½ teaspoons potassium nitrate in ½ cup warm water
  3. Soak strips until saturated
  4. Hang to dry

Adding Scent

Tinctures work better than essential oils for papers. Gum and resin tinctures produce the best results.

  1. Empower your tincture
  2. Coat one side of each strip with tincture
  3. Dry completely
  4. Store in airtight containers

Use one tincture per paper rather than mixing.

Using Papers

Light one end, let it catch fully, blow out the flame, and place in censer. Papers can also substitute for charcoal: burn an unscented paper and sprinkle loose incense over it.

Ensure air circulates beneath the paper for consistent burning. Prop it in sand or salt, or rest it on a heat-safe object.

The Censer

Any heat-safe container works. Elaborate thuribles and simple bowls of sand function equally well. Choose based on personal preference and practicality. Sand or salt in a bowl protects surfaces from heat and provides a base for propping stick incense.

Burning Combustible Incense

Light the tip, wait for it to glow, blow out the flame, and place in censer. Visualize your goal as it burns.

Burning Noncombustible Incense

  1. Light a charcoal block and place in censer
  2. Wait for sparking to stop and the block to glow evenly
  3. Sprinkle a small amount of incense onto the block

Add more as smoke diminishes. Large amounts extinguish the charcoal. Resin-heavy blends burn longer than herb-heavy ones. Leave ash in place unless the scent turns unpleasant.

Charcoal Blocks

Self-igniting blocks contain saltpeter and spark dramatically when lit. Available at religious supply and occult shops in various sizes.

Light by holding with tongs or tweezers, or light directly in the censer. If a block resists lighting, it may have absorbed moisture. Keep trying until it glows evenly red.
