Recipe Grimoire

Infusion Guide

At its core, an infusion is about coaxing one substance to take on the energetic properties of another. Water and oil are the two most common receiving media, and if you have ever steeped a cup of loose-leaf tea, you already grasp the fundamental principle. Infusions are the gateway through which botanical power enters potions, ritual baths, floorwashes, magickal oils, and a wide range of other preparations.

Vinegar Infusions

Vinegar infusions use apple cider vinegar as the receiving medium. Pour one cup of apple cider vinegar over a quarter cup of your chosen herbs and allow them to steep for roughly a month, adjusting by a week or so depending on how much cellulose is in the plant material. Agitate the jar daily throughout the steeping cycle. When the cycle is complete, strain the liquid into a clean glass jar and compost the spent herbs.

Decoctions

Decoctions are necessary when working with the tougher, more rigid parts of a plant. Woody leaves, thick stems, dense roots, bark, and seeds all have a more resistant cell wall that a simple infusion cannot fully break down. To decoct, bring water to a boil, add the measured herbs, then reduce to a very low simmer and cover. Allow the mixture to gently release its properties over an extended period.

When preparing a magickal decoction, allow yourself to enter a meditative state and remain present with the herbs throughout the process. This has a practical benefit as well, since a heat source should never be left unattended. Once the decoction is finished, strain the plant material and compost it.

Water Infusions

The baseline ratio is one teaspoon of dried herb (or roughly one and a half teaspoons fresh) to each cup of boiling water. When blending several herbs together, scale each ingredient's portion individually rather than simply adding more total material.

Use a non-reactive vessel, glass rather than plastic. Pour boiling water directly over the botanicals and allow the mixture to steep for anywhere from five to fifteen minutes. Whether you strain out the solids or leave them in depends on the working. A floorwash performs better with the plant matter filtered away, while a potent ritual bath may gain strength from leaving it in, even though that makes cleanup considerably messier.

Concentrated Infusions

When you need something stronger, intended for baths or floorwashes rather than internal use, pack in a more generous amount of botanical material and pour just enough boiling water to submerge it. Let the brew cool to room temperature on its own, then strain or leave the solids depending on the application.

Cold Oil Infusions

A slower, no-heat alternative to the water bath method. Cover fresh or dried herbs in a carrier oil and store the jar in a dark, dry place such as a cupboard. Allow the herbs to soak for a minimum of two weeks and up to a month, then strain the oil through a cloth and transfer it to a dark, covered container.

Cold-infused oils retain the scent and some properties of the herb used. They can be used for culinary purposes when tonic herbs are chosen, and magickally to anoint candles, clear crystals, charge magickal tools, or consecrate a person by anointing pulse points.

Oil Infusions

Infusing oil takes a bit more effort than water, but nothing beyond a basic home kitchen setup. The working ratio is one ounce of fresh herb (or half an ounce dried) per cup of oil. Exceeding these proportions is not advisable unless a specific formula calls for it.

Place the plant material in a stainless steel bowl and cover it with oil. Warm gently over a water bath: a saucepan about a quarter full of simmering water, with the bowl sitting above the bottom rather than resting directly on it. A proper double boiler works too. Oil burns easily, so vigilance is non-negotiable. Keep the bowl covered, stir from time to time, and maintain a gentle simmer for around thirty minutes. If the oil starts smoking, bubbling hard, or scorching, the scent will go harsh and the infusion is lost.

After cooling, pour the oil through several layers of cheesecloth or a fine non-metallic strainer. Run it through a second time if needed. Getting every bit of plant matter out is critical, because any solids left behind will cause the oil to spoil. Essential oils or flower remedies called for in the recipe go in after straining, once the oil has cooled completely. Store the finished product in an airtight container.

A slow cooker offers a hands-off alternative to the water bath. Use the same proportions, set the temperature to low for about two hours, and strain exactly as described.

Solar-Charged Infusions

Steady warm, sunny weather opens the door to a method that produces unusually potent results, because the finished infusion carries the energy of the sun alongside the botanical properties. Pack the plant material into a jar with a snug lid, cover it completely with oil, and stir in one tablespoon of apple cider vinegar. Set the jar in direct sunlight during the day and move it to a warm cupboard at night (or, on warm evenings, under the moon). Maintain this cycle for a full two weeks, then strain as you would any oil infusion.

Flower Petal Infusions

Delicate petals respond best to a no-heat technique. Pull the petals apart and fill an airtight jar generously with clean, dry petals. Pour in an oil whose own scent is as neutral as possible, so the flower's fragrance comes through without competition. Let the petals soak for three days, giving the jar an occasional shake and keeping it in sunlight by day and a warm cupboard overnight.

After three days, strain the petals out through cheesecloth or a non-metal sieve, discard them, and keep the oil. Add a fresh batch of clean, dry petals to the same jar and pour the reserved oil back over them. Repeat the full three-day steeping and straining cycle. Each round deepens the fragrance. Three passes typically produces the intensity most practitioners want. After the final straining, make sure every last bit of petal is removed.

Preserving Infused Oils

Any sizable batch of infused oil that will not be used right away benefits from some form of natural preservation. A quarter teaspoon of simple tincture of benzoin (the plain variety, not the compound tincture sometimes labeled Friar's Balsam) per cup of oil is a reliable option. Benzoin comes from styrax gum, which carries its own traditional associations with purification and protection.

Vitamin E serves as another preservative, though much of what you find on the market is synthetically produced. Puncture a single capsule and squeeze the contents into each cup of oil. Jojoba, which is technically a liquid plant wax rather than a true oil, resists oxidation naturally and can be blended with other oils to extend their shelf life. Whenever you combine oils, keep the total oil-to-botanical ratio at the standard level.
