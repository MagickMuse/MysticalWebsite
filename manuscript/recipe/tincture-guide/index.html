Tincture Guide

Tinctures are scented liquids created by soaking dried plant materials in alcohol, which captures the fragrance. They work just as effectively as oils in magickal practice, stimulating ritual consciousness through scent and adding their energies to spellwork. The process is quick, easy, and often more reliable than other methods for capturing certain plant scents.

The Alcohol Question

The alcohol used for magickal tincturing must be ethyl alcohol (also called ethanol or grain alcohol). This is a natural product distilled from grain, sugar, or grapes.

Do not use isopropyl (rubbing) alcohol. It is distilled from petroleum products and has a sharp odor that makes it unsuitable for capturing fragrances.

For best results, you need alcohol of at least 70 percent strength (140 proof). Higher concentrations like 96 percent (192 proof) also work well. Standard vodka at 90 proof (45 percent) is not strong enough to produce the best scent.

Creating Tinctures

Materials Needed:

  • Dried plant material (fresh herbs will not work due to water content)
  • Ethyl alcohol (70% or higher)
  • Mortar and pestle
  • Small bottle with tight-fitting lid
  • Small funnel
  • Coffee filter or cheesecloth
  • Castor oil or glycerine (for stabilizing)

Process:

  1. Grind the herbs. Reduce dried plant material to the finest possible powder using a mortar and pestle. This is especially important for woods like sandalwood. Consider buying woods pre-ground.
  2. Empower the herb. Focus on the magickal goal of the tincture you are creating.
  3. Combine herb and alcohol. Pour the powdered herb into a small bottle with a tight-fitting lid. Using a funnel, add just enough ethyl alcohol to wet and cover the herb. Cap tightly.
  4. Shake daily. Shake the bottle vigorously every day for one to two weeks. Each time you shake, visualize the tincture's magickal goal.
  5. Strain. Using a coffee filter or cheesecloth in a strainer, strain the alcohol. Work quickly since alcohol evaporates when exposed to air.
  6. Test the scent. If the scent is not strong enough (common with materials other than gums and resins), add more herb to the bottle and pour the strained alcohol back over it. Repeat the shaking and straining process.
  7. Final test. Apply a drop or two to your wrist. Wait until the alcohol evaporates, then smell. Many tinctures do not smell true while still in the bottle.
  8. Finish and store. When the plant scent has completely overpowered the alcohol odor, filter one last time, add a few drops of castor oil or glycerine to stabilize the fragrance, and bottle. Label and store in a cool place out of direct sunlight.

Dilution Option

If you want to stretch your alcohol supply or capture plant scents that are not fully soluble in pure alcohol, dilute the ethyl alcohol with distilled water. Some scents transfer better in a water-alcohol mixture.

Using Tinctures

Do not drink magickal tinctures. Many plant materials used in tincturing are harmful if swallowed, and high-proof alcohol is not safe for consumption.

Safe uses include:

  • Incense papers: Tinctures work well for scenting incense papers, especially gum and resin tinctures or any heavily fragranced variety.
  • Anointing skin: Some tinctures can be applied to the skin to bring the plant's power within you. The alcohol evaporates quickly, leaving the scent. Always test on a small area first. Some tinctures irritate skin, leave stains, or create gummy residues (especially frankincense and copal). Lavender, clove, and patchouli tinctures are generally fine for anointing, but all alcohol-based tinctures can dry sensitive skin.
  • Anointing tools: Apply to magickal tools, sachets, candles, and jewelry.
  • Bath water: Add to ritual baths.
  • Blending: Mix with oils or add to ointments.

Virtually all ritual uses for oils also apply to tinctures.

Percolation Method

Percolation is an alternative to the standard soaking method that uses gravity to extract the soluble components of an herb. It is slightly more involved but produces results much faster.

Pack dried, powdered herbs into a cone-shaped container or closed funnel and moisten them with solvent until they reach a "wet sand" consistency. Cover the herbs with a mixture of alcohol and water and allow them to steep while controlling the flow of liquid through the material. Collect the percolation in a separate container below, and compost the spent herbs when the process is complete.

Blending Tinctures

Tinctures can be combined just like oils. Use equal parts unless your intuition suggests otherwise. All blends listed in the Recipe Grimoire that use oils can also be made with tinctures.

All herb tinctures are safe for skin anointing, though the alcohol content can be drying. Gum and resin tinctures may leave sticky residues.

As with all magickal herbal products, use tinctures with visualization and intention.

Caution

  • Always test tinctures on a small skin area before broader application
  • Some tinctures stain skin and fabric
  • Resin tinctures often leave sticky residues
  • Store away from heat and direct sunlight
  • Keep out of reach of children and pets
