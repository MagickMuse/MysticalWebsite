The Cross Quarter Days (also called White Sabbats or Greater Sabbats) are those that mark the halfway point between solstices and equinoxes. These four dates form a wheel of seasonal transitions, each one standing at the astronomical midpoint between the sun's major turning points.

The Four Cross Quarter Days

May 1st — also known as Beltane, May Day, and Walpurgis

August 1st — also known as Lammas and Lughnasadh

November 1st — also known as All Saints' Day and Samhain

February 1st — also known as Candlemas and Imbolc

Historical and Cultural Significance

Pagan festivals and holy days correspond to each of the Cross Quarter Days. These dates have been recognized as spiritually significant threshold times for millennia, marking the midpoints of the seasonal journey and the height of each season's particular energy.

In various parts of Europe—notably Scotland and Ireland—the Quarter Days were when rents fell due to the landlord, perhaps necessitating the need for some extra magic. This practical economic reality tied to the agricultural calendar made these days doubly significant: they were both spiritual observances and financial reckonings, times when the year's work was literally called to account.

Agricultural vs. Pastoral Traditions

While many modern Pagan traditions observe both the Cross Quarter Days and the solstices/equinoxes (creating the eight-spoke Wheel of the Year), historically different cultures emphasized different points:

Agricultural traditions tended to favor the solstices and equinoxes, marking the sun's journey and planting/harvest times.

Pastoral Celtic-rooted traditions acknowledged the Cross Quarter Days as their primary festivals, tied to livestock cycles, grazing seasons, and the movement of herds.

Most contemporary practitioners don't celebrate all eight equally, but choose those that resonate with their practice, ancestry, or local land rhythms.
