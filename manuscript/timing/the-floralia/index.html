The Floralia was the Roman festival honoring Flora, the goddess for whom flowers are named. (Yes, there is another goddess named Fauna—they are distinct deities.) Flora was indigenous to the Roman region although she was there before the Romans arrived. She is believed to be of Sabine or Oscan origin, representing a pre-Roman Italian goddess tradition.

The Goddess Flora

Flora is the spirit of blossoming flowers and springtime. She embodies the flowering of all nature, including human fertility and sexuality. Flowers indicate the promise of reproduction—flowers lead to fruit as surely as sexual intercourse leads to babies. Flora is the spirit who embodies both the pleasures of the moment and the promise of the future.

Timing and Origin of May Day

The Floralia is believed to be the ancestor of all May Day celebrations. It was celebrated annually from April 28th through the beginning of May, marking the peak of spring's flowering and the transition into the fertile abundance of early summer.

The Festival's Sacred Character

The Floralia honored the female body. Beautiful Flora may be understood as the original Queen of the May—the prototype for all later May Queens who would preside over springtime fertility festivals throughout Europe.

Celebration in the Nude

The festival was celebrated in the nude until the third century CE when Roman authorities demanded that revelers be clothed. This enforced modesty represented a shift in Roman attitudes toward sexuality and the body, as earlier Romans had no qualms about celebrating Flora's generative power through unabashed bodily display.

The festival survived in this clothed fashion until the next century, when all Pagan festivals were banned under Christian Roman rule.

Flora's Sacred Flowers

Although all flowers are sacred to Flora, her favorites are the fragile, transient bean blossoms. These delicate flowers bloom briefly but abundantly, embodying both the fleeting nature of pleasure and the promise of nourishment to come, as bean blossoms lead to protein-rich beans that sustain human life.

Connection to Beltane and May Day

The Floralia's timing and themes directly connect it to the Celtic festival of Beltane and to secular May Day celebrations. All share:

  • Celebration of fertility and sexuality
  • Honoring of a female figure (Flora/May Queen)
  • Emphasis on flowers and flowering
  • Joyful outdoor revelry
  • Recognition of spring's generative power

Modern Application

For contemporary practitioners, the Floralia offers a template for celebrating springtime sexuality, fertility, and the sacred feminine without shame or concealment. Flora reminds us that pleasure and reproduction are intertwined, that beauty serves a biological purpose, and that celebrating the body is a form of honoring the divine forces of creation.
