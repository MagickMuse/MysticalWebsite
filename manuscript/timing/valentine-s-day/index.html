The ancient rites of the Lupercalia cast a long shadow down the centuries, lingering well after Christianity took hold. Most of the festival was far too openly pagan for the zealous reformers of the early Church, who wanted something closer to their own values and morality to set in its place.

The Many Saints Called Valentine

The original Valentine was really an amalgam of two or three different men, all bearing the name and all martyred for the Christian cause; one of them was either put to death or buried in Rome on February 14. A good deal of spin was needed to make St. Valentine a convincing replacement for the Lupercalian excesses. One story held that he defied the Emperor Claudius, who had decreed that fighting men should abstain from sex lest their strength be sapped. Claudius had no love for the new religion, and to be a Christian then was hazardous to one's health, yet Valentine went on preaching despite the death sentence that hung over anyone caught doing so, and he is said to have presided over forbidden Christian weddings. Another legend tells that, awaiting execution, he fell in love with the jailer's daughter and left her a note signed "from your Valentine."

From Lottery to Love Notes

The Lupercalia had included a custom in which the young men drew lots for the available young women, the chosen couples spending the festival together with seduction as the clear aim. The Church devised a lottery of its own, though a tamer one: people drew the names of Christian saints from a hat and then tried to model themselves on those worthies for the coming year. Unsurprisingly this stirred far less excitement than its saucier forerunner, and the older practice of drawing lots to pair off couples returned in the fifteenth century, a kind of medieval speed dating, its intentions supposedly more innocent than those of the Lupercalia.

The memory of the Lupercalia proved very hard to stamp out, and today the Church rarely marks St. Valentine at all. As a secular celebration of love and romance, however, the day is a roaring success, and the heart, the great symbol of love, is seen everywhere.

The Birth of the Valentine Card

The little slips of paper bearing the women's names from those old lotteries are the distant ancestors of the Valentine cards that are now such an enormous business, and which by tradition should be sent anonymously. Valentine's Day is the second busiest card-sending season of the year after Christmas, so the original saints might be glad to be remembered even as the Church has largely let them go.
