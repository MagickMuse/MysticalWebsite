🍷Seder

Passover is a spring festival of liberation and renewal, marking the moment a people passed from bondage into freedom. At its heart lies a striking image of threshold protection: the blood of a sacrificed lamb smeared on the doorposts and lintels so that death would "pass over" the marked house and spare those within. The marking of a doorway to turn away harm is a motif of protective magick found far beyond any single tradition. The festival is intimately bound to the spring, and to the Christian festival of Easter, whose timing is reckoned in relation to it.

Religious Associations

Passover commemorates a crucial moment in Jewish history, effectively the birth of the Jewish nation as the Children of God. The Book of Exodus tells how God sent the final and most terrible plague upon Egypt, in which every firstborn male, human and animal, was killed, sparing no one, not even the pharaoh's family. The Israelites were told to smear their lintels with the blood of a sacrificed lamb so that the Angel of Death would "pass over" their homes and spare their children. The name of that sacrificed animal, the Paschal Lamb or Korban Pesach, may explain the name of the feast; the other explanation is the "passing over" itself. Sheltered inside their marked houses, the Israelite families ate the lamb while the plague raged outside, and the next day the grief-stricken Egyptians told them to leave.

Passover runs for seven or eight days, beginning on the fourteenth day of the month of Nisan, usually somewhere in April. Before it begins, every scrap of leavened or fermented food, biscuits, cakes, bread, even whiskey, is removed from the house. The first and last two days are holidays on which no one works, and families gather to share special meals. The most important of these is the Seder, eaten on the first night.
