Although much of what we know of ancient festivals is speculative, some facts about the Lupercalia are firm. The festival predates the founding of Rome in the eighth century BCE, a survival from a time when the site of what became one of the world's great cities was still a wilderness of hills haunted by wolves, where shepherds struggled constantly to protect their flocks from these predators.

Any creature that is feared is also revered, worshipped in an attempt to appease the collective spirit of the animal. So the wolf, a grave threat to people and livestock alike, was deified in many places. The God Lupercus was known as "the one who drives out the wolves," and part of the festival was quite literally about driving the wolf from the door. Lupercus is also identified with Faunus, or Pan, the God of shepherds. Later the wolf would return as a protective symbol in the legend of Rome's founding, when a she-wolf suckled Romulus and Remus in a cave on the Capitoline Hill.

Lupercalia traditionally fell on February 15, its purpose to purify the city by driving out evil spirits and demons and so to bring the joined blessings of fertility and good health. It was also called the Februa, from the Latin for "to purify," which in turn gives us the name of the month.

The celebrants were priests called the Luperci, the "Brothers of the Wolf," who wore only goatskins, in keeping with Faunus. Priestesses made cakes from the previous year's grain harvest and brought them to the Lupercal Cave on the Palatine Hill, where the ritual sacrifice of two goats and a dog opened the proceedings. These sacrificial animals were called Februa. Their blood was smeared on the foreheads of two of the Wolf Brothers, who were meant to laugh, and the sacrificial knife was then wiped with wool soaked in milk.

Once the animals had been cooked and shared in a feast, the Brothers dressed themselves in some of the goatskin and cut the rest into strips. They ran around the walls of the town, lashing the women who lined the route with the bloody thongs; since the goat was a potent symbol of sexuality and fertility, the women hoped to absorb these qualities. The name Lupercalia may even derive from the phrase luere per caprum, "to purify by means of the goat."

When the Romans moved through Europe, they carried some of the Lupercalian rites with them. One such custom was a kind of love lottery, in which young men drew the names of available women from a box and the pair were partnered for the duration of the festival, and perhaps longer.

Religious Associations

The wildly pagan character of the Lupercalia did not suit the newly established Christian faith, and in AD 496 the Pope outlawed the festival, naming St. Valentine the patron saint of lovers and setting his feast day on February 14, Valentine's Day. Many features of the ancient festival were absorbed into the new religion, though in greatly diluted form.
