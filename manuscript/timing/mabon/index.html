Astrological Timing and Associated Planets

Astronomical equinox marking the waning point of the sun. Sun enters 0 degrees of Libra in the Northern Hemisphere, Sun at 0 degrees of Libra in the Southern Hemisphere. The alignment of planets changes from year to year.

Pronunciation and Timing

Pronounced "may-bon." Mabon coincides with the autumn equinox, approximately September 21st, when day and night are temporarily equal. It is considered a time for contemplation and reflection. According to the old Celtic calendar, Mabon was the "second harvest" following Lughnasa.

The Sacred Symbolism

Mabon is a harvest festival in the manner of traditional Thanksgiving harvests. This is the time to honor the trees. The cornucopia serves as its primary symbol, representing the abundant harvest and the gratitude owed to the earth.

Spiritual Challenges for Modern Practitioners

Mabon is among the more difficult feasts for modern people to appreciate. It is more than just acknowledgement of the equinox and the coming of winter season. This festival was a crucial spiritual experience for those responsible for gathering their own food, whether through the seasonal harvest or the seasonal slaughter. Food production was once a communal activity; in essence, the harvest, whether flora or fauna, sacrifices their lives so that people can live. Mabon is the festival of thanksgiving and purification that attempts to maintain vital spiritual balance.

The Deity Mabon

The name "Mabon" derives from a hunting deity, the child of Modron. Modron and Mabon may be titles rather than names. Modron is believed to mean "mother" or "divine mother," while Mabon may mean "young man" or "son."

Mabon is simultaneously the youngest and oldest of souls. He is eternally young and embodies male fertility. Reminiscent of stories of changelings, Mabon was stolen from Modron three days after birth and disappeared for many years. He is believed to have been held captive in the otherworld. Mabon fades into the afterworld at Samhain to emerge in spring, a male counterpart to Persephone.

Modern Practice

Celebrating an Ancient Holiday in a Modern World

The equinox, with its trade of light for dark, stirs strong emotions of loss as summer's ease passes. While harvest and equinox celebrations have changed among modern Pagans, the core meaning remains: life is precious, and we are lucky to sustain it. Mabon acknowledges shared secular values to experience the full, rich scope of this spoke on the Wheel. This sabbat is both celebratory and solemn, happening at one of the busiest times of year. With themes of gratitude, death, grief, and looming winter, it's time to acknowledge mixed feelings and tend to our inner balance. Taking time out to hear ourselves and the Divine during action-packed phases is crucial. Because it's the harvest, even routine organizing to make life flow more easily in the cold season becomes a spiritual act. Even the most trivial action can become spiritual if performed with intent—preparing your home practically, planting winter bulbs with prayer, sweeping clutter to make room for new harvest fruits, or singing to the land while outside.

Modern Pagan Celebrations

Different Pagan traditions celebrate the Autumn Equinox in varied ways. Celtic Reconstructionists observe only the four major fire festivals (Samhain, Imbolc, Beltane, Lughnasadh) and don't do anything specific for Mabon. Pagan Pride is autumn's largest pan-Pagan celebration worldwide, where Pagans gather for public rituals and food drives to educate the public about Pagan religions and reduce xenophobia. Started in 1998 with seventeen US communities and one Canadian, it grew to over 40,000 attendees globally by 2005, borrowing its name from the Gay Pride movement to celebrate openness rather than hiding. Druids celebrate Alban Elfed ("Light of the Water"), observing darkness consuming more time than light and thanking the Mother for her abundance manifested in the harvest. Hellenic Pagans celebrate Boedromion (September), honoring different harvest gods for nine days with offerings and libations. Heathens call it Winter Finding, holding blots with offerings of ale and bread to Odin, followed by meals where participants pass drinking horns making boasts, vows, or honoring ancestors. Traditional Witches (Craft practitioners, Hedge Witches/Walkers) use shamanic methods and deep nature connection, celebrating season turns as befits their regions with private practices. Neopagans celebrate Mabon as a day of personal balance, designing their own rituals or honoring seasonal change through daily living. Eclectic Witches draw from multiple traditions, practicing Mabon if it speaks to them using self-designed rituals. Celtic Pagans may call it Feast of Avalon ("land of apples"). Stregheria (Italian Witchcraft) calls it Equinozio di Autunno, honoring earth as the Lord of Light becomes the Lord of Shadows and Janus dies and departs for the underworld.

Modern harvest festivals worldwide include: UK Harvest Festival (Sunday nearest full moon before equinox) with decorated churches, blessed farmer baskets distributed to the poor, and town celebrations; Polish Dozynki (mid-September to late October) with harvest masses, processions led by best harvesters wearing grain garlands, vodka toasts, and manor feasts; German Erntedankfest with church services displaying harvest bounty for the poor followed by civic processions with Pagan-rooted traditions; Oktoberfest (originally October 12, 1810, now September) celebrating Prince Ludwig's wedding and regional agriculture with beer, festival rides, and fairground trade. Jewish September holidays share spiritual parallels: Rosh Hashanah (New Year with shofar, challah, apples and honey); Yom Kippur (making amends and seeking forgiveness); Sukkot (expressly celebrating fall harvest, literally meaning "rejoice"); Simchat Torah (completing annual Torah reading, dancing with scrolls). US Thanksgiving descends from Harvest Home festivals, likely recreated by Pilgrims.

The Natural World at Mabon

September was a time for negotiating contracts and agreements—take cues from harvesters and work out deals with credit companies, landlords, or service people; read up on negotiation tactics and ask for raises; review bills for potential breaks. Harvest season coincides with hunting season—preserve garden goods for winter through canning, freezing, pickling, and drying (find dehydrators at thrift shops, instructions in cookbooks and online). Get outside while precious autumn lasts before winter arrives—walk in woods, enjoy foliage, observe animals preparing, identify plants. Go apple picking at orchards offering entertainment like apple canons, hayrides, and local wines; afterward make apple butter, pies, or dried apples. Visit wine or beer tastings at proliferating local vineyards and small breweries hosting autumn events and tours, celebrating sacred beverages in Pagan pantheons. Build bonfires (check city legal restraints and drought conditions) to roast items, meditate on flames, dance around while singing/chanting, toast to health and prosperity, and tell stories of old-world faeries or King Arthur.

The Prevailing Energies and Energetics of Mabon

Mabon calls for acknowledging mixed feelings and tending inner balance. Practical winter preparation becomes spiritual with intent: plant bulbs to bloom in spring while singing or praying to the land, winterize your house, review calendars and to-do lists to accomplish tasks in small chunks. Watch sunsets and sunrises—the sun feels especially precious in fall as land darkens each day. Spend a week watching sunset go down (sing or chant to it) or wake half an hour before sunrise to watch its progress, deepening connection with the dark half of earth's light cycle. Honor the Harvest Moon (September new crescent, August full moon, or September full moon depending on tradition), creating your own moon ritual or participating in Scottish divination where young people burnt grain bundles stuffed with peas and beans, then hid seeds in embers—whoever spotted the seed secured their future spouse's love.

Moon Magick at Mabon

The autumn equinox mirrors the last quarter moon phase. Like that half-lit moon suspended between fullness and darkness, Mabon brings a temporary equilibrium between light and shadow before the cycle descends into its darker passage. Day and night stand briefly equal, but from this point forward, darkness gains the upper hand.

The myth of Persephone's return to the underworld captures the spirit of this transition. Just as she surrenders the sunlit world above, practitioners at Mabon begin their own journey inward, confronting themes of loss, letting go, and surrender that deepen as winter approaches. This is the second of three harvests, and while gratitude still flows, the energy tilts unmistakably toward decline and introspection.

Reflect on which spiritual and physical aspects of your life need to be brought into balance before the final harvest at Samhain. Spells and practices centered on introspection, spiritual cleansing, and banishing are especially potent now. The balanced light of the equinox lends power to any working that seeks equilibrium, whether you are releasing what no longer serves you or fortifying what must endure the dark months ahead.

The Merging of Traditions

Traditional activities include: Host barbecues or potlucks-share food (wheat bread, corn, fresh greens, local meats) to celebrate harvest community. Go dancing-harvest suppers concluded with dancing; visit clubs, re-create Irish reels, watch Morris dancers, or try trance dancing for cathartic relief. Make corn dollies-fashion effigies from wheat or fruits/vegetables (find instructions online), use as centerpieces, keep in places of honor with small offerings, or include in games where people hide with water guns along procession paths or weigh dollies with stones symbolizing next year's heavy harvest. Make wreaths-using wheat or hay decorated with seasonal ribbons, hang over doors. Visit horses-Michaelmas/Riding Day featured horse races with man and woman on each horse; visit petting zoos, farms, or rescue shelters to do something nice for horses, then eat oatmeal for dinner. Have processions-organize parades to gardens for harvest-gathering tasks or fashion scarecrows for garden parades with children using pan drums, rattles, and kazoos, staking effigies in garden centers while cheering "Praise to the land god!" and "Praise to the harvest!" Make harvest baskets-take children to grocery stores to pick nonperishable items (canned goods, diapers, hygiene products) for local food shelves, assemble in baskets, pray that recipients receive good luck and bountiful harvests. Participate in Pagan Pride-volunteer at local events or organize rituals; if none exist locally, consider starting one. Perform sacrifice—set up altars with pictures of war heroes and emergency workers, share sacrifice stories, write thank-you letters, or make your own sacrifices through community service, visiting nursing homes, road-cleaning, sharing garden abundance with neighbors, hosting barbecues, or spending time at dying persons' bedsides.
