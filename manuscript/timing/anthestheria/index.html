🍷Anthestheria

Type
Holidays
Culture
Greek
Also called
Festival of Flowers
Entities1
Dionysus

Etymology and Name

Anthestheria translates as "the festival of flowers," marking the arrival of spring and the awakening of new life. The festival's name derives from the Greek word for flowers, reflecting its celebration during the blooming season when Dionysus returns to the world.

Festival Structure and Timing

The Anthestheria was held for three days in the month of Anthesterion (February/March), making it one of several annual festivals honoring Dionysus in Greece. According to some analyses, the festival corresponds with Dionysus' birth itself, honoring him in his triple aspect as infant, husband, and dying god.

The Wine Opening Ritual

Opening the new casks of wine involved profound symbolic significance. The wine casks were half-buried in Earth during fermentation, making their removal analogous to birth, specifically a Caesarian section that mirrors Dionysus' own birth story. His mother Semele died before he was born; the unborn child was surgically removed from her womb and sewn into his father Zeus' thigh, where he matured until the proper time.

Ritually unearthing and opening the casks re-enacts Dionysus' birth. His devotees share in the deity's essence by consuming the wine, accomplishing spiritual communion through this sacred drink.

Participants and Celebrations

Initially the festival was celebrated by women and children, though many aspects fall under mystery traditions requiring secrecy. The more female-oriented devotional practices ultimately became disreputable and illegal, leading to suppression of information.

The festival welcomed everyone to the celebration: men, ancestral spirits, dead souls, and various spiritual entities. The first two days honored the deity and new wine, punctuated by secret celebrations for mature women, initiation rituals for children, and general revelry.

The Dual Nature of Celebration

The Anthestheria operated on two distinct levels. It functioned as a public festival with communal celebrations open to all, while simultaneously serving as a mystery celebration for Dionysus' most devoted servants. The maenads and other initiates celebrated secret rites in his honor, apparently including the Great Rite, the sacred marriage between deity and devotee.

The festival's three nights were reserved for women's mysteries. The maenads celebrated privately in the mountains and forests, with little surviving information about these rites. Mature women were understood to play the role of brides of Dionysus during this time. Some legends suggest that Dionysus' marriage to Ariadne coincides with this festival, though other traditions place their wedding on May Eve.

Among the festival's primary goals was the stimulation of personal and agricultural fertility, connecting human sexuality, divine union, and the earth's productive capacity.

Changing Attitudes Toward the Dead

Rituals and celebrations evolve over time, and attitudes toward ghosts changed dramatically during the Anthestheria's history. What appears to have originally been a day devoted to honoring dead ancestors eventually transformed into a time of fear and protective measures.

Household doorposts were smeared with pitch in an effort to keep ghosts out. Many shrines and temples were kept tightly sealed on this day, allegedly to prevent ghosts from entering and lingering longer than their allotted time on Earth. Another explanation suggests that this day belongs only to Dionysus and Hermes, and therefore other spirits were prevented from leaving their shrines and joining the rituals.

Ritual Conclusion and Purification

The festival concludes when women carry pots of cooked grains and vegetables to the marshes to bid farewell to the dead with the ritual incantation: "Begone Ghosts! The Anthestheria is over!"

When rituals are conducted correctly, the end result is the removal and purification of malevolent ghosts, low-level spirits, and spiritual debris. This purification aspect makes the Anthestheria simultaneously a celebration of life, death, and renewal.

Themes and Spiritual Focus

The festival was devoted to birth, death, purification, and fertility, encompassing the full cycle of existence. It celebrated Dionysus as Lord of New Life and Wine, honoring both the deity's birth and the annual ritual opening of new wine casks.

Modern Practice

Modern versions and adaptations of the Anthestheria are celebrated by some Neo-Pagans, who draw inspiration from the ancient Greek practices while adapting them for contemporary spiritual work. These modern celebrations may incorporate wine blessing rituals, ancestral honoring, purification work, and celebrations of spring's arrival and fertility.

Reference: The Ancient Athenian Rite

In the Athenian calendar, the Anthesteria is held on the eleventh through the thirteenth of Anthesterion. It is at once the Festival of Flowers, a feast of the dead, and a drinking festival centred on Dionysus Limnaios, of the marshes.

Pithoigia, the Opening of the Jars

The first day is Pithoigia, the Opening of the Jars, the broaching of the new wine. Celebrants gathered near the temple and opened the wine-jars, pouring libations to Dionysus and drinking the rest.

Khoes, the Day of Cups

The second day is Khoes, the Day of Cups. Drinking matches were held, with the prize being a skin of wine, and the day carried an erotic atmosphere. On the following evening, at the point when it was almost the beginning of the next ritual day, a sacred marriage was performed between the queen and the God, in the inner chamber of the temple, which was opened only for that night.

Khutroi, the Day of Pots

The third day is Khutroi, the Day of Pots, devoted to the cult of the dead. Pots containing cooked vegetables and seeds, the traditional food of the dead, were set out for the wandering spirits. Precautions were taken to keep the spirits from coming too near, and celebrants chewed hawthorn, smeared their doors with pitch, and tied ropes around the temples. At the close of the festival the spirits were driven out with the cry, Out you Keres, it is no longer Anthesteria!

On a normal calendar. The Anthesteria falls on the eleventh through the thirteenth of Anthesterion, generally landing in February or early March of the Gregorian calendar, and shifts each cycle.
