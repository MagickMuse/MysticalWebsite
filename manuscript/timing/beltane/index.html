Historical and Cultural Background

Etymology and Name Variations

Beltane is the conventional modern spelling, while Bealtaine represents the traditional Irish spelling. In Wales, the festival is known as Calan Mai. The name derives from multiple possible sources, with general consensus that Beltane means "bright fire".

The name may derive from "bel" (light) or "bil" (luck). It may also honor the Celtic deity Bel or Belenus. Another possibility suggests that Bel is either derived from or identical to the pan-Semitic fertility deity Baal, connecting this Celtic festival to broader ancient Near Eastern fertility traditions.

The Celtic Year: Dark Half and Bright Half

The ancient Celtic year was divided into two halves rather than four seasons, based on limited surviving information. This structure differs fundamentally from the modern Western quarterly division:

The dark half is initiated with Samhain, corresponding to October 31st (Halloween).

The bright half is initiated by Beltane, corresponding to April 30th (May Eve).

This calendar structure may be visualized as akin to a yinyang symbol, with Beltane proclaiming the start of the bright yang portion. This division gave Beltane added resonance in Celtic lands as it inaugurates the second half of the year, making it metaphysically understood as the moment when Earth's generative, reproductive, and sexual energies are at their peak.

Historical Timing and Observance

Beltane officially begins at moonrise on the evening before the first day of May. As the Celtic fire festival marking the beginning of summer, it is the Celtic festival corresponding to May Eve. Rituals are held during Beltane to enhance and increase the fertility of land, people, and animals. A celebratory feast welcomes the newly awakened Earth. Witches and fairies are out and about tonight.

Much of what we know about Celtic festivals derives from Ireland, although the Celts once dominated much of Europe. Indications suggest similar festivals were held elsewhere in Celtic Europe, evidenced by the prevalence of May Day celebrations throughout the continent.

The Sacred Bonfires

Fire may be understood as a little bit of the sun on Earth. In the spirit of the metaphysical adage "as above, so below," the magical power of the sun was rekindled and enhanced by the Beltane bonfires. These bonfires were known as "bel-fires" or bale fires, joyfully celebrating and proclaiming the return of fertility (life) to Earth.

Beltane bonfires were ritual fires traditionally kindled by friction or by sparks from a flint. To this day, some traditionalists resist the allure of matches or lighters and insist that others do so as well.

The bonfires convey the magical, healing, energizing force of fire. To benefit from this positive radiant energy, people dance around the fire, jump over it, crawl through it once it gets low, and drive their livestock through. Although any animal can benefit from the bale fires, cattle, the sacred cows so intrinsic to Irish myth, are especially associated with Beltane. When there are twin fires or multiple fires, people dance between them and lead animals between. The ultimate goal of these rituals is disease prevention, termination of bad luck, and renewal of fertility and creativity.

Sacred Sexuality and Festival Character

Although a sacred day, Beltane was a happy, raucous holiday, not a serious, solemn one. It is impossible to celebrate Earth's sexuality without simultaneously reveling in human sexuality. Beltane was one of those anarchic festivals where everyday constraints were thrown to the winds.

The Christian Church would eventually condemn the carnal licentiousness of Beltane rites, accusing the populace of indiscriminate copulation. Although defamatory, these accusations weren't without a vestige of truth, however disapproval stems from perspective and perhaps jealousy. After all, some people were having fun when others weren't.

Children whose birthdays fell near Imbolc (which occurs precisely nine months later) were affectionately known as "Beltane babies" and were considered to be special children with strong psychic powers and favored by the fairies.

Witches, Persecution, and Sacrifice

Beltane was understood as a witches' festival, when witches came out to play, as well as a day sacred to devotees of the Fairy Faith. According to Sir James Frazer, author of The Golden Bough, "every woman who fetches fire on May Day" was considered a witch in sixteenth-century Ireland.

Perhaps their very visibility on this date made those with magical or Pagan inclinations vulnerable to those with other orientations. Notions of sacrifice, and especially of sacrificial witches, permeate many historic Beltane traditions, and May became a time when witches and their animal allies were persecuted:

Cats and rabbits discovered in the fields in Ireland during Beltane were traditionally understood as witches in disguise and frequently killed on the spot, often by being tossed into the bonfires.

Litters of kittens born during the entire month of May were feared as potential witches' familiars and summarily drowned.

"Burning the witches" persisted in the Scottish Highlands into the eighteenth century. Young men took bits of the burning Beltane bonfires onto pitchforks, then ran through the fields shouting "Fire! Fire! Burn the witches!" The fire scattered through the fields enhanced their fecundity, which it actually does.

Modern Incorporation

The joyful aspects of Beltane have been incorporated into contemporary Wicca. Aspects of the festival devoted to the sun, human sexuality, and the regeneration of life and magic power are emphasized.

Traditional Foods: Beltane Cake and Caudle

Beltane Cake

Special cakes are traditionally part of Beltane rituals, usually featured at the conclusion of the celebration. Rituals and the style of cake depend on region and tradition. Sometimes one large cake is made and shared, sometimes smaller individual cakes are distributed.

The tradition emanates from the Scottish Highlands and the actual recipe for the cake, also known as a bannock, derives from Scottish cuisine. Usually made from Pine and/or Barley flour, milk, and eggs, cakes traditionally have a scalloped edge and are decorated with knobs, frequently nine knobs or multiples of nine. A thin batter made of beaten eggs, milk or cream, and a little oatmeal was often brushed over the top of the cake before baking. As Beltane is a solar feast, those Neo-Pagans who incorporate this ritual add spices associated with the sun to the cakes, for instance cinnamon, cloves, and saffron.

Various rituals and traditions involving these cakes exist:

The Blackened Bit: At the close of Beltane festivities, the Master of Ceremonies or ritual officiant presided over the distribution of one single cake, large enough for a piece for every participant. One bit was previously marked, usually with charcoal (hence its name—"the blackened bit"). The cake was sliced and distributed; whoever received the blackened bit became the Beltane Carline.

Nine Knobs Ritual: In a ritual traditionally combined with the creation and consumption of the Beltane Caudle, each participant was given an individual oat and/or barley cake decorated with nine raised square knobs. Each person faced the central fire, so that they stand in a circle. Together they break off knobs, one by one, throwing them over their shoulder without looking back. The throwing of each knob is accompanied by a different invocation.

The first invocations are dedicated to protective spirits; then propitiatory offerings are made to predatory ones. Descriptions of offerings to protective spirits are somewhat vague: written reports skirt exactly who is being thanked. However, offerings of propitiation are made directly to the presiding spirits of predatory animals. Thus:

  • "This I dedicate to you, preserve my horses."
  • "This I dedicate to you, preserve my sheep."
  • "This I give to you, Fox, spare my sheep!"
  • "This I give to you, Hooded Crow, spare my chicks!"

Following these offerings, each individual consumed the remainder of their cake and shared the Beltane Caudle.

This tradition originally derives from the Scottish Highlands where participants were primarily concerned about agriculture and livestock. By adjusting the invocations, however, it is easily adapted to suit participants with other concerns.

Divination Cakes: Sometimes Beltane cakes were intended for divination, not consumption. Participants rolled their individual cakes down a hill. Anthropologists suggest that this action mimics the sun's motion and/or recalls the ancient Druid tradition of rolling burning wicker wheels down hills. If the cake arrives at the bottom of the hill unbroken, the person to whom it belonged could anticipate a happy, fortunate year. If the cake broke en route, misfortune was predicted.

Beltane Caudle

The word "caudle" is related to "cauldron" and describes a concoction traditionally made from blending eggs and grain with ale or wine, brewed in a cauldron. This blending of beverages and grains was once quite popular.

The Beltane Caudle was incorporated into a Scottish Highland ritual also involving Beltane cakes. Sir Walter Scott described the traditional ritual: a square trench was cut into the ground, leaving the turf in the middle. A wood fire was built for the cauldron, which was filled with eggs, butter, oats, and milk. Copious quantities of beer and wine were added: each participant was expected to contribute his share. (The suggestion is made that much beer and wine were also enjoyed independently prior to and during ritual preparations.)

The ritual began by spilling some of the caudle on the ground as a libation. Individual Beltane cakes were distributed; the ensemble then made various invocations to protective and dangerous spirits. Once the invocations were complete, the caudle was consumed. The remainder of the Beltane cakes might be consumed or incorporated into different rituals.

Ways to Celebrate Beltane

It's best to celebrate Beltane outside in order to appreciate nature's fullness. Because Beltane is a fertility holiday, many of its rituals contain sexual symbolism.

Maypole Garland Ritual

The Maypole, around which young people dance, is an obvious phallic symbol. Witches often decorate the Maypole with flowers in recognition of the earth's beauty and fruitfulness. Sometimes a woman who seeks a partner will toss a circular garland over the top of the pole, signifying the sacred union, as a way of asking the Goddess to send her a lover.

Ribbon Wishes on a Tree

Write wishes on colorful ribbons and tie them on a tree. As the wind moves the ribbons, your wishes are carried to the spirits.

Cauldron Jumping for Fertility or Creativity

Another fertility ritual utilizes the cauldron, symbol of the womb. Those who wish to become pregnant build a small fire in the cauldron, then jump over it. If you prefer, you can leap over the cauldron to spark creativity in the mind instead of the body.

Prosperity Magick

Beltane's connection with the earth and fullness makes this sabbat an ideal time to perform prosperity magick. Incorporate peppermint, parsley, lavender, alfalfa, cedar, or money plant into your spells. This is also a good time to make offerings of food and wine to Mother Earth and the nature spirits.

Modern Beltane Practice

Common Themes

Modern Beltane festivities have a lighthearted and raucous feel. Rituals focus on fertility, prosperity, health, protection, purification, and growth as summer is ushered in. Spellwork gravitates toward love, passion, romance, and sex magick. Music, dancing, feasting, singing, picnicking, and fireside rituals are frequently incorporated. Flowers and vegetation symbolize Nature's abundance and fertility.

Most celebrations are held outdoors when possible, with merrymaking and magick throughout night and day. Sex, love, laughter, romance, and seasonal celebration take center stage. Nature walks, picnics, potlucks, Maypole dances, and romantic games are popular activities.

Rural Celebrations

Rural Pagans focus on Nature's fertility and protection of family, homestead, and property. Large fires are lit for magickal rituals protecting livestock and crops. Some drive livestock between two fires to protect animals from disease and injury, following ancient Celtic practice. Walking property boundaries while performing protective rituals and uttering blessings is common. Homes and altars are decorated with local wildflowers and vegetation. Most rural Pagans celebrate alone or with small groups of immediate family and nearby friends.

Urban Celebrations

City Pagans face challenges—difficult to build bonfires among skyscrapers, sparse vegetation in concrete jungles. Creative solutions include using candles and fireplaces instead of bonfires, visiting florists for fresh foliage, or using artificial flowers. Many celebrations occur indoors or in community parks. Urban rituals typically focus on personal goals: self-improvement, creative fertility, purification, romance. Houseplants and pets may feature in magick and ritual.

Celtic Reconstructionist Practice

Aims for historical accuracy, timing celebrations by observing Nature. When hawthorn trees flower, Beltane has arrived. Alternatively, celebrated on the first full moon after hawthorn blossoms or when sun reaches 15 degrees Taurus. Often use Gaelic name Lá Bealtaine.

Two ritual bonfires are lit; livestock, pets, and people pass between them for blessings and protection. Candles are lit from the main fire and brought home, reflecting the tradition of relighting hearth fires from sacred Beltane flames. Urban practitioners use torches or candles.

Traditional Celtic foods and beverages are prepared. Flowering tree branches are brought inside. Rowan crosses (equal-armed crosses made from two rowan wood pieces tied with red thread) are hung for magickal protection. May bushes are used, deities honored. Wells are visited for prayers, praises, and offerings.

Wiccan Practice

Focus on fertility and sexuality, with themes of abundance, protection, purification, growth. Maypole dances often take center stage at large community gatherings. Bonfires, revelry, merrymaking, and mayhem abound. Atmosphere balances reverence with fun and frivolity. Bawdy behavior, friendly flirtation, and sexual discourse become standard.

Some covens reenact the myth of lusty young god mating with fertile maiden goddess through ritual sex or symbolic representation (wand/scepter plunged into cup). This ritual union brings experiential understanding of earth's fertilization.

Ritual clothing includes flower garlands and bright colors—green and yellow predominate, with pink, white, and pastels invoking spring, purity, romance. Sometimes skyclad. Homes and altars dressed in flowers and spring colors.

Solitary Wiccans may meditate in sunshine or by candlelight. Prayers and offerings (food, water, flowers, herbs) given to God, Goddess, and sometimes faeries. Rituals are joyful, reverent, celebratory. Magick focuses on love, romance, abundance, growth, prosperity. Popular time for divination using tarot, runes, pendulums, or I Ching.

Heathen Practice

Celebrates Germanic holiday Walburg (Walpurgis Night) on April 30 and May Day on May 1. A time when magick is afoot and spirits wander. Mark occasion with great bonfire, simple feast, and toasts to Wotan (Odin) and goddesses of magick.

Modern Druid Practice

Celtic Revivalist style, adapting ancient beliefs for current times. Often eclectic, merging traditions. May honor goddess Danu (earth mother) and god Belenos (sun king). Celebrate fertility, union, sexuality, protection, purification. Time to honor the dead. Faeries make extra mischief as veil between worlds thins (like Samhain). Some wear bells to keep naughty faeries at bay. Prayers for health, prosperity, protection, love. Offerings of bread, beer, or mead left for gods and dead.

Traditional Witchcraft Practice

Based on pre-Christian animism, traditional folk magick, often polytheistic. Varies by place, local culture, environment. Some celebrate solstices/equinoxes, some celebrate cross-quarter days, most don't celebrate both. Agricultural traditions favor solstices/equinoxes; pastoral Celtic-rooted traditions acknowledge cross-quarter days like Beltane. Celebrations include bonfires, rituals, orgiastic revelry, reverence, magick.

Neopagan Practice

Broad category of nature-based spirituality. May celebrate alone or in groups. Daytime picnics popular at public parks, mountainsides, riversides. Potlucks standard. Nature walks before or after feasts. Rituals and magick vary in formality and form. Solitary Neopagans may follow traditional paths or craft personal proceedings based on taste, need, ability, intuition.

Eclectic Witchcraft Practice

Draw from various traditions and personal systems. Emphasize natural energies and seasonal tides, working with moon, sun, stars, earth, elements. Rituals emphasize springtime energies. Fires lit in solidarity with sun's rising strength. Themes: abundance, growth, prosperity, fertility, love, sexuality. Rituals very joyful. Fresh vegetation adorns altars and practitioners. Sometimes plants are all that's worn—or nothing at all.

Consider Beltane perfect time to honor union of energies manifesting Nature's blossoming. Sex magick practiced solo, with partner, or multiple partners—strongly magickal focus or highly reverent communion with divine. May involve group stimulation directing energy toward shared goal, or partners representing lunar Triple Goddess and solar Horned God. Solitary practitioners may self-stimulate while invoking god of spring. Practices vary widely.

Modern Beltane Gatherings and Festivals

Beltane Fire Festival—Edinburgh, Scotland

Held on Calton Hill April 30. Modern arts and cultural event organized by Beltane Fire Society. "Investigative theatre" with planned and spontaneous theatrical performances, drumming, ritual throughout the night. Great procession led by May Queen circles the hill. Symbolic reenactment of Green Man's death and rebirth. Participants follow drumming to find spontaneous revelers and impromptu performances. Attracts 12,000+ people annually.

Beltania—Florence, Colorado

Pagan celebration and music festival in early May. Four-day festival: camping, rituals, workshops, live music, large-scale Maypole dance with 100+ ribbons. Green Man and May Queen crowned and celebrated. Drum circles, dancing into the night. Skyclad camping area (18+) for magick and mischief. Bardic circles, spontaneous singing, storytelling around campfire. Hosted by Living Earth, open Neopagan group and community church in Denver metro area.

May Day Parade and Festival—Minneapolis, Minnesota

40+ years of community-wide celebration. Parade, ceremony, festival draw 50,000+ participants and spectators. Puppets, dancers, performers in giant masks (some 10+ feet tall) fill streets with music, joy, laughter. Free Speech section for community groups' causes.

Public ceremony to awaken slumbering summer. Pageant with dancers, live orchestra, four giant puppets (prairie, sky, river, woods). 200+ participants. Culminates in drumming while red sun flotilla paddles across lake to awaken Tree of Life—all-ages Tree of Life ceremony. Festival follows with live bands, dancing, food, poetry, grassroots organization booths, canoe rides.

May Day Fairie Festival—Spoutwood Farm, Pennsylvania

Annual event early May at organic farm outside Glen Rock. Celebrates summer's arrival and return of faeries and nature spirits. Arts, crafts, live music, dancing, storytelling, faery and gnome habitat tours, faery tea parties, Maypole. Three-day family-friendly festival attracts 16,000+ people annually.

Blue Ridge Beltane Festival—Greenville, Virginia

Three-day camping event. Rituals, drumming, workshops, vendors, bands, fire spinners. Highlights: lighting Bel-Fire, Maypole dance, Inner Sanctum (adults 18+ for sacred sexual workshops/rituals/encounters). Youth zone with kid-friendly arts, crafts, games, rituals.

The Beltane Gathering—Darlington, Maryland

Five-day celebration of sacred sexuality and personal freedom at 200-acre retreat center. First week of May. Group rituals, workshops, presentations on sacred sex topics, sensual feast. Vendors sell sex toys and magickal tools. Camping event and magickal retreat. Public sex allowed, kink welcome.

Beltain Festival—Butser Ancient Farm, England

Near Petersfield, Hampshire. All-ages crowd. Hog roast, picnicking, live music. Children craft flower and wicker crowns. Star of show: 30-foot wicker man set ablaze. Festivalgoers write hopes and wishes on paper, stuff inside wicker framework. Burning symbolizes end of winter, promise of summer. Met with great rejoicing, loud noise, raucous celebration.

Beltane—Thornborough Henge, North Yorkshire, England

Annual since 2004 at prehistoric site. Free gathering offering alternative to commercial festivals. May Day fool, mystery play, fire-lighting ceremony. Focuses on Brigantia (goddess of old northern England kingdom). Revelers often camp overnight. Thornborough Henges: largest group of prehistoric earthworks in Britain, extensive henges/stone monuments/Cursus. Dated to Neolithic period (3000-2000 BCE).

Popular for handfastings—jumping over fire, jumping over broom, binding lovers' hands. Costumes encouraged (flower garlands popular). Drumming, fire rituals, fun, fellowship, Beltane romance. 500+ attendees from region.

Modern Secular Celebrations

Many communities honor May Day with secular celebrations focused on entertainment rather than spirituality, but ancient Pagan remnants remain discernible.

United Kingdom

Many towns host community May Day festivals centered around grand Maypole. Bonfire rituals still traditional. Limerick, Ireland maintains continuous community bonfire ceremonies into modern times.

Central and Northern Europe

Walpurgis Night celebrated April 30 or May 1. Fireside dancing, feasting, merrymaking prevalent.

Germany

Walpurgis Night and May Day (April 30-May 1). Bonfires lit, fireside purification rituals. Celebrations get rowdy, pranks common—harmless acts like rearranging things or swapping neighbors' patio furniture, but also more raucous/malicious pranks.

May Day honors worker's rights—rallies, marches, demonstrations. Maypoles prominent. Public spring festivals.

Small "maypoles" as love tokens—decorated branch placed in desired lover's garden as declaration of affection, believed to bring luck in romance. Many spend time outdoors enjoying sunshine, anticipating warmer days.

Czech Republic

April 30: purification and celebration. Straw and cloth fashioned into "witch," tossed into fire to symbolically burn dark and dismal. Sometimes brooms replace witches. Large meals near bonfire, dancing, singing. May 1 celebrated as day of love, couples visit local parks together.

Estonia

Spring Day begins sundown April 30 through May 1. Carnival atmosphere. Streets filled with revelers dressed as witches reflecting superstition that night is when real witches gather en masse for ritual and magick. Celebrate spring's arrival, magick and mayhem in the air. Humbler classes get extra regard—students and workers honored with day off.

Finland

May ushered in with large parties and public celebrations. Parades for wider community. Smaller groups (friends, families, students) gather for picnics. Mead-like beverage called sima often served.

Newfoundland

Still local custom in some places to decorate May Bush—small tree or shrub with brightly colored ribbons and trimmings to herald and celebrate spring.

France

First day of May: Lily of the Valley sold along streets. Flower symbolizes springtime, believed to attract good luck. Legend: flower first sprang when Eve exiled from Garden of Eden—wherever Eve's tears fell, Lily of the Valley burst from earth.

May Day is national Labour Day (Fête du Travail). Worker's rights demonstrations. Working classes celebrate day off as banks and most stores close.

Suggested Activities

Beltane reminds us of our dependence on earth's fertility. As tenders of crops, caretakers of animals, conscious beings setting boundaries of possibility, humans have important job ensuring survival—both through physical/mundane actions and magickal abilities expanding perception and potential for greater beauty and bigger bounty.

Review Your Defenses

Strong solar energies make Beltane perfect for protective magickal workings.

Review both magickal and mundane defenses. Inspect home's window/door locks, make repairs. Hang small hawthorn piece over each entrance for magickal protection. Assess personal habits and lifestyle—adopt extra precautions for dangerous situations or risky behavior. Walk with buddy at night instead of alone. Consider self-defense class. Use protection during sex. Make personal protective charm: tie pinch of salt in white cloth (associated with protective solar energies). Carry to repel danger.

Get Creative

Vibrant, creative, fertile energy flow helps creative projects take flight. Write book or poem, finish painting from closet, use that art clay. Draw picture, take photos, make music, choreograph dance, crochet hat, decorate cake.

As you create, envision current wishes coming true. Feel and experience what it will feel like when that happens. Let energy and emotion flow through body into art. You'll have Beltane masterpiece reminding you that you're a creative spirit, like Mother Earth.

Enjoy Nature

Renew and strengthen connection to Nature. Tend outdoors—weed, prune, water, fertilize. Plant flowers, vegetable seeds, or tree. Take nature walk, enjoy picnic in park. Notice plants, sounds, scents, sensations. Touch vegetation. Enjoy sunshine on skin. Observe—see yourself as integral, beautiful part of it all.

Can't get outdoors? Get close to houseplants, gently stroke foliage or water while thinking about how plant lives and grows through cooperation of sun, water, plant, soil. No houseplants? Gaze out window, take in what you can. Close eyes, remember what you see/hear/sense. What lies beyond your view? Envision clearly, imagine yourself in this wild of your own creation.

Get Spiffy

Purification time—drive away impurities and baneful influences from people, animals, land, possessions, homes. Winter's threat passed, instinct to shake off lingering darkness and danger. Urge to spruce up ourselves and homes with springtime makeover.

Ritual bath: place sea salt, lavender, or purifying minerals/herbs in bath water. Visualize physical or spiritual impurities flowing out of body into water. After bath, treat yourself to spiffy springtime outfit and new haircut/style. Feel renewed, refreshed, in tune with Beltane's fertile, vibrant, thriving energy.

Fire rituals: candles or campfires. Circle fire or carefully jump over flames to purify mind, body, spirit.

Home purification: Clear clutter, remove dust to banish blues. Vase of fresh flowers energizes living space, reminds of Nature's beauty. Sage fumigation for home, ritual space, magickal tools—chases away unwanted influences, clears stagnant energies. Alternative: salt water or mist of lavender essential oil and spring water. Spritz perimeter of each room, beginning east, proceeding clockwise.

Go Crazy

Excellent time for adventure. Rash, bold, spontaneous behavior widely embraced. Impromptu trip to exotic locale, short road trip to neighboring town, muster courage to hit on special someone. Try daring sport, new hobby, new class. Face a fear. Be daring, embrace fun and carefree spirit. Empowers you to make most of exciting, dynamic time of year.

Make Way to the Water

Wells, rivers, lakes, sacred water sources often visited to honor earth's fertility. Water symbolic of femininity, life, creativity, goddess energies—perfect accompaniment to Beltane proceedings. Rituals and picnics near large bodies of water, offerings at holy wells and sacred springs.

Make trip to water source. Admire water's beauty, make wish, leave gift or two. Ensure gift isn't pollution—natural items like rocks or flowers, small amounts of birdseed, fruit, vegetables are appropriate offerings.

Moon Magick at Beltane

Beltane mirrors the waxing gibbous moon. Past the equinox, daylight has overtaken night, and the energy of the season tilts decisively toward partnership. Just as the waxing gibbous swells with gathered momentum, the cross-quarter fire of Beltane pulses with the sense that combining forces now can produce something far bigger and more beautiful than anything attempted alone.

The moons of this season carry spring's familiar themes but layer them with an extra emphasis on beauty, love, and sensuality. In the spirit of partnership, connect with nature allies and use fresh, living ingredients in your spells. Cast magick for love, for cocreation with the universe, or for blessing any endeavor that needs a surge of fertile energy. The fae are thought to be especially active now, and their wild, creative current can amplify lunar workings.

With the light still growing toward the solstice, the time is ripe to pour intention into whatever you want to see flourish. Let your moon magick at Beltane carry a sensuous edge: engage the senses, explore the lushness of living, and trust that the merging of energies will summon abundance.

Traditional Beltane Activities

String Beads or Flowers for a Blessing

String beads or flowers while speaking:

May the God and the Goddess and the power of the Elementals bless me now and always be with me.

Make Paper May Day Baskets

Secretly hang on the doorknobs of neighbors:

  1. Fold a square piece of decorated paper diagonally
  2. Glue or tie a yarn handle through punched holes
  3. Place a few spring flowers inside the basket
  4. Hang on doorknobs of front doors, mailbox magazine holders, or other available features

Bonfire Wishing

Jump over a bonfire or campfire and make a wish as you jump.

Beltane Bread

Ingredients:

  • 4 cups sifted flour
  • ½ cup ground almonds
  • 2 cups sugar
  • 1 tube of almond paste
  • ½ tsp. baking powder
  • 1 tsp. cinnamon
  • 5 eggs

Directions: Preheat oven to 375°F. Combine ingredients. Work dough to medium soft and shape into flattened balls. Place on ungreased cookie sheet. Bake until golden brown, about 20 minutes. Cool, then ice with a white Solar Cross. May also be done as a loaf.

Quick Almond Biscuits

  1. Measure Bisquick per box recipe
  2. Add small portions of almond extract, sugar, cinnamon, and 1–2 eggs
  3. Bake as directed
