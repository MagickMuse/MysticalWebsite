🐺The Lupercalia

The Lupercalia was a complex and ancient Roman festival of purification that also served to celebrate and generate human fertility and honor wolves. Although standard explanations suggest that this festival of fertility and purification was initiated by Romulus and Remus in honor of the she-wolf who rescued and nursed them, the festival is believed to be far more ancient.

Presiding Deities

The deities who preside over the Lupercalia are Juno and the wild horned spirit Faunus.

Faunus: The Wild Horned One

Faunus is the primordial spirit of wild nature, the male generative principle. He also mediates the balance between wolves and their prey and between shepherds and wolves. This dual role as protector of both predator and prey reveals Faunus as a deity of natural balance—understanding that the wild ecosystem requires both hunters and hunted.

Juno: Matron of Rome

Juno, the ancient Matron of the city of Rome, is the only deity with two months named in her honor: the eponymous June and February. Juno—or Uni—was an Etruscan deity whose presence in the Eternal City pre-dates the Romans. Her authority and importance thus extend back into deep time, to the indigenous peoples of the Italian peninsula.

Purification for the New Year

The religious ceremonials at the heart of the Lupercalia purified the land and its inhabitants for the New Year. This is crucial to understand: February was the last month of the ancient calendar; the New Year began with the vernal equinox, when the sun entered the first astrological sign, Aries.

Thus the Lupercalia served as a final cleansing before the new annual cycle began. It cleared away the accumulated impurities, misfortunes, and stagnant energies of the old year, preparing both land and people to enter the new year fresh and fertile.

Etymology of February

The name "February" derives from two meanings:

Februare: To Cleanse

The Latin word februare means "to cleanse" or "to purify." This etymology makes February's essential character clear: it is a month of purification, of clearing away the old to make room for the new.

Juno Februata

Juno Februata has two interpretations:

  • Juno of the Fevers of Love: Acknowledging the passionate, sexual energy of the fertility rites
  • Juno Who Provides Purification: Emphasizing her role in the cleansing ceremonies

Both meanings capture essential aspects of the Lupercalia: the purification that clears away obstacles to fertility, and the fevered passion that ensures conception.

The Nine Days of the Festival

During the nine days of the Lupercalia, from February 13th through the 21st, dead souls wandered the Earth, consuming the essence of the food and drink that the living offered them.

This timing places the Lupercalia within the larger festival of the Parentalia (February 13-21), the Roman festival honoring dead parents and ancestors. The Lupercalia thus intertwined purification, fertility, and ancestor veneration—cleansing the living, honoring the dead, and ensuring the continuation of the family line through fertility rites.

February 14th: Valentine's Day and the Lupercalia

Today February 14th is St Valentine's Day, a holiday that for many signifies nothing more than the obligation to buy flowers and chocolates. The roots of Valentine's Day go deeper.

The First Day of Lupercalia

February 14th marked the first day of the Lupercalia. The day honored Juno, in her fertility aspect, and the male spirit Faunus, or Lupercus. On February 14th, Juno and Faunus respond to women's pleas for fertility.

This is the ancient root of Valentine's Day—not merely romantic love, but specifically fertility and conception. The flowers and sweets of modern Valentine's Day are pale echoes of the powerful fertility magic worked on this day in ancient Rome.

The Opening Ceremonies

The Arrival at the Lupercal

The annual festival opened with the arrival of the Luperci, Faunus' "wolf-priests," at the Lupercal—the cave on the Palatine Hill where the wolf nursed Romulus and Remus.

This cave was sacred ground, the mythic birthplace of Rome itself. By beginning the festival here, the Romans connected the Lupercalia to Rome's founding, to the wolf who saved the city's legendary founders, and to the wild power of Faunus.

The Sacrifices

Dogs were sacrificed for purification, goats for fertility. These were eaten by the priests.

The choice of animals is significant:

  • Dogs: Associated with purification, protection, and the boundary between civilization and wilderness
  • Goats: Sacred to Faunus, embodying raw fertility and sexual potency

By consuming these sacrificed animals, the Luperci internalized their qualities—the purifying power of the dog, the fertile virility of the goat.

The Blood Ritual and Juno's Cloak

Following the priests' meal, the goatskins were cut up. The Luperci smeared themselves with the blood and dressed themselves in "Juno's cloak," the torn patches of goatskin.

This transformation was profound:

  • The blood smearing made them sacred, marked by sacrifice
  • The goatskins connected them to Faunus and to primal animal power
  • Wearing "Juno's cloak" placed them under her protection and made them her agents

They became liminal figures—neither fully human nor fully animal, neither entirely civilized nor entirely wild, serving as intermediaries between the divine powers and the people of Rome.

The Februa: Fertility Whips

Pieces of goatskin were formed into whips, known as a februa (from which "February" derives its name).

Either the priests or specially chosen young boys would then run around the Palatine Hill striking at people with these whips, particularly barren women.

The Fertility Strike

Women struck by the februa were believed to be rendered fertile. Conception was believed ensured as was easy childbirth and healthy babies.

This was not a punishment or humiliation but a blessing. The strike of the februa conveyed:

  • The fertile power of the sacrificed goat
  • The blessing of Juno, goddess of childbirth
  • The wild generative force of Faunus
  • Purification from whatever blockage prevented conception

Women's Participation

Women positioned themselves strategically around the hill to guarantee that they would be struck, usually upon their outstretched hands.

This detail is crucial. Women were not passive victims but active participants. They sought out the februa, extended their hands to receive the strikes, and through this ritual claimed the fertility blessing for themselves.

The Lupercalia thus honored women's desire for children and their agency in seeking divine assistance to conceive.

Connection to Other February Festivals

See February Feasts, Candlemas, and Imbolc for the broader context of February's purification festivals.

The Lupercalia was part of a network of early February celebrations focused on:

  • Purification and cleansing
  • Fertility and new life
  • Honoring divine mothers and feminine power
  • Preparing for spring and the new year

Across cultures—Roman Lupercalia, Celtic Imbolc, Christian Candlemas—February emerges as a month sacred to purification, fertility, and the stirring of new life beneath winter's surface.

The Festival's Ancient Roots

Although attributed to Romulus and Remus, the Lupercalia is believed to be far more ancient. Its elements—wolf veneration, fertility rites, blood sacrifice, sacred caves, running rituals—suggest roots in prehistoric Italian religion, predating Rome itself.

The festival likely preserves traditions from:

  • Pre-Roman Italic peoples
  • Etruscan religious practice
  • Ancient pastoral cultures' relationship with wolves and fertility

Rome inherited and adapted the Lupercalia, but did not invent it. The festival's power derived from its antiquity, from its connection to forces older than civilization.

Christian Suppression

The Lupercalia was officially banned in 494 CE, as Christianity sought to eradicate Pagan festivals. The Church attempted to replace it with Candlemas (February 2nd) and the Feast of the Purification of the Blessed Virgin, overlaying Christian purification themes onto the date.

Yet the festival's essence survived—not in official religious observance, but in the secular celebration of Valentine's Day on February 14th. The flowers, the romantic gestures, the focus on love and partnership—these are distant echoes of the Lupercalia's fertility magic.

Modern Resonance

The Lupercalia teaches us:

  • Purification prepares for fertility: Before new life can come, old blockages must be cleared
  • Wild nature has sacred power: Faunus reminds us that the untamed, animal aspects of existence are divine
  • Women's agency in fertility: The Lupercalia honored women's desire for children and their active seeking of divine assistance
  • Balance in nature: Faunus mediates between predator and prey, teaching that both are necessary
  • Blood and sacrifice create transformation: The Luperci's blood-smearing and the februa strikes were not symbolic but materially powerful acts

For contemporary Pagans and witches, the Lupercalia offers a template for February magic focused on:

  • Fertility workings
  • Purification before major life transitions
  • Honoring wild, untamed aspects of the divine
  • Working with animal spirits and powers
  • Women's reproductive sovereignty and sacred sexuality
