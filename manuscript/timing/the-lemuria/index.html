The Lemuria was the second annual (but oldest) Roman commemoration of the dead, held on three odd days in May: May 9th, 11th, and 13th. During these days, the dead walk the Earth and must be propitiated.

The Lemures: The Dangerous Dead

Lemures was the term used for these walking revenants, hence the name of the festival in their honor. They were understood as the angry, volatile, dangerous dead—spirits requiring appeasement and protection. Unlike the honored Manes of The Feralia, the Lemures represented a more threatening aspect of ancestral spirits.

Origins and Etymology

According to Ovid, the holiday derives from the death of Remus, whose death at the hands of his brother Romulus is reminiscent of the biblical Cain and Abel. Remus' blood-stained ghost appeared to Romulus and demanded a festival in his honor. Remuria eventually became Lemuria.

There are also suggestions that the festival predates the arrival of the Romans in the region and has its origins in an Etruscan holy day, indicating deeper pre-Roman roots to this observance of the dangerous dead.

The Midnight Ritual

There is a detailed description of the festival ritual in Book 5 of Ovid's Fasti. The ceremony was performed by the paterfamilias (the male head of the household) and followed a precise sequence:

The Ritual Steps:

  1. Midnight Rising: The paterfamilias arose at midnight, the liminal hour when the veil between worlds is thinnest
  2. The Fig-Hand Gesture: He made the life-affirming gesture of the fig-hand (thumb between first and second fingers mimicking the sexual act)—a protective gesture against malevolent spirits
  3. Purification: He cleansed his hands in pure water
  4. The Bean Ritual: Walking barefoot through his home, he spit beans while proclaiming: "With these beans I redeem me and mine." This action was repeated nine times. The use of beans connects to their ancient association with the dead and the Underworld, as well as their protective and exorcising properties.
  5. The Banishment: At the conclusion, the paterfamilias ritually bathed, then banged on metal pots and pans while proclaiming: "Begone, ancestral spirits!" This too was repeated nine times. The number nine held sacred significance, and the clanging of metal served to drive away the dangerous spirits.

Theological Context

While The Feralia honored the benevolent Manes (deified ancestors who wanted only recognition and remembrance), The Lemuria dealt with a darker category of the dead. The Lemures were restless, potentially malevolent spirits who needed to be simultaneously appeased and expelled—recognized but firmly sent away from the living world.

Modern Application

For contemporary practitioners working with ancestral veneration, The Lemuria offers a framework for addressing difficult or troubled ancestral spirits. Not all dead are peaceful; some carry anger, trauma, or unfinished business. This festival acknowledges that reality and provides ritual tools for protection while still honoring the dead's presence and power.

The ritual's emphasis on the number nine, protective gestures, beans as magical intermediaries, and loud banishment suggests a balanced approach: respect without submission, acknowledgment without invitation to linger.
