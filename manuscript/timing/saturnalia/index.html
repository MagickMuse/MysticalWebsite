🎄Christmas

Many of the great festivals of the ancient world were folded into later cultures, and the Saturnalia is among the clearest examples: its customs shaped a great deal of how Christmas is kept today. The Romans found the feast such a delight that they carried its practices with them wherever they settled.

A Roman Midwinter Feast

The Saturnalia began as a feast of dedication at the temple of Saturn in his guise as the God of crops and sowing. It fell on December 17, once the autumn planting was finished, though it soon stretched across a whole week in spite of repeated official efforts to rein it in. On this day the cords that bound the feet of Saturn's statue through the rest of the year were loosed, a symbolic act accompanied by sacrifices that set the God free. Saturn was later personified as Old Father Time, the stooped and robed figure whose sickle cut down the harvest of passing hours.

The Roman writer Seneca, in an account from AD 50, captured the mood:

It is now the month of December, when the greatest part of the city is in a bustle. Loose reins are given to public dissipation; everywhere you may hear the sound of great preparations, as if there were some real difference between the days devoted to Saturn and those for transacting business.

In what may be a very early case of "bah, humbug," Seneca later grumbled that the whole crowd had let itself go in pleasures.

Misrule and Revelry

The Saturnalia was a season when the ordinary rules were relaxed. The toga gave way to informal dress, gambling was permitted, and feasting and drinking to excess were the order of the day. Everyone, slaves included, wore the pileus, the cap of a free man. Masters even served a banquet to their slaves in a carefully staged reversal of roles, a custom later echoed in Scotland and elsewhere in the United Kingdom in the ceremony of the Lords of Misrule. At the original Roman feast Saturn himself was present as an effigy or statue, presiding over a riotous occasion.

Gifts and Greenery

The exchange of gifts we keep at Christmas was first a part of the Saturnalia. A set of first-century poems describes the presents given, a varied lot that included combs, writing tablets, perfumes, sausages, books, and even parrots. Homes and public places were hung with greenery in honor of the God of crops and vegetation and as a token of hope for a fertile year ahead, another practice that has carried down the centuries into the Christmas season.
