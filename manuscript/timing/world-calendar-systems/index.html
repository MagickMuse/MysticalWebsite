World Calendar Systems

This guide provides an overview of major calendar systems used in magickal and ritual timing. Each system offers distinct frameworks for aligning workings with celestial and cultural cycles.

Astrological (Western Tropical)

Several astrological systems exist worldwide. The most common in Western practice is the tropical system, established by the mathematician and astrologer Ptolemy in the second century CE. Other systems include the Vedic and sidereal systems used in India.

The astrological year begins at the vernal equinox. The twelve zodiac signs function as astrological "months." All dates listed are approximate.

Chinese

The Chinese calendar is a luni-solar system, calculating time based on both lunar and solar cycles. Popular tradition holds that some version of this calendar has been in use for nearly five millennia, though archaeological evidence suggests closer to three and a half millennia. Many East Asian calendar systems share similar structures.

Each year contains twelve months determined by lunar phases, with an intercalary month added when necessary. Each month begins at midnight on the dark moon (when the sun and moon are conjunct) and continues until the following dark moon.

The northern winter solstice falls within the eleventh month. The Chinese New Year typically occurs on the second new moon after the winter solstice, usually corresponding to the new moon in Aquarius. This timing may shift if an intercalary month is present.

Months are numbered but also carry traditional names. Intercalary months remain unnamed. Names vary by region, era, and tradition, but the following are fairly standard:

Coptic

The Coptic calendar, also called the Egyptian or Alexandrian calendar, serves as the ritual calendar of the Coptic Orthodox Church. It shares close ties with the Ethiopian calendar. This system divides the year into thirteen months: twelve of thirty days each, plus a thirteenth intercalary month whose length depends on leap year status.

Based on the ancient Egyptian calendar, the Coptic system is sometimes considered the oldest calendar still in active use. Several month names honor ancient Egyptian deities. All dates are approximate.

Ethiopian

Ethiopia encompasses many languages, and month names vary by region. Multiple calendar systems exist within the country, but this one serves as the ritual calendar of the Ethiopian Orthodox Church.

Islamic

The Islamic calendar is a pure lunar calendar containing twelve lunar months. The year lasts 354 or 355 days. Each month begins with the appearance of the new moon.

This is a movable calendar, meaning months migrate through the seasons and are not anchored to solar events like equinoxes or solstices. A given month may occur in winter one year and summer another.

Jewish

The Jewish calendar is a luni-solar system with twelve months of 29 or 30 days each. Each month begins with the appearance of the new moon. A thirteenth leap month, called an "embolismic month," is added as needed to maintain synchronization with the solar year. In Hebrew, a year containing a leap month is called a "pregnant year."

Like the astrological and Iranian calendars, the first day of the Jewish calendar year corresponds to the northern vernal equinox. However, Rosh Hashanah, the Jewish religious New Year, is celebrated on the first day of the seventh month rather than the first. An old saying describes Nissan as the beginning of the months, but Tishrei as the beginning of the year.

Tibetan

The Tibetan calendar is a highly complex luni-solar system influenced over centuries by the calendar systems of China, India (Sanskrit), and Mongolia. Most years contain twelve months of 29 or 30 days each. Each month begins with the new moon.

A thirteenth (leap) month is added as needed to maintain alignment between solar and lunar phenomena. "Double" days and "skip" days are also incorporated to preserve celestial harmony.

The Tibetan calendar resembles the Chinese calendar but is not identical. Month lengths differ, and the New Year may fall on different dates. Months are both numbered and named after their ruling animal signs.

Vietnamese

The traditional Vietnamese luni-solar calendar closely resembles the Chinese calendar but occasionally differs. Each year contains twelve months of 29 or 30 days. Approximately every third year, a thirteenth "leap month" is added to reconcile lunar and solar cycles.

The winter solstice always falls within the eleventh month. Unlike other systems, Vietnamese months are numbered (one through twelve or thirteen) rather than named.

Hellenic

Whoever is of pure mind and of blameless life, and desires to do sacred deeds, may face the Gods with courage on the blessed path of great honour, guided by hope, to lead a good life.

From a temple inscription at Arsameia on the Nymphaios.

The traditional Hellenic calendar is a luni-solar system that has measured years, months, and days by the motions of the Moon and the Sun for thousands of years. Where neighbouring cultures such as the Egyptians built purely solar calendars, the Hellenes preferred the lunar rhythm with adjustments to keep step with the solar year.

A year is one complete seasonal cycle, from one winter to the next or from one summer to the next, set by the earth's revolution around the sun. The year is divided into lunar months that follow the full lunar cycle from New Moon through Full Moon and back to New Moon, set by the moon's revolution around the earth. A further unit of time, the Olympias, marks the four-year span between successive Olympic Games and is counted from 776 BCE, the traditional date of the first Olympic Games.

The first day of every month begins with the first sighting of the new moon and is called Noumenia, or in contemporary terms Protominia. Each day runs from sunset to the next sunset. Lunar months alternate between 30 and 29 days. A 30-day month is called a full month, and a 29-day month is called hollow. Each lunar month is then divided into three Decamerons of ten days apiece.

The days of the first Decameron are called Histamenos and counted upward: 1st Histamenos, 2nd Histamenos, 3rd Histamenos, and so on. The middle ten days are called Mesountos and counted in the same upward fashion. The last ten days are called Phthinontos and counted downward, from 10th Phthinontos toward the close of the month, mirroring the waning of the moon. The final day carries the name Enin kai Nea, the Old and New, marking the joint between the dying month and the one beginning.

The lunar year falls eleven days short of the solar year, so adjustments are needed to keep the calendar in step with the seasons. Solar phases such as the solstices and equinoxes are factored into the calculation, and an intercalary month is inserted at set intervals to absorb the difference. In Attic practice this additional month was called Poseideon B, and no festivals were held during it. Across two successive Olympiads the intercalary month was added on years three, five, and eight.

The modern Hellenic calendar follows the Attic system, since more is known about it than about the calendars of other regions, and it is calculated by the same method the ancestors used. According to the Attic calendar the first month, Hekatomvaion, opens on the Noumenia following the summer solstice, generally between 21 and 23 June. Other cities began the year at different solar markers: Sparta, Rhodes, and Miletos after the autumn equinox, Chios after the spring equinox, and Delos after the winter solstice. The lunar months themselves were calculated identically across all these regions, anchored to the phases of the moon.

By using the fixed event of the summer solstice, the precise start of each new year is determined. The sighting of the New Moon then sets the count of days and months, and from that count flow the dates of every holy festival.
