Correspondences for Lughnasadh (Lammas)

Spiritual Focus and Keywords

Gratitude, abundance, blessings, celebration, harvest, reflection, introspection, discernment, sacrifice, the necessity and inevitability of both life and death

Magickal Focus and Suggested Workings

Excellent time for magick focused on prosperity, gratitude, abundance, strength, growth, protection, honoring ancestors, making offerings in exchange for blessings, personal transformation, and communicating with the dead.

Sacred wells, hilltops, and cemeteries are ideal sites for magickal workings. It is also a great time to collect wild herbs and make new tools for magick.

Astrological Timing and Associated Planets

Astronomical midpoint between the Summer Solstice and Autumn Equinox.

Sun at 15° Leo in the Northern Hemisphere. Sun at 15° Aquarius in the Southern Hemisphere.

Some Pagans time the sabbat astronomically, while others celebrate on August 1 (often sundown July 31 to sundown August 1). Others observe August 5 as “Old Lammas.” Many also follow Nature’s cues, celebrating when wild berries ripen or first crops reach fruition.

Lammas: The Christian Integration

Etymology and Church Origins

The word "Lammas" derives from the Old English hlaf ("loaf") and maesse ("mass" or "feast"). It was a harvest holiday of the early English Church celebrated on August Eve. Loaves baked from the year's first ripe grain were blessed in Church, sanctifying the harvest and giving thanks for the bounty of the earth through Christian ritual.

Two Theories of Origin

There are two versions of the origins of this feast:

Theory One: Christian Integration of Lughnasa

Lammas represents an attempt to integrate the Celtic Pagan festival of Lughnasa into the Christian calendar. Although also a harvest festival, Lughnasa honored the important Celtic solar deity Lugh. By creating a Christian feast day with similar timing and harvest themes, the Church could overlay Christian meaning onto existing Pagan celebrations.

Theory Two: The Corn Mother's Deeper Roots

Devotion to Lugh may itself have been superimposed on an earlier holy day dedicated to the Corn Mother and her dying son. In this deeper layer of mythology, the Corn Mother mourns her son—the spirit of the grain who must die so that humanity may eat. This sacrificial god eventually transforms into the Christian archetype of the Mater Dolorosa, the Mother of Sorrows who mourns her crucified son.

This theory suggests layers of religious transformation: an ancient grain mother mythology, overlaid with Celtic devotion to Lugh, further overlaid with Christian harvest blessing.

Lammas in Modern Wicca

Lammas is celebrated as one of the important Wiccan sabbats. Although either name may be used—Lughnasadh or Lammas—the Anglo-Saxon name Lammas tends to be favored in modern Wicca. This preference reflects Wicca's roots in British folk tradition and its integration of both Pagan and folk Christian elements.

Romantic Enchantment

Lammas, celebrated on August Eve, is often a night devoted to romantic enchantment. The ripening of grain mirrors the ripening of love, and the abundance of the first harvest creates fertile ground for love magic and romantic divination.

Lughnasa: The Ancient Celtic Festival

Pronunciation and Spelling

Pronounced Loo-nah-sa. Also spelled Lughnasad. In modern Irish Gaelic, the month of August is known as Lunasa.

The Month-Long Celebration

Lughnasa Day is an ancient Celtic harvest feast celebrated on August 1st and for the fortnight preceding and following that date. Four weeks are dedicated to honoring the Celtic solar deity Lugh, Spirit of Craftsmanship, Light, Victory, and War: the last two weeks of July and the first two weeks of August, which roughly correspond to the dates when the sun is in the astrological sign of Leo, the sign that belongs to the sun and epitomizes its power.

Although the modern Wiccan sabbat is almost always devoted solely to the eve of July 31st leading into Lughnasa Day on August 1st, ancient people may have had more leisure time and more time to devote to spirituality (and fun). August 1st was merely the culmination of a month of celebrations.

The Three Sacred Days of Purification

The three days prior to Lughnasa Day were particularly sacred and devoted to purification. Those three days are dedicated to Ireland's ancient solar goddess Ana; an earlier, more primordial deity than Lugh, the entire festival may once have belonged to her.

During these three days leading up to the Celtic festival, water was taboo. There was no bathing and no fishing prior to the Sacrifice of the Grain King (or the Grain Bear, Grain Horse, or Grain Wolf).

Agricultural Origins

Lughnasa is an agricultural rather than a pastoral celebration. It was a late introduction, at least in its present form, to Irish festivals, brought perhaps by continental devotees of the deity Lugh, a relative late-comer to the Irish pantheon.

Layers of Mythology

There are various legends about how and why Lugh initiated the festival that bears his name. Those legends about Lugh may be correct; however, just as Christianity would eventually transform Lughnasa into its harvest feast Lammas, so Lughnasa is superimposed on an earlier holy day devoted to the Corn Mother and her dying son.

The Waning Sun

Although it's still hot in August, the festival marks the waning of the sun. Days are noticeably shorter than they were at the last major festival, Midsummer's Eve, which corresponds roughly with the Summer Solstice. The beginning of the end of summer is in sight.

The Sacrifice of the Harvest King

Lughnasa is a celebration of the harvest but also a sacrifice of the Harvest King. John Barleycorn must die if the people are to live or, as that other proverb goes, you shall reap what was sown. The festival was intended to ensure a plentiful harvest.

During Lughnasa, Lugh fights the Evil Lord of Blight for possession of the harvest. This mythic battle represents the eternal struggle to protect the crops from disease, pests, and weather that could destroy the year's food supply.

Fire Festival and Water Taboo

Lughnasa was a fire festival characterized by bonfires. Fire may be understood as pieces of the sun brought down to Earth—a way of capturing and holding solar power during this time when the sun begins to wane.

The water taboo during the three purification days created a stark contrast: the element of fire reigned supreme while water, usually associated with cleansing, was forbidden. This inversion highlights the special magical nature of these preparatory days.

Lugh: The Pan-Celtic Solar Deity

Lugh was an extremely important Celtic deity, not least because (along with Brigid) the widespread veneration of Lugh indicates the existence of pan-Celtic spiritual traditions. At one point, the Celts ruled a huge swathe of continental Europe before being forced to the very edges of the land mass.

However we don't really know all that much about Celtic cosmology and ancient religion. The Celts left very little if any writing, and what exists is filtered through the eyes of outsiders, like Romans or Celtic Christian converts.

Names and Epithets

Lugh's name is spelled variously depending on location:

  • Lugh: Irish spelling
  • Lleu Llaw Gyffes: Welsh name, meaning "The Bright One of the Skillful Hand"
  • Lugos: European name, meaning "raven," important figure in Gaul

European Cities Named for Lugh

At least 14 European cities are named for Lugh including:

  • Laon
  • Leyden
  • Loudon
  • Lyon: Old name was Lugdunum, "the fortress of Lugh." The city is believed to have been his cult center. Coins associated with that ancient city bore the images of ravens, which may be a reference to Lugh (or Lugos as he was known there).
  • Carlisle in England, formerly Lugubalium

It's been suggested that many European churches dedicated to St Michael the Archangel were built over sites once dedicated to Lugh.

The Sacred Marriage

Lughnasa means "the marriage of Lugh." There is a tremendous romantic component to the celebration. Lugh the sun and the Earth Mother renew their wedding vows annually during the full moon in August and invite all to gather and revel with them. Lughnasa celebrates the consummation of their sacred relationship.

The Nine-Month Cycle

It precedes the spring festival of Beltane, which symbolizes the birth of the bright half of the Celtic year, by exactly nine months. This is not coincidence—the sacred marriage at Lughnasa births the light half of the year at Beltane.

The High King's Marriage

It's not an affectation to say that this is the day the solar deity weds the Earth. Once upon a time, that was meant quite literally. This was the day when a High Priestess, channeling the goddess who embodied the land of Ireland, ritually wed the High King of Ireland. The consummation of their marriage enabled him to rule for yet another year.

This sacred marriage ritual ensured the king's legitimacy and the land's fertility—the two were understood as inextricably linked.

Legends of Lugh's Involvement

Although the sacred marriage and the Corn Mother's sacrifice of her son or young lover no doubt precedes Lugh's associations with this date, there are also various versions of how Lugh became involved:

Tailtiu's Memorial

Lugh ordered a commemorative feast to honor his foster-mother, Tailtiu. On August 1st, a great festival was held at Teltown on the Boyne River in Ireland. The town allegedly takes its name from Lugh's foster mother who is buried there. Lugh instituted games in her honor.

The Teltown Marital Fair

An ancient marital fair took place in Teltown, perhaps initiated by Lugh. It was a time to begin as well as formalize relationships. Men would stay on one side of the fair, women on the other, while go-betweens served as mediums to make arrangements. Similar marital fairs still occur in rural Berber areas of North Africa.

Lugh's Two Wives

Lugh has two wives, granddaughters of the King of Britain. When they died, Lugh requests that these women's lives and memories be commemorated every August 1st. His wives' names are Nas and Búi. (Búi is another name for Cailleach Bhéara.)

Harvesting Botanicals

Lughnasa is an occasion for blessing and harvesting botanicals for the coming year. In Northern climates, plants and their volatile oils are at the height of their power just before decomposition begins. The turning point from summer to autumn is the perfect moment to capture plant magic.

Christian Integration

In Britain, Lughnasa and similar festivals weren't banished but were integrated into Christianity. St Columba, for instance, allowed his monks to maintain their Lughnasa celebrations although he renamed it the "Feast of the Ploughman." Lughnasa evolved into the festival of Lammas.

Underground Devotion and Witches' Sabbats

A deity who identifies himself as a sorcerer is attractive to those who practice witchcraft. Lugh or Lugos seems to have been a very important deity in Europe; post-Christianity, devotion to him seems to have gone underground, at least for a while.

This is suggested by reports of witches' sabbats held at the Puy de Dome, the 5000 foot peak in the Auvergne region of France, full of caves and grottoes, where Lugh maintained a sanctuary. The mountain's height, its caves perfect for secret gatherings, and its association with Lugh made it an ideal location for those who continued the old devotions.

The August Herbs: Sacred Botanical Ritual

In Northern Europe, August Eve, the night preceding Lughnasa, is the opportunity to create the botanical amulet known as the August Herbs. If proper ritual is followed, it's believed that these nine sacred herbs will bestow various blessings during the upcoming year including protection from malevolent magic and volatile weather. They attract love, stimulate romance, enhance sexuality, and ease labor pains as well as the passage into death.

The Ritual Instructions

  1. Timing and Silence: For maximum power, pick the August herbs before sunrise while maintaining complete silence.
  2. Dress: The original instructions suggest that the harvest must be accomplished while naked but if this is unrealistic then at least be barefoot and bareheaded.
  3. The Nine Sacred Herbs: Gather a bundle of:
    • Arnica
    • Calendula
    • Dill
    • Lovage
    • Mugwort
    • Sage
    • Tansy
    • Valerian
    • Yarrow
  4. No Iron: No iron can be used in the harvest, so no modern knives. Gather the herbs with your hands or with a ritual stone or crystal knife. If they're hard to pick, you can bite through the stems. Don't petition for blessings but keep a still, serene, blank mind.
  5. Ornament the Bundle: Ornament the bundles with blue cornflowers and red corn cockles. Add a border of low growing herbs like chamomile or mother-of-thyme.
  6. Complete the Charm: Place a stalk of millet, rye or other grain in the very center of the bundle, tie with a red ribbon and hang it within your home.

Modern Challenges and Focus

Lughnasa or Lammas is one of the more obscure witches' holidays. Pagan aspects of the festival were suppressed long ago and the Christian feast of Lammas was never entirely reinstated in Britain after the Reformation.

Lammas is considered amongst the eight major sabbats of the Wiccan Wheel of the Year, however the roots of this holiday are so agricultural and rural that it often stymies modern Neo-Pagans, frequently no less urban than anyone else in the twenty-first century.

Modern Lammas festivals often focus on the romantic aspects of the feast. It's a wonderful night for love magic as well as for enjoying the first fruits of the harvest, including grain and wine.

Modern Practice

Celebrating an Ancient Holiday in a Modern World

For many modern Pagans, finding personal significance in Lughnasadh can be challenging. As one of three harvest celebrations, this sabbat marks the very beginning of the harvest season—the point where the first fruits have reached maturity. While few of us maintain more than a small garden, the harvest's relevance remains profound: our lives depend on it just as much as our ancestors' did. Though we purchase food from grocery stores stocked with imports from distant lands, this creates only an illusion of independence from the natural world. The earth is our mother, and regardless of our three-piece suits, fancy cars, and air-conditioned luxury, we remain utterly dependent on the planet for food, water, shelter, and clothing. Lughnasadh celebrates this reality—the gratitude we feel for life, the thankfulness for our continued existence, and the delight of seeing effort begin to pay off. What we've personally sown is now in the early stages of being reaped.

Modern Pagan Celebrations

Though modern Pagan Lughnasadh customs vary widely, common themes of harvest, gratitude, and reflection abound. The holiday is seen as a time to celebrate summer's ending while looking forward to fall. Potlucks, picnics, and feasting are popular, along with gathering wild herbs. Magick and rituals focus on abundance, protection, fertility, and themes of death and renewal. Offerings are given to deities like Danu or Lugh, to ancestors, to the dead in general, or to nature spirits like dryads and vegetation-protecting nymphs. Despite differences, these offerings share a universal message: "Thanks!"

Celtic Reconstructionists base their practices on historically verified Celtic rites, celebrating Lá Lúnasa or Tailtiu in early or mid-August, timed to coincide with local berry season. Wild berry picking is central, with feasts featuring berries in every dish from main course to dessert. Ceremonial offerings of berries or other foods are presented to gods and land spirits, and those in hurricane-prone areas may offer to Lugh or the Cailleachan (storm hags) for weather protection. Wiccans celebrate Lughnasadh, Lammas, or August Eve with rituals and feasts giving thanks for blessings and celebrating skills and efforts. Prayers and offerings (especially bread and fruit) are given to the god of grain and goddess earth, along with games, baking, arts and crafts, and gatherings. Heathens and Asatru celebrate Stikklestad Day (July 29) honoring martyrs who died resisting Christian conversion, Radbod Day (August 9) remembering the Frisian king who refused conversion, and Freyfaxi (August 1, 19, or 23) with blots to Frey honoring fertility and the harvest. Modern Druids celebrate Lughnasadh as the wedding feast of Lugh and his symbolic marriage to the land, often gathering in groves for rituals featuring Lugh's spear symbolism, offerings of blessed bread and beer, drumming, wild-harvested foods, and handfastings or oath-making. Traditional Witches follow local cultural traditions, celebrating either cross-quarter days like Lughnasadh or the solstices/equinoxes, practicing traditional customs like singing, dancing, offerings, feasting, and riding brooms through fields to banish decay and welcome increase. Neopagans and Eclectic Witches combine elements from multiple traditions, hosting community rituals or solitary celebrations focused on harvest thanksgiving, mourning the corn king's symbolic death, reflection, gratitude, and life cycles, with magick for protection, prosperity, and abundance, offerings to spirits, corn dollies, and general merrymaking.

Major festivals include: Green Spirit Festival (Circle Sanctuary, Wisconsin) with rituals, workshops, and mugwort harvests; Iowa Lammasfest (Coralville Dam area) benefiting raptor conservation; Sacred Harvest Festival (near St. Paul, Minnesota) drawing hundreds for a nine-day tribal community celebration; Santo Domingo Pueblo Feast Day and Green Corn Dance (New Mexico) blending tribal custom with Catholicism; Tullamore Show (Ireland) as the country's largest single-day agricultural gathering; Craggaunowen Lughnasa Festival (County Clare, Ireland) with historic reenactments; Timoleague Festival (West Cork, Ireland) with races and entertainment; Eastbourne Lammas Festival (England) featuring costumed processions and Morris dancers; and St. Andrew's Lammas Fair (Scotland), Europe's oldest surviving street fair.

The Natural World at Lughnasadh

Rural Pagans with gardens experience the magick of growing plants from seed to maturity, witnessing the culmination of effort from themselves, the sun, earth, elements, and the plant itself. Popular activities include harvesting crops, picking wild herbs or berries, enjoying evening bonfires, and cooking feasts highlighting the harvest—berry jams, fruit and nut pies, roasted root vegetables, and fresh bread. Rituals are held fireside, waterside, or on hilltops with offerings of first fruits. City Pagans face challenges but benefit from established communities, gathering for sabbats featuring feasting, drumming, dancing, and ritual. Food from grocery stores is still viewed as sacred and magickal, symbolizing nature's glory and earth's gifts. Urban dwellers seek the outdoors through bike rides in parks, drives to the country, or simply noticing parking lot trees and office shrubbery. Those without farms can visit pick-your-own operations or forage for wild plants like onion, mint, dandelion, and blackberries (with proper identification and care not to over-harvest or damage vulnerable areas).

The Prevailing Energies and Energetics of Lughnasadh

The August sabbat carries an unusual quality of being both opening and closing, culmination and commencement—the in-between time just after midday and before sunset, the crossroads where contrasting forces join to form an open path extending in all directions. Summer ends but autumn hasn't begun; the sun is past its apex but hasn't reached its nadir; immediate hunger subsides as crops ripen but anxiety about completing the harvest lingers. Magickally, this creates a doorway or portal into dimensions not easily accessed regularly, making it ideal for highly transformative workings on a grander scale. It's time to work magick you ordinarily wouldn't, challenge your limitations, and suspend doubt—perfect for landing dream jobs, rare opportunities, and changing the world in significant ways. The season also calls for reflection, introspection, and reconnection with earth, ourselves, and other planet inhabitants. Observe shifting seasons and ponder shifts in your own life, reconnect with friends and family through gatherings, serve humanity through volunteer work, and honor or communicate with loved ones who have passed.

Moon Magick at Lughnasadh

Lughnasadh mirrors the waning gibbous moon. The prominence of light has begun to diminish, and the energy of the year shifts toward gratitude and gentle release. Efforts already in motion may still bear fruit, and that potential keeps the drive alive, but there is an undeniable sense that an ending approaches on the horizon.

As the first of three harvests, this is when seeds and intentions sown in earlier months enter their first wave of reaping. The harvest moons begin to rise now, and a full moon at this time carries special weight, casting light long into the night for those laboring to gather the earth's bounty. That extended moonlight transforms practical work into something sacred, blurring the line between toil and ritual.

Connect to the land and to your ancestors during Lughnasadh lunations. Offerings made in genuine gratitude can strengthen the spirits who support your remaining manifestation work through the waning half of the year. Lean into the abundance of the grain harvest by weaving grain into your spellcraft, whether through knotwork with wheat stalks, candle magick dressed with cornmeal, or the deeply magickal act of baking bread with intention.

The Merging of Traditions

Traditional activities include: Honor the dead—visit cemeteries with picnic lunches or offerings of flowers, fruit, and libations; perform grave maintenance at neglected sites; conduct genealogical research and display photos of deceased loved ones. Bring in the harvest—visit pick-your-own farms to experience harvest firsthand; forage for wild edible and magickal plants (with absolute certainty of identification and safety). Go magickal tool hunting—scout for fallen branches for staffs or wands, appealing stones for spellwork or rune crafting (respecting "take nothing" policies where they exist). Get cookin'—prepare feasts with fresh seasonal ingredients simply seasoned; bake bread from scratch (even foolproof recipes like Irish soda bread); host potluck dinners; dine outdoors at sunset. Play games—host miniature Tailteann Fairs with football, frisbee, foot races, sack races, and other competitive displays of skill, awarding prizes to winners.

Traditional Lughnasadh Activities

Empowered Rainwater

Collect rainwater in a non-metallic container and add mugwort to create water for empowering objects.

Sand Candles

Make Sand Candles to honor the Goddess and the God of the Sea:

  • Melt wax from old candles in a coffee can set in a pot of boiling water
  • Add any essential oil for scent
  • Scoop out a candle mold in wet sand in any design (scoop a bowl with three finger holes to form a three-legged cauldron candle)
  • Clip the wick to a stick over the center and gently pour in the melted wax
  • Unmold when wax hardens by lifting out and brushing off excess sand

Indian Corn Necklace

String colorful Indian corn on black thread for a necklace. Feed it to the birds at Yule.

Witch's Bottle

Create and bury near your house entry a small, protective Witch's Bottle full of broken glass, sharp pointy things, and urine for home protection.

Corn Dolly

Make a Corn Dolly to save for next Imbolc:

  • Double over a bundle of wheat, tying it near the top to form a head
  • Take some of the fiber to twist into arms (can tie together to hold flowers)
  • Decorate with a dress and bonnet or garland of flowers

Cornbread Sticks Recipe

Bake corn-shaped cornbread sticks using an iron mold:

  • 1 cup flour
  • ½ cup corn meal
  • ¼ cup sugar
  • ¾ tsp salt
  • 2 tsp baking powder
  • 2 eggs

Harvest Chant

Recite when serving the cornbread at dinner:

The Earth Mother grants the grain;

The Horned God goes to His domain.

By giving life into Her grain;

The God dies, then is born again.

Solar Wheel or Corn Man Wheel

Make a Solar Wheel (eight ears of corn) or Corn Man Wheel (five ears):

  • Pull a wire hanger into a circle, keeping the hook for hanging
  • Hot glue the tips of eight or five ears of Indian corn to a cardboard disk at the center
  • Carefully spread and wrap some husks around the wire
  • Cover the center cardboard with a flower or extra husk
  • Let dry overnight and hang on the front door

God of the Grain, Lord of Rebirth; Return in the Spring to renew Mother Earth.
