🏮Obon Festival

Type
Holidays
Culture
Japan
Also called
Bon Festival, Feast of Lanterns

The Buddhist festival Obon is celebrated annually from the 13th to the 15th day of the seventh month of the Japanese calendar, which corresponds to July 15th if the lunar calendar is used or August 15th if the solar calendar is followed.

Purpose and Meaning

Obon commemorates the ancestors who are believed to return to Earth at this time to visit surviving relatives and descendants. This is a joyful reunion rather than a fearful visitation—the returning spirits are welcomed, honored, and celebrated before being gently guided back to their realm.

Guiding the Spirits Home: The Lanterns

Rituals vary depending upon region and family, however Obon is typically characterized by lanterns, which are hung in front of houses to guide dead souls back home. These glowing beacons serve as spiritual lighthouses, showing the ancestors the way through the darkness to their living family members.

Traditional Observances

Cemetery Visits: Visits to the cemetery are customary, where families clean and tend ancestral graves in preparation for the spirits' return.

Offerings to the Ancestors: Offerings to the ancestors traditionally include cake, fruits and flowers, rice, and vegetables. These offerings are placed on altars at home or in shrines and temples, creating sacred spaces where the living and dead can share communion.

Bon Odori: Dancing for the Dead: Traditional dances called bon odori are performed during the festival. These dances are believed to comfort and please returning ghosts, expressing gratitude and love through movement and music. Communities gather for these folk dances, which vary by region but share the common purpose of honoring the ancestors.

Guiding the Spirits Back: Floating Lanterns

When Obon is over, the lanterns are placed in living waters—streams, rivers, lakes, and seas. There they float and will guide the ghosts back to their own realm. This beautiful ritual, known as tōrō nagashi (floating lanterns), creates rivers of light as hundreds or thousands of lanterns drift away, carrying the ancestors gently back to the spirit world.

Modern Observance

For contemporary practitioners working with ancestral veneration, Obon offers a model for creating a complete cycle of ancestor work: welcoming the spirits, honoring them with offerings and celebration, and then respectfully guiding them back to their proper realm. The emphasis on light as a guiding force—both to bring the spirits home and to send them back—provides a powerful symbol for ancestor communication work.
