📅December 25🎉SaturnaliaYule

Christmas falls on December 25 and is, for most, the height of the midwinter festivities, though the shift from the Julian to the Gregorian calendar created a fourteen-day discrepancy, so some keep January 6 as the "true" Christmas. The name means "Christ's Mass," yet the day is kept across the world by Christians and non-Christians alike.

Older Sun Gods

Long before it belonged to Christianity, this date was the feast of the Phrygian sun god Attis, honored far and wide and said to have been born in the land that is now Turkey. Attis was later eclipsed by Mithra, whose story runs uncannily parallel: born on the same day in humble circumstances to a virgin mother, he died and was resurrected, and his faith promised a brotherhood of all people and eternal life in exchange for a pure moral code. Mithraism spread through Europe with the Roman soldiers who carried it on campaign, and at least one temple to Mithra still stands in Britain, just south of Hadrian's Wall in Northumberland. In Mithraism the day was Dies Natalis Invicti Solis, the Birthday of the Unconquered Sun. That title captures the real heart of the festival: the need to bring light and cheer into the darkest stretch of the year, as the wheel of time turns through the winter solstice, has always mattered more than the particular divine figure honored on the day.

Yule and the Yule Log

Before Christianity the Anglo-Saxons called this midwinter festival geol, the forerunner of Yule, a name still used by those who wish to keep the festival free of Christian association. Some customs of geol survive, above all the Yule Log. Today it is most often a log-shaped cake dressed with plastic holly and lopsided robins, but the original was a vast section of a tree that had to be found rather than felled. It was dragged to the largest hearth in the district and burned for the length of the festivities, a sign of light and warmth in the dark and a reminder of the Sun. The Yule candle carried the same meaning, and its great size let it burn for a long time. Churches took up the custom too, raising towering candles over the congregation as emblems of spiritual light. That mammoth candle has since shrunk into the advent candle, marked in twenty-four segments that burn down from December 1.

Saturnalia and Father Christmas

Christmas also owes much to the Roman Saturnalia. During this festival Saturn, the god of time, was loosed from his bonds, gifts changed hands, and the social order was turned upside down as servants and masters traded places, a custom later echoed in the Lords of Misrule. Saturn returns in the figure of Father Christmas, the generous giver of gifts to all and to children above all (see Santa Claus). He appears again at New Year as Old Father Time, aged and careworn and oddly thin despite the mince pies and sherry, dressed in sackcloth and carrying a sickle.

Decline and Revival

Enthusiasm for Christmas faded after the Reformation, in large part because puritanical church authorities condemned its excesses as "papist." The holiday was banned outright in England in 1647, and although there were pockets of defiance the celebrations dwindled. By the early nineteenth century there was a real chance the festivities might be forgotten altogether. They were revived largely by Charles Dickens, whose A Christmas Carol is still held by many as the very heart of the Christmas message: goodwill to all, a time for families, and generosity of spirit.

Greenery

Bringing greenery indoors is one of the clearest survivals of the older festival. The Holly tree shelters nature spirits, and its prickling leaves are phallic emblems of fertility; the Church reads the same leaves as the crown of thorns and the red berries as the blood of Christ. A cup carved from Ivy was said to guard against drunkenness, which tied it to Dionysus, a powerful presence during the feasting of the Saturnalia, though the Church tends to overlook the link. Mistletoe carries such overt sexual symbolism, the pale berries likened to semen and the custom of kissing beneath hanging sprigs, that its pagan roots have resisted Christianization, and it remains barred from many churches. The Christmas tree began on the European mainland as a way of honoring the spirit of the World Tree by bringing it into the home and decorating it. German communities in Britain already kept decorated trees, but the custom took hold widely only after Prince Albert, consort of Queen Victoria, brought one into the royal household. Purist Christians reconcile the tree by reading it as the cross, the "tree" on which Christ died.

The Christmas Pudding

Sharing food has always been central to the celebration, and what appears on the table has shifted with fashion, though meat has usually featured prominently. Early mince pies held vine fruits and spices and were sometimes topped with a pastry figure of the baby. The round, cannonball-shaped Christmas pudding is a relatively recent arrival, adapted in the seventeenth century. By tradition it should contain thirteen ingredients, one for each of the apostles and one for Jesus. The old practice of hiding charms and trinkets in the pudding is fading, but each token once carried its own meaning. Among them were a boot, a bell, a thimble, a ring, a wishbone, a button, a horseshoe, and the silver sixpence. The boot meant travel, the thimble a happy but single life, and the ring, marriage.
