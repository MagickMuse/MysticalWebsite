Card Grimoire

Tarot Origins

For centuries, seekers have turned to this beautiful oracle for guidance, insight, and answers to life's pressing questions. While the tarot's true origins remain shrouded in mystery, several theories attempt to trace its roots.

Theories of Origin

Some scholars believe the cards date back over two millennia to ancient Egypt and the legendary library of Alexandria. Others suggest the Crusaders carried them westward from the Middle East. A third tradition credits the Romani people with introducing tarot to Europe.

Further Theories and Suppression

To the Scottish ministers who denounced it, the deck was nothing less than the Devil's Picture Book, and for long stretches of its past the Christian establishment eyed it with unease. The cards kept their pull regardless, bound up with the old streams of hidden knowledge, the Kabbalah, the craft of witches, the pagan ways, and alchemy among them, and they held their place as an instrument of divination wired to the planets and the signs. To Carl Jung, their pictures were a parade of the deep, shared figures of the psyche.

That a thing called arcana, a word for the secret and the concealed, should have murky beginnings is only fitting. Lineages have been claimed for it running back to Hermes Trismegistus, reputed father of alchemy, and to Egypt, India, Arabia, and China by turns. By one telling the Knights Templar brought the deck west, its pictures a screen for Saracen magickal teaching kept clear of a Church quick to persecute, and the knight was struck from the ordinary pack so the Templar link would go unnoticed. Medieval churchmen suspected the cards of spreading the Gnostic doctrines of the Cathar heretics, and across the years from 1378 to 1450 they fell under bans in Regensburg, Marseilles, Paris, Siena, Venice, and the north of Italy. Later the same Church set about bending them toward Christian teaching, just as it had once swallowed the lore of the Zodiac.

The scholar Court de Gebelin, much occupied with the deck, held the name to be Egyptian in root, joining Tar for road with Ro for royal, so that the word announced a royal road toward wisdom. A second guess hangs on the palindrome ROTA TARO ORAT TORA ATOR, taken to mean that the wheel of the taro tells of the law of Hathor.

Renaissance Card Games of the Rich and Famous

Whether these origin theories hold truth cannot be said with certainty. What is known is that tarot shares a clear lineage with Renaissance playing cards, with similarities still visible in modern poker decks.

Italy's Tarocchi

During the 1400s, Italian nobility played a game called Il Trionfos using cards known as trionfi (triumphs). Wealthy patrons commissioned artists to create these cards, either hand-painted or printed using carved woodblocks.

These weren't simple gambling games. They were creative exercises where players drew inspiration from the card imagery to compose poems about one another. The oldest surviving deck is also among the most exquisite: the Visconti Tarot, commissioned by the Duke of Milan in the mid-fifteenth century. Many original cards from this deck, painted on gold and silver foil, now reside in New York's Morgan Library & Museum.

Islamic Card Games

Card games flourished in the Arab world during the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, particularly among the wealthy and military elite. The Mamluk pack significantly influenced modern tarot. This deck contained:

  • Four suits: coins, cups, swords, and polo sticks
  • Ten numbered cards per suit
  • Four court cards per suit

Sound familiar? This structure mirrors today's Minor Arcana. The cards likely served both gaming and fortune-telling purposes, as evidenced by prognosticating rhymes inscribed on them:

As for the present that rejoices, thy heart will soon open up.
O thou who hast possessions, remain happy and thou shalt have a pleasant life.
With the sword of happiness I shall redeem a beloved who will afterwards take my life.

The remaining original cards are housed in Istanbul's Topkapi Museum. Images are viewable at World of Playing Cards.

The Spanish Card Game of Love

Fifteenth-century Spanish nobility played Juego de Naypes with forty-nine colorful cards designed for fortune-telling and revealing secrets, especially romantic ones. The deck featured four suits based on women: maidens, wives, widows, and nuns. Each card revealed what someone loved or desired most, with verses matching the card's number in line count.

By the seventeenth century, professional cartomancers (card-reading fortune-tellers) practiced their craft throughout Spain, laying out cards in patterns resembling modern tarot spreads.

Tarot in the Modern Era

Early tarot decks, though beautifully designed, often lacked the narrative imagery familiar to modern readers, making interpretation more challenging. The turn of the twentieth century brought renewed enthusiasm for the tarot alongside growing interest in Spiritualism and ritual magick, along with an entirely new approach to the cards.

The Tarot's Two Books

Modern tarot decks comprise two "books": the Major Arcana and Minor Arcana. The word arcana means "mysteries" or "secrets."

The Minor Arcana resembles both Renaissance playing cards and modern poker decks (though poker has only three court cards per suit). Minor Arcana cards describe everyday events and experiences, while Major Arcana cards reveal cosmic, spiritual, or divine forces (destiny itself) at work.

The Rider-Waite-Smith Deck

In 1909, tarot underwent a transformative shift. Arthur Edward Waite, a prominent occultist, commissioned Pamela Colman Smith, an artist and theatrical designer, to create a unique deck. Both belonged to the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, an influential mystical society Waite would eventually lead as Grand Master. This affiliation explains the deck's Golden Dawn symbolism.

Under Waite's direction, Smith painted seventy-eight images that became the tarot cards published by William Rider & Son in 1910.

The deck's revolutionary feature? All cards, not just the Major Arcana, depicted storytelling scenes conveying their meanings. Readers could now interpret cards intuitively through imagery alone. The Ten of Swords shows a figure lying face-down with ten swords in their back, instantly evoking treachery, deception, and pain. The Three of Cups depicts joyful women dancing together, clearly celebrating friendship and happy times.

Over a century later, the Rider-Waite-Smith tarot remains the most popular and influential deck worldwide.

The Thoth Tarot

Opinions on the Thoth Tarot tend to be polarized. Painted by Lady Frieda Harris under direction from the notorious English occultist Aleister Crowley between 1938 and 1943, its striking imagery and vivid colors captivate some tarotists while unsettling others. This is fitting, given its controversial creator.

Though not published until 1969 (after both Crowley and Harris had died), it has remained continuously in print ever since.

The Thoth Tarot draws on diverse esoteric, scientific, spiritual, and philosophical traditions including alchemy, astrology, and ceremonial magick. (Thoth was an Egyptian deity who served as scribe to the gods.) Crowley renamed some Major Arcana cards, calling card fourteen "Art" instead of "Temperance," for instance. He also altered court card titles, which some readers find confusing.

Tarot Innovations

Today, tens of thousands of tarot decks exist, with artists continuously creating more. Whatever your heritage, interests, or aesthetic preferences (Celtic, Japanese, Native American, gardening, baseball, cats, unicorns, vampires, zombies, or even gummy bears), a deck exists that speaks to you.

While most tarot cards are rectangular, the Motherpeace Tarot features round cards. Emerging in the 1970s alongside feminism's rise, it celebrates 30,000 years of women's culture. Some decks feature oversized Renaissance-style cards; others are pocket-sized miniatures. Some include extra cards beyond the standard seventy-eight, or blank cards for personal creation. Many come with companion books explaining interpretations according to the designer's philosophy.

Original unpublished decks can be viewed online. Blogs and social media groups offer vibrant forums for enthusiasts to share ideas. Free instant readings are available online. As technology evolves, the tarot (a living, evolving medium) will undoubtedly continue to innovate.

Magick, Witchcraft, and the Tarot

Perhaps you've practiced witchcraft for years and recently discovered tarot. Or maybe tarot readings guided you into the witches' world. However you arrived here, whatever inspired this path: welcome!

Not all witches work with tarot, but many do. As explored in the introduction, tarot and witchcraft share deep connections. The Magician card displays four tools witches use in magickal practice: wand, chalice, pentagram, and sword/athame. The magus raises one hand skyward and points the other earthward, embodying the axiom "as above, so below" and the union of heaven and earth. Some decks also feature these four tools on The World card.

The four Minor Arcana suits take their names from these tools, symbolizing the four elements witches recognize as fundamental energetic forces:

Examine The World card in the Rider-Waite-Smith deck: winged elemental symbols appear in the corners, representing the archangels and universal forces. The same might be said of the tarot.

The Arcana and Their Hidden Order

The four lesser suits sort themselves not only by element but by charge. Fire in the Wands and air in the Swords run to the active and the masculine, while water in the Cups and earth in the Coins or Pentacles run to the receptive and the feminine. One reading threads the suits through four life-stages borrowed from Tantric thought: Sambhoga, the season of pleasure, where the cup first takes on the taste of experience; Nirmana, the constructive years of early adulthood, signed by the wand, scepter, or vajra; Artha, the heaping-up of worldly goods in the suit of coins; and Moksha, release and the practice of dying well, borne by the sword as the emblem of letting go.

The twenty-two trumps of the Major Arcana have earned the title quintessence of occultism, their images paired with the letters of the Hebrew alphabet. That same twenty-two counts the paths strung between the Sephiroth on the Tree of Life, and a number of the cards mirror the Sephiroth in turn, reaching from the Fool as the shapeless void of the Ain Soph to the Wheel of Fortune as the Kingdom. They keep company with the Zodiac too: the Lovers with Gemini, the Moon with the crab or lobster of Cancer, Strength with the lion of Leo. Readers often bundle the twenty-one numbered trumps into three sevens, the first for spirit, the next for soul, the last for the world of matter, leaving the Fool to stand alone with no number at all.
