Card Grimoire

Preparing Your Deck

Before using a deck of playing cards for divination or spellwork, a few simple steps will transform an ordinary deck into a dedicated magickal tool.

Choosing a Deck

A brand new deck purchased specifically for divination works perfectly well, but so does one that has been sitting in a drawer for years. From a metaphysical perspective, an older deck already woven into the fabric of daily life may actually hold an advantage, since it already carries a connection to its owner.

If multiple decks are available, examine them closely before choosing. Playing cards vary more than most people realize, particularly in the court cards. Kings, Queens, and Jacks differ in posture, clothing, crown design, and the angle at which they hold their symbols. The design on the card backs also matters, as it may catch the eye and stir intuition during shuffling. No deck is inherently superior to another, but working with one that feels right strengthens the connection between reader and cards.

Removing the Jokers

The first practical step is to remove the Jokers from the deck. Despite a persistent claim that the Joker descends from the Fool card of the tarot, the two are unrelated. The Joker was invented in the United States during the 1860s for the card game Euchre, where it served as a trump card originally called the "Best Bower." The name "Joker" likely derives from the German spelling of Euchre (Juker). While both the Joker and the Fool function as trump cards in their respective games, the resemblance ends there.

Some readers choose to keep the Jokers and assign them meanings (the black Joker as the Fool, the red Joker as the Magician). This is a valid personal choice with historical precedent, since all playing cards were originally game pieces before becoming divinatory tools. However, the four suits already form a complete and well-balanced symbolic system without them.

Cleansing the Deck

Cleansing removes any residual energy the deck may carry, whether from years of card games or from the people who manufactured and packaged it. Even a new deck has passed through many hands before reaching its owner.

To cleanse a deck with salt, place the cards in a small bowl and bury them completely. Salt carries the magickal properties of purification and protection. A simple spoken intention works well during this process, something like: "Let this salt drive out any energy that does not serve my highest purpose." Leave the cards buried overnight. Working without the box allows direct contact between salt and cards for a stronger cleanse. Any stray grains caught between cards will fall out during the first few shuffles.

Consecrating the Deck

Consecration formally dedicates the deck to its new purpose as a tool for divination and magick. The method depends entirely on the practitioner's spiritual path. Norse-oriented practitioners might invoke Odin's guidance. Hellenic or Roman practitioners could petition Apollo. Wiccans may prefer to consecrate within a cast circle. The specific form matters less than the intention behind it: affirming that the work done with these cards carries purpose beyond entertainment.

Once consecrated, the deck should never be used for casual card games. If someone accidentally uses it for a round of Old Maid (which can happen, since it looks like any other deck), simply cleanse it with salt again and reconsecrate. No permanent harm is done.

Storing Your Deck

Two approaches exist, and neither is objectively better. Choose whichever resonates and stay consistent.

Sacred separation. Wrap the deck in linen or silk when not in use. Touch it only during readings or magickal work. Some readers following this approach never allow anyone else, including querents, to handle the cards. The deck remains hidden and untouched except when called upon, reinforcing its status as something set apart from mundane life.

Proximity bonding. Keep the deck close at all times. This approach draws on the magickal law of contagion: two things in sustained contact form a link between them. Having the deck nearby encourages impromptu readings and deepens the energetic bond between reader and cards. The deck becomes a constant companion rather than a reserved instrument.

Storage Options

Whichever overall approach feels right, the physical container matters too. A few reliable options:

Wooden boxes. Sturdy and long lasting, wood carries a grounding, earthy quality and doubles as a way to keep a deck out of reach of curious children or pets.

Silk wraps. Many traditions treat silk as a barrier against negative energy, making it a simple way to keep a deck's energy contained and undisturbed.

Pouches. A sewn pouch takes up little space and can be matched to a deck's color palette or theme for a personal touch.

Tins. A favorite for readers who travel with their deck, a tin keeps cards protected from damage and can often be found secondhand for character as well as function.

Whatever the choice, the deck should stay somewhere dry, safe, and easy to reach. A tool left buried under other belongings does little good when a reading is needed on short notice.

Cleansing and Charging Ritual

Even a well cared for deck can start to feel energetically heavy, or its messages may seem harder to read clearly than usual. When this happens, it helps to consider three things: personal energy, the energy of the reading space, and the energy of the deck itself. Readers who are especially sensitive to energy may benefit from a regular practice of clearing their space in addition to their cards.

A cleansing and charging ritual does not need to be elaborate to be effective, and a simple routine is easier to maintain consistently. A few approachable methods:

Resetting the order. Sorting the deck back into its original sequence resets it both physically and symbolically.

Moonlight. Leaving the deck on a windowsill during a full moon lets its light recharge the cards; the full moon occurs roughly thirteen times a year, offering regular opportunities for this practice.

Smoke. Passing the deck through the smoke of herbs such as sage, palo santo, or lavender is believed to clear away stagnant energy. Always research any plant material before burning it, and ventilate the space well.

Salt and herbs. Resting the deck in a shallow dish of rock salt and cleansing herbs can draw out unwanted energy while also reconsecrating the cards.

Crystals. Storing a deck alongside stones such as Citrine, Smoky Quartz, Black Tourmaline, or Amethyst is thought to support positivity, protection, and intuitive clarity, respectively.

Sound. Singing bowls, bells, or drums can be used to clear a deck's energy through vibration.

None of these practices are required. Some readers maintain an intentional ritual every time they use their deck, while others never reorder their cards at all. Either approach is valid as long as it fits the reader's own style.
