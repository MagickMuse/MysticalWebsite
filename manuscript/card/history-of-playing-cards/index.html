Card Grimoire

History of Playing Cards

Playing cards have traveled across continents and centuries, evolving from Chinese woodblock prints into the familiar deck of 52 cards used worldwide today.

Origins in China

The earliest known playing cards appeared during the Tang Dynasty in China, created sometime in the 800s using woodblock printing. These first decks lacked the suits and numbered sequences we recognize today, but they established the fundamental concept of a portable set of printed cards used for games and, likely, for fortune-telling.

Westward Through Persia and Egypt

As cards migrated along trade routes through Persia and into the Mamluk Sultanate (which governed Egypt and parts of western Arabia from the 1200s to the 1500s), they evolved considerably. The Egyptian Mamluk deck featured four suits, twelve pip cards, and three court cards per suit, a structure remarkably close to modern playing cards. The court cards displayed abstract geometric designs rather than human figures, likely reflecting Islamic restrictions on depicting sentient beings.

Arrival in Europe

Playing cards entered Europe in the 1300s, probably through Mamluk trade connections. The first European decks carried suits of Coins, Cups, Polo-Sticks, and Swords. Since polo was largely unknown in Europe at the time, Polo-Sticks quickly became Clubs or Cudgels.

From there, suit symbols continued to evolve along regional lines. Three broad categories emerged:

  • French: Diamonds, Hearts, Clubs, Spades (used in the UK and US today)
  • Latin: Coins, Cups, Clubs/Batons, Swords (used in Italy and Spain)
  • Germanic: Regional variations with distinct imagery

Birth of the Tarot

In the mid-1400s, card makers in northern Italian cities added a "trump" suit of twenty-two illustrated cards to Latin-style decks. These expanded decks were called carte da trionfi (triumph cards), known today as tarot. The oldest surviving examples were painted for the Visconti-Sforza family, rulers of Milan. Like standard playing cards, these decks were originally used for card games, not divination.

The trump cards featured elaborate imagery rooted in Roman Catholic symbolism, depicting figures like the Pope, the Devil, and Judgment Day. This religious connection was likely strategic, since card games were frequently condemned by both Church and secular authorities. Cards were sometimes called "the devil's picture book."

Cartomancy and Sortilege

Despite official disapproval, card games persisted as popular entertainment. Both playing cards and tarot were almost certainly used for divination alongside gaming. This practice, called cartomancy, belongs to a broader category of divination known as sortilege: the drawing of lots. Sortilege encompasses divination with dice, runes, and any closed set of symbols drawn at random and interpreted. Playing card divination can be documented as far back as 1540.

The Mystical Turn

Tarot began to take on overt mystical significance in the eighteenth century, when a French Protestant minister named Antoine Court speculated that the tarot derived from the Egyptian Book of Thoth. He also linked the twenty-two trump cards to the twenty-two letters of the Hebrew alphabet. These ideas generated fresh interest in tarot as a spiritual system, though the non-trump suits still looked much like ordinary playing cards, with an Eight of Swords being simply a picture of eight swords.

The Rider-Waite-Smith Revolution

The pivotal shift came in 1909, when occultist Arthur Edward Waite commissioned artist Pamela Colman Smith to illustrate all 78 cards of a tarot deck. Both were members of the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn. Smith created descriptive scenes for every card, not just the trumps, giving readers visual cues for interpretation. The deck, published by William Rider & Son in 1910, replaced overt Christian imagery with broader mystical symbolism (the Pope became the Hierophant, the Papess became the High Priestess).

This illustrated approach gave the Rider-Waite-Smith deck a practical advantage over both playing cards and earlier tarot decks. If a reader forgot the meaning of the Ten of Swords, the image of a figure impaled on the ground made the general interpretation obvious. Over time, tarot became the dominant tool for card divination.

Playing Cards Endure

Despite tarot's rise, playing cards never lost their place in the cartomancer's toolkit. Their long history, accessibility, and the unique quality of imageless divination continue to make them a powerful and legitimate system for magickal practice.
