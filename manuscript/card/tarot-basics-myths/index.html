Card Grimoire

Tarot Basics & Myths

A tarot deck functions as a tool, and like any tool it carries real limitations. It serves readings, meditation, self-exploration, guidance, and even play, but it cannot perform tasks outside that scope, the same way a hammer outperforms a deck of cards when driving a nail.

What Tarot Can and Cannot Do

Approaches to the deck vary widely, and no single method stands as the only correct one. Some readers treat tarot as sacred, building ritual and dedicated space around its use, or centering an entire magickal practice on the cards without ever using them for divination. Others approach the deck intellectually, exploring the psyche through its pictures and symbols. Some use it within healing or counseling work, while others read only for themselves in a purely intuitive manner. The practice takes the shape of whoever works with it.

Cards can aid in solving problems, offer new perspective on a situation, support meditation, or map a path forward. They can also mark the start of a professional practice built around helping others at pivotal moments.

A genuine, holistic reading stays as positive and healing as possible, even when a querent faces a difficult period. The harder cards in a deck deserve full attention rather than avoidance, holding space for both reader and querent through rocky passages.

Skill with the cards grows in direct proportion to time invested, the same as any other practiced craft. A deck will not clean a house or cook a meal, but its capacity to transform inner and outer experience remains remarkable for a simple set of cards.

Understanding Tarot Reversals

A tarot reversal occurs when a card appears upside down in a reading. Cards become reversed through shuffling, whether that happens naturally or a reader intentionally shuffles some cards into the deck reversed. Some readers work fluently with reversals, while others simply turn a reversed card upright and read only its upright meaning.

Reversed meanings often represent underlying issues, blocked energy, or delays in a card's influence on the reading. They can also point to a key energy that has yet to manifest or that has been diminished. This nuanced approach deepens personal introspection, acknowledging the many sided nature of a situation and encouraging consideration of different perspectives and underlying factors within the querent's story.

Whether a reader learns and works with reversed meanings comes down to individual style and preference. Some readers learn upright and reversed meanings side by side. Others build a solid foundation in upright meanings first and return to reversals later. Still other readers work only with upright cards, trusting that a reading's message comes through regardless of a card's orientation.

Both upright and reversed meanings appear throughout the card descriptions in this grimoire, enriching understanding of how each card can manifest in a reading. New readers are encouraged to practice with upright meanings alone for a while before incorporating reversals, gradually finding what approach works best with experience.

Tarot Myths Debunked

Common misconceptions about tarot persist widely, rooted largely in old stereotypes and religious rhetoric that outlived their original context. Some myths circulate even among readers themselves. Fact-check any claim that feels off.

Myth: A Tarot Deck Must Be Gifted, Never Bought

This enduring belief has no real basis. The best judge of which deck to work with is the reader. A gifted deck whose imagery fails to resonate can be genuinely frustrating to use. Any deck taken up for practice should feel personally inspiring, so buying a first, second, or third deck directly is entirely appropriate.

Myth: Tarot Is Evil, Linked to Dark Magick and Possession

This idea traces back to religious belief and outdated popular portrayals of tarot readers. The deck reflects the full range of life, including its challenging and taboo aspects, the same way life itself holds both ease and struggle. No tool carries inherent evil. Tarot is neutral and reflects the intentions of whoever uses it. A small number of people, generally those unfamiliar with actual tarot practice, believe the cards themselves hold power or attached spirits. In truth, the cards are simply cards, and meaning comes from interpretation. Ceremonial decks and alchemical symbolism appearing on some cards do not make them nefarious. They simply reflect esoteric design traditions that unfamiliarity tends to make suspect.

Myth: Tarot Cannot Be Read for Friends and Family

No rule restricts who may receive a reading. Friends and family who welcome a reading make excellent practice querents. Increased familiarity with someone's life can introduce bias over time, and delivering difficult truths to someone close can be uniquely hard, at which point referring them to another reader becomes reasonable. Anyone learning tarot should expect requests for readings once others find out, and should feel free to decline any reading that feels premature or uncomfortable.

Myth: Reading Tarot Requires Psychic Ability

Some psychics use tarot, but reading skill is learnable by anyone. Interpretation style remains a personal matter, and while intuitive ability often grows through consistent work with the cards, no baseline psychic gift is required to read effectively.

Myth: Drawing the Death Card Predicts Literal Death

If this were true, tarot events would be far more dangerous than they are. The Death card typically signals transformation, change, or the close of a phase rather than physical death. Genuine correlations with physical death are rare and generally coincide with an already known illness or an approaching end of life. The card marks a natural part of life's cycle rather than an omen of doom.

Myth: Self-Readings Are Always Inaccurate

Opinion divides on this point. Some readers avoid self-readings, believing personal investment distorts interpretation, while many others find real value in reading for themselves. Awareness of one's own closeness to a situation determines whether outside perspective would help. Daily draws and reflective self-readings remain valuable tools for learning card meanings and gaining insight into personal circumstances.

Myth: Predictions Are Set in Stone

Predictive readings describe potential outcomes based on current circumstances, not a fixed future. Decisions and actions taken afterward can shift the outcome described.

Myth: Skilled Readers Achieve Full Accuracy

No reader, regardless of experience, achieves complete accuracy, particularly in predictive work. Claims of perfect accuracy reflect overconfidence rather than reality.

Myth: Tarot Belongs to Specific Religions or Occult Traditions

Tarot originated as a card game and evolved into a flexible tool unbound by any single religious or occult framework. It adapts to whatever spiritual or secular context its user brings to it.

Myth: Only Professionals Read Accurately

Reading skill is not restricted to professionals. Accuracy depends far more on a reader's interpretive ability and a querent's openness than on professional status.

Myth: Certain Cards Always Predict Bad Outcomes

Cards such as Death, the Tower, and the Devil carry reputations for doom, but their meanings span a wide range, including liberation, transformation, and the shedding of old patterns.

Tarot functions as a card system suited to many kinds of readings and as a meaningful aid within magickal and ritual work. It does not fix a future in place. Free will and personal choice continue to shape outcomes moment to moment. Skepticism from others rarely shifts through argument alone, and continued growth as a reader over time remains the strongest response.

Tips for Reading and Interpreting the Cards

Reading tarot is a multifaceted skill, and there's no single right way to engage with a card or a spread. As you build experience, starting with single-card draws and working outward, the approaches below can help you unpack a card's meaning from different angles. Some will resonate with you more than others, and that's expected; everyone lands on their own mix of techniques that clicks.

Technique One: First Impressions

Start simply by looking at the card and noticing what draws your eye first — a figure, an object, a color. That initial impression often carries useful information. Ask yourself what caught your attention first and what it might suggest about the card's meaning.

Technique Two: Color Theory

Color choices in tarot carry symbolic weight rather than being purely decorative. Notice the dominant colors on a card — does red suggest passion or aggression? Does blue suggest calm or emotional depth? Consider what the card's color palette adds to its meaning.

Technique Three: Numbers Matter

Each number carries its own symbolic and vibrational quality — ones often point to beginnings, tens to completions, and so on. Ask what the card's number might reveal about cyclical patterns or key stages in your (or your querent's) life.

Technique Four: Figures and Symbols

Notice the human figures or animals shown on the card and what they're doing. Each carries its own symbolism and story that can point toward the energies or actions tied to the card's meaning.

Technique Five: Environment and Background

A card's setting — a garden, a desert, a battlefield — sets an emotional tone that's as informative as its figures. Ask whether the environment feels welcoming or unsettling, and let that inform the overall energy the card brings to a reading.

Technique Six: Symbols

Every symbol included on a card is intentional and often carries layered meaning. Reflect on the symbols you notice and where else you've seen them before, and consider their origins for a deeper reading.

Working through these questions invites ongoing rediscovery of your deck; even familiar cards can reveal something new. This approach deepens both your readings and your personal relationship with the cards, and it's especially useful when getting to know a new deck or one with unfamiliar imagery.

Preparing for a Tarot Reading

Every reader develops their own approach to preparing for and conducting readings, whether for themselves or others. Some build rituals around their practice and treat the cards as part of a larger spiritual discipline; others do nothing special before a reading. Preferences around space vary too: some readers want a dedicated reading space, while others read wherever and whenever.

Much of this comes down to personal style, but practicality plays a role as well. Reading in a crowded, loud venue might be genuinely difficult for a highly sensitive or empathic reader, yet if you've committed to reading at an event, you work with the setting you're given.

Exercise Six: What Kind of Tarot Reader Will I Be?

These questions can help you explore your own preferences as a reader. There are no right or wrong answers, and your answers may shift as you grow into the practice. Something that sounds ideal in theory doesn't always feel right once you try it. Experiment with different settings and rituals before and after your readings to find your own fit.

  • Do I have a spiritual practice, and would tarot be part of it?
  • What atmosphere do I want to create for my readings?
  • What parts of my personality do I want to bring into my readings?
  • Do I want to read for others, or just for myself?
  • Will I stick with one deck, or use several?
  • Where would I love to do my readings?
  • Are there places I know I don't want to read cards?
  • Do I have any other preferences for my reading environment?
  • Am I comfortable with others touching my deck, or do I want to be the only one who does?

Exercise Seven: Grounding and Centering Techniques

Grounding and centering practices stabilize your energy, reconnect you with your body, and bring you back into the present moment. Doing this after a reading helps close down any energy you opened during the session and signals to your mind and body that it's complete, which is especially useful if a reading leaves you feeling drained.

Below is a simple practice you can use before or after a reading. It may take a few tries before it really lands, so don't worry if you don't feel much the first time. Some readers like to hold a grounding stone, such as black tourmaline or hematite, during this exercise.

  1. Sit comfortably with your feet flat on the ground, barefoot if you can. Rest your hands in your lap and close your eyes gently.
  2. Bring your attention to your breath, in through the nose and out through the mouth, without forcing it. Let your focus settle at the soles of your feet; a little warmth or tingling there is normal.
  3. Picture roots growing from the bottom of your feet (or wherever your body touches the ground) deep into the earth: your foundation and connection to groundedness and security.
  4. With every breath, imagine those roots reaching deeper, drawing up energy and nourishment. Sit with that connection for a few breaths, even if you can't picture it vividly.
  5. Shift your attention to the crown of your head, imagining branches reaching upward into the sky, full of leaves catching sunlight and moving in the breeze, clearing any mental fog as they sway.
  6. Feel yourself connected to earth below and sky above, centered between them. With each inhale, draw in whatever healing or energy you need; with each exhale, release anything you're ready to let go of back into the earth to be transformed. Let the element of air clear your mind of intrusive thoughts.
  7. Offer a quiet thanks to the elements and whatever guides or higher forces resonate with you. Let your branches settle back into your body and your roots return to your feet. When you're ready, open your eyes. Consider jotting down what you experienced, and make sure to eat and drink something afterward.

Significator Cards

Significator cards are intentionally selected from the tarot deck to represent the querent in a reading. Usually, the card chosen represents the querent's zodiac sign or matches their personality. Selecting a significator card can anchor the reading around that person, letting you see how the significator interacts with the other cards drawn. Using a significator is especially helpful when reading without your querent physically present, keeping your focus on them throughout a remote reading.

Significator cards can help place your querent inside the reading by visually representing them, showing how the symbols in the reading or the other people involved affect them. Using a significator lets you see whether the surrounding energies are oppressive or supportive and how they interact with each other, giving you a clear visual snapshot of what's happening immediately around the reading's subject.

Selecting a significator card is straightforward. Find out your querent's zodiac sign, then choose the court card connected to that sign, usually the one matching their sun sign. What matters is the dignity the card represents, not the physical appearance of the figure on it.

Another approach is to pull the page, knight, queen, and king from the suit tied to your querent's zodiac sign. For example, for Taurus, an earth sign, pull the Page, Knight, Queen, and King of Pentacles, then show your querent all four and ask which one feels most like them. They don't need any tarot knowledge to answer.

Depending on a deck's artwork, you may not always like the card you'd typically use as your own significator. If that happens, feel free to pick a different card that better fits how you see yourself.

Once you've chosen a significator card, place it in the reading. Many readers set it in the center of the table and build the spread around it, so they can see what's opposing, above, below, and surrounding the querent. Others place it at the top of the table, separate from the spread itself.

Notice whether readings feel different with a significator card versus without one, and experiment to see what works for you. Significator cards should add value to your readings, not stress. If they feel distracting or unnecessary, there's no obligation to use them.
