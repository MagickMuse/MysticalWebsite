A writing and declaiming ceremony for composing a name poem that honors who you are now, especially after a forge experience of transformation.

Quantities

Candle

Directions

  1. Create sacred space, light a candle, and invoke Brigid's presence to illuminate insight and inspire word-weaving.
  2. Write down the attributes, symbols, turning points, and challenges that reveal your deepest self, proclaiming your power and whispering your vulnerabilities alike.
  3. When finished, stand and speak the poem aloud, declaiming it as an offering to Brigid.

Celtic bards created name poems used as prayer, meditation, affirmation, or spell, poems that could enlighten or confound those to whom they were spoken. Writing a name poem is a powerful way to honor what one is now, what one has been, and what one hopes to become, and it is especially meaningful after a forge experience that leaves the self transformed.

A name poem can take many forms. A bard might sing a poem of naming as both introduction and riddle, playing with double meanings that reveal their secrets only to those who understand the metaphors used. A fragment attributed to the Welsh bard Taliesin illustrates this style:

I have been a narrow sword,
A drop in the air,
A shining bright star,
A letter among words
In the book of origins.
I have been lanternlight
For a year and a day,
I have been a bridge
Spanning threescore rivers.

Another well-known example is the name poem sung by the poet Amairgin upon first setting foot on Ireland's shore:

I am a dewdrop in the sun.
I am the fairest of flowers.
I am a boar for boldness.
I am a salmon in a pool.
I am a lake in a plain.
I am a word of skill.
I am the point of a weapon.

To begin, create sacred space in whatever way feels right, at an altar or in a spot that feels creatively inspiring. Light a candle and invoke Brigid's presence to illuminate insight and inspire the word-weaving. Start writing down the attributes that would let someone know you very well, down to your deepest soul. Proclaim the most powerful self, whisper the most vulnerable self, and delve into the mysteries of the psyche to give them a name. Incorporate symbols that feel meaningful, astrological lore, alphabetical plays on the letters of a name, or poetic metaphors for life experiences.

Other things a name poem may include: spiritual turning points, events that shaped a life for good or ill, challenges faced, or the ways they were survived. Look back over the journey of a life to see where growth and deepening have occurred. A name poem may also give name to a shadow self: anger, fear, temper, failings, and faults, alongside skills and strengths. All these are part of a whole self and may need naming as part of healing.

Let the words pour out without too much editing; there is no way to do this wrong. A name poem may change and grow over time, marking only who a person is today, at this point on the path, so this is not a place for modesty or humility, except perhaps to proclaim them as virtues held.

When the poem is finished, read it aloud. In the bardic tradition, no poem was complete until it was declaimed by voice: stand and speak it freely, sing it, offer it to Brigid. It is a powerful exercise to share in a trust-filled group as well as alone.
