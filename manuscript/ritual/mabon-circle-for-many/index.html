🕯️Mabon Circle for Many

◇Form
Circle RiteOffering Rite
◇Method
InvocationChant/MantraMeditationDirectional MagickElemental
◇Category
Ceremony
◇Quantities
- Effigy made of wheat or other harvested plants - Wreaths made of flowers (one for each ritual participant if possible) - Bell - Libations: cider, wine, juices, or olive oil - Beer - Tray, table, or flat board for altar - Jar containing soil from each participant's garden (or potting soil) - Box that fits the effigy - Binders or cheat sheets with ritual parts for participants - Brown or black candles - Hurricane lamps or wind-shelter candleholders - Silly hat or paper crown for the harvest lord - Feast table with autumn decorations (leaves, acorns, tablecloths in browns, reds, yellows, oranges, greens) - Pictures or figures of geese and pigs - Collection box for food shelf donations
◇Directions
1. Purpose: This ritual connects participants with the combination of pride, grief, loss, and gratitude that Mabon brings forth. It affirms the links of the group to one another, helping them recognize each other as part of a functional community while honoring and giving thanks for the sacrifice of the dying god. Like Harvest Home, the entire group will have different, equally important roles; those in the ritual represent the reapers and the Lord and Lady of the Manor. Those preparing the feast represent the village or greater community and handle the business of life while the workers are in the field. 2. Setting: Outside. A picnic shelter is ideal for this type of ritual. 3. Pre-Ritual Preparation: Before beginning, divide ritual roles. Draw from a hat (or use a similar random method) to determine who is in the ritual and who conducts the feast. At least three group members should handle the feast. 4. Those planning the feast should be given at least a week to coordinate details, including a call to the entire group for potluck dishes. 5. Those handling the feast should also plan for music and games. 6. On the day of the ritual, set up a clean altar space. 7. The altar should be as clear as possible. 8. The bulk of the space should go to housing the box for the effigy. 9. Additional space should be set aside for libation bowls or simple representations of the God and Goddess. 10. On either side of the effigy, place candles inside hurricane lamps or similar candleholders that can block wind. 11. Designate a table outside the circle for the feast. 12. You may also collect canned goods and personal items for donation to a local food shelf; place a box at the end of the table for this purpose. 13. Decorate the edges of the table with autumn leaves, acorns, and tablecloths in browns, reds, yellows, oranges, and greens. 14. Also, add pictures or figures of geese and pigs. 15. Assign all participants a specific cleanup task ahead of time. 16. Pre-Ritual Meditation: One person should read this guided meditation to the entire group before splitting off into the ritual circle and the feast preparation circle. Have everyone who is able sit on the ground or on a picnic bench. Then begin the meditation: 17. Take three deep breaths. 18. (Pause) Feel the air around you on your skin. 19. Take off your shoes, if able. 20. Feel the ground against your feet, the way it presses up to you as you press your feet down against it. 21. Listen to what's going on around you: wind blowing, other people shouting, laughing, or talking, and traffic roaring in the distance. 22. Everything you hear is a part of the life of the land. 23. You are a part of the life of the land. 24. Take a moment to breathe that into you. 25. (Pause) Feel the pulse of the land beneath your feet. 26. Feel how it reaches up and pulses into you. 27. Take a moment to feel how it pulses into the people next to and around you. 28. Take a moment to imagine how a plant might feel that pulse. 29. (Pause) 30. Now, picture yourself standing in the middle of a wheat field. 31. Each sheaf reaches toward the sky. 32. These stalks are gigantic, at least a foot taller than the tallest among us. 33. You have a row to yourself. 34. Through the pulse of the earth, feel your companions standing in the other rows. 35. A wind blows through the wheat, causing a ripple of grain to run through the field like a wave. 36. You see it as one stalk knocking into another before bending back upright. 37. In the wind, you hear a whispering: "This is how it feels, this is how it feels." 38. Impressions come to you of the seed buried in the earth. 39. You feel hot and moist and dark, the sense of sweat a reassurance of life. 40. Each drop of moisture, each touch of pleasant warmth makes you feel more expansive until, at last, you burst free, raising your body up to meet the sun. 41. In this space, reaching for the sun, you feel vulnerable. 42. Loving warmth becomes too hot, too close, and the cooling rain helps you breath in and out according to that subterranean beat that regulates you. 43. Then the rain keeps pouring, more than you can drink. 44. You start to feel your roots loosen and again there is that sense of vulnerability. 45. Who do you call to for protection? 46. Suddenly the rain stops. 47. You find you have grown stronger. 48. When wind blows, you bend but do not break. 49. When rain comes, you tighten your roots against the soil and drink deep, raising it to your crown, your crown always reaching toward the solar rays. 50. One day, you find yourself preoccupied. 51. You have grown as much as you can, and now you find yourself extending, expanding. 52. Your body extends and changes and, in a burst of relief, you drop seeds from your stalks, sweet children stemming from you that will, in an ecstasy of heat and moisture, someday burst from the ground. 53. You feel the satisfaction of having finished. 54. Then, you feel just a little less heat from the sun, just a little more rain. 55. You are tired and wish to rest. 56. Rather than stand tall, you want to lie flat on the field as the ground cools and tightens under the burden of frost. 57. This is as it should be. 58. (Pause) 59. Come back to yourself, standing in the wheat fields. 60. Now the wheat is lying flat on the ground. 61. The harvest came, and reapers moved by you as you communed with the grain. 62. You can see one another, standing in the wheat rows, you can feel that sense of completion, the hard work that went to making this field flat, and yet more hard work is to come in converting it to things needed to survive the winter. 63. From somewhere beneath the stalks you hear a voice whispering, "Remember this feeling." 64. Take a moment to pause and ask, "Who are you?" Take a moment to let the Mother of the Grain tell you who she is to you. 65. (Pause) 66. Come back to where you began in time and space. 67. Wiggle your fingers and toes, take a big, deep, refreshing breath, and open your eyes. 68. You are ready to begin this ritual. 69. The Ritual: The Priest or Priestess casts the circle, saying: 70. Circle, full circle, we come to this point where the harvest is waning though our spirits are buoyant. Into this circle, we call those who labor. Once we give thanks, a blessed feast we will savor, with thanks and through grace, we encircle this place to give thanks for the harvest and give due to the Fates! 71. A member of the group calls to the east, saying: 72. Hail to the east, to the winds that have blessed us! Be gentle of breeze and gentle of gust, it is by your currents that the seeds have their thrust! 73. A member of the group calls to the south, saying: 74. Hail to the south, to the warm that does linger. Keep us so sweetly with wood and with cinder, it is by your passion that we turn seed to timber! 75. A member of the group calls to the west, saying: 76. Hail to the west, to the waters and wet! Give moisture to us and nourish the rest, it is by your succor and sweat that we give our respect! 77. A member of the group calls to the north, saying: 78. Hail to the north, to the earth turned and tilled, give to us the place where our seed may be spilled, it is by your mercy that we have a future to build! 79. The Priest or Priestess invokes the Goddess, saying: 80. Hail to the Goddess, Great Mother and Lover, she who guides us from hot work to winter slumber. In your grief do you lead us, in your joy we do rise, as your husband lays dying, we give thanks for the wheat, corn, and rye! 81. Pour a libation of apple cider, red wine, juice, or honey. 82. Then, the assisting Priest or Priestess invokes the God, saying: 83. Hail to the God, Green Man of the soil, we have followed you each season to know where to toil. We mourn you, we grieve you, we lay you to rest, a great king you are and you gave us your best. We thank you, we mourn you, we raise our hands to the sky; you have allowed us this living all that you may die. 84. Pour a libation of beer and honey and light a brown or black candle to indicate fertility and sorrow. 85. The Priest or Priestess moves to stand before the effigy, facing the west. 86. He or she anoints it with honey and olive oil, saying: 87. We thank you, Lord of the Harvest, Divine King of the Land. In the moment of your dying, let us thank you for the good you have brought us. 88. He or she moves to the middle of the circle, where the assisting priest or priestess joins him or her. 89. The Priest or Priestess that invoked the Goddess says: 90. Come forth, lord and lady of the reapers! 91. Two members of the group, both wearing garlands on their heads, act as pallbearers, hoisting the effigy from the table and walking it around the circle. 92. They stop together before each member present. 93. Each person confronted thanks the effigy for the good things that happened that year, for lessons learned, and for difficulties survived. 94. They may ask the king also to bring words of blessing to loved ones now passed beyond the veil. 95. If desired, letters of petition or small offerings may be tucked inside the coffin. 96. As each person gives his or her personal thanks, the others in the circle should begin a solemn chant. 97. This helps keep attention to the ritual itself and allows those speaking to the effigy some modicum of privacy. 98. One such chant might be: 99. Hail to the King of the dying light, well earned is your rest at autumn tide. Thoughts of your grace at summer's height will give strength to us through the winter. 100. When the circumambulation has finished, the pallbearers should set the effigy down at the feet of the two Priest or Priestesses in the center of the circle, remove the wreaths from their heads, and confer them to those at the center of the circle, saying: 101. Gracious Lady and Gracious Lord of this land, we crown you. 102. You are that which the harvest has given us. 103. The Priest or Priestess that invoked the Goddess rings a bell as the Priest or Priestess that invoked the God announces: 104. This day I invite you, all who have labored, to break bread with me! 105. The attendees should raise a cheer. 106. The Priest or Priestess should then set the effigy back on the table. 107. The Goddess-invoking Priest or Priestess should sprinkle a handful of dirt over the figure, saying: 108. Go to the earth, O Dying God. The grounds will freeze, and we will grieve. We will greet your return in the spring with great joy! 109. The accompanying Priest or Priestess says: 110. This night will be a gracious feast; in it, we remember him who gave us all in life that we call sweet. 111. Everyone: Blessed be. 112. The Priest or Priestess that performed the first invocation pours a final libation to the God and Goddess on the ground, saying: 113. Blessed Lady, our thoughts are with you as we go into the dark of the year, as you go into the dark of the earth. Be welcome at our feast this night; our solemnities are done! Blessed be! 114. The assisting priest or priestess then steps up, taking the bottle from the first priest or priestess and pours a libation, saying: 115. Blessed Harvest Lord, we salute you and give great thanks for your sacrifice. It is by your body that we may live and live again. Be welcome at our feast this night; it is in your honor we have it! Blessed be! 116. The Priest or Priestesses (Lord and Lady of the Land) step back into the circle as those who called the original quarters resume their original places at each quarter point in the circle. 117. The person who called to the north raises his or her arms and says: 118. To the north! The soil now may rest, frozen in the Lady's breast. We shall see you in the spring when you render us the young new king! Farewell! So mote it be! 119. The person who called to the west raises his or her arms and says: 120. To the west! We watch the streams stop their flow and await your rain that turns to snow. Blanket us in your cold, while deep in the earth the new god grows. Farewell! So mote it be! 121. The person who called to the south raises his or her arms and says: 122. To the south! We move our fires from field to hearth, gathering embers close to our hearts. In light is hope and comfort given, in darkest days we come to you, our spark within. Farewell! So mote it be! 123. The person who called to the east raises his or her arms and says: 124. To the east! The breezes must give way to gales that bring the coldest streams of air. Beneath your wind the earth will pale, and we will face what we must bear. Farewell! So mote it be! 125. The Priest or Priestess who cast the circle steps to the edge of the circle in the north and moves widdershins (counterclockwise) to open the circle, saying: 126. The circle cast this autumn night has filled with solemnity and delight; we take it to bless the harvest table: Come ye, come ye all who are able! So mote it be! 127. The Feast: Retire to the feast that features as much garden or homemade fare as possible. While those performing in the circle enacted the ritual role of the reapers, those preparing the feast will be enacting the ritual role of the villagers. The ritual of meal preparation is a common one. Most adults know how to set a table and cook for a guest. Meals for large groups usually only happen on holidays. 128. Do what you need to clean and prepare the food, add any decorations to the table you can assemble from what you find in the space around you. 129. After all is prepared, chant a blessing together over the meal: 130. Corn mother, grain mother, Demeter, Ceres, hail to you! Great mother, blessed mother, bless this food as we praise you! 131. While people are eating, one of those who prepared the feast should don the silly hat and wander around the table, collecting coins to go to the food shelf or to raise funds for the next ritual. 132. Once finished, the "harvest lord" should raise a toast to the group, highlighting the themes of gratitude and loss and mentioning accomplishments of other group members. 133. Closing: During cleanup, someone should put on music so that people may start dancing or chatting once they finish their tasks. A game of horseshoes (the closest equivalent to the wheat-felling games of Harvest Home) is an appropriate way to end the celebration. Any children in attendance can go collect acorns and nuts, in keeping with the old nutting traditions. Use the nuts in crafts and spellwork for the coming Samhain season.
◇Description
A large group ritual honoring the dying god and the harvest through shared ceremony, effigy procession, and communal feast. Participants take roles as reapers and feast preparers, mirroring the Harvest Home tradition where the entire community has different, equally important roles in celebrating the autumn equinox.
