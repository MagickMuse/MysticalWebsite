◇Directions
1. Prepare the altar with the wine, incense, breads, sweets, ribbons, baskets, torches, holy water bowl, and crowns within reach, then form two processions: the bride's on the left facing the altar, the groom's on the right. 2. Lead the bride's procession with a piper, two torch bearers, a young girl carrying rose petals and wheat, and the bride accompanied by her closest relative. 3. Lead the groom's procession with two torch bearers, the groom and the paranymphos, and a young boy carrying red and white ribbons. 4. Bring both processions to the altar in a semicircle, crown the couple, and have the children distribute the ribbons and rose petals with wheat to the guests. 5. Light the altar fire, recite the Opening invocation to the Blissful Gods, and libate red wine. 6. Make holy water from a lit olive twig and aromatic water; offer a lock of the bride's hair to Artemis (or roses to Apollon from the groom), then wash both foreheads with the holy water. 7. Have the bride recite the Prayer to Artemis and the Moirae with frankincense and perfume, then libate red wine. 8. Have the groom's mother offer honey and walnuts to the bride with the accompanying words. 9. Have the groom recite the Prayer to Apollon with frankincense, then libate red wine. 10. Give a loaf of bread to each of the bride and groom, recite the Prayer to Zeus and Hera with styrax and perfume incense, then libate red wine. 11. Lay the breads on the altar and have the priest/ess join the couple's wrists with the gold ribbon and recite the validating prayer. 12. Circle the altar three times clockwise with the paranymphos while guests shower the couple with rose petals and wheat, exclaiming "prosper" three times after each circle. 13. At the altar's centre, have the couple recite the Hymn to Aphrodite and Eros together with perfume incense, then libate red wine. 14. Have the bride's mother offer sweet quince preserve to the groom with the accompanying words. 15. Pour libations and have the couple recite the Prayer to Peitho together with perfume incense. 16. Close together with the closing invocation, libate red wine, and seal the working. 17. Have the children offer sweets to the guests with the accompanying words. 18. At the couple's home or reception, shower them with flowers, grains, raisins, and walnuts, then serve the wedding cake.
◇Description
A Hellenic wedding ceremony that founds a new household under the protection of the gods of matrimony, joining the couple through prayers, libations, and a ritual joining of wrists.

A Hellenic wedding ceremony founding a new Oikos (EE-kos, household) under the protection of the gods of matrimony: Zeus, Hera, Artemis, Aphrodite, Eros, Peitho, and the Moirae. The version adapted by the modern Labrys community condenses the traditional three-day rite into a single day.

Ceremony Script

Prepare the altar in the chosen venue, indoor or outdoor, and arrange the wine, incense, breads, sweets, ribbons, baskets, torches, holy-water bowl, and crowns within easy reach. Once the guests have taken their places, form the two matrimonial processions. The bride's procession stands on the left facing the altar; the groom's on the right.

The bride's procession is led by a piper, followed by a pair of torch bearers (one male, one female). Behind them walks a young girl whose parents are both still living, carrying a basket of rose petals and wheat. The bride follows, accompanied by her closest relative (usually her father or brother).

The groom's procession is led by a pair of torch bearers (one male, one female), followed by the groom and the paranymphos (pah-RAH-neem-fos, the sponsor of the marriage, of any gender). Behind them walks a young boy whose parents are both still living, carrying a basket of red and white ribbons.

The processions move toward the altar simultaneously and form a semi-circle around it. Facing the altar, the groom stands on the right, the bride on the left, and the paranymphos stands between them. The paranymphos places crowns of laurel, olive leaves, or fresh seasonal flowers, woven with red and white ribbons, on the couple's heads. The young boy and young girl distribute the ribbons and the rose petals with wheat among the guests. The couple, the paranymphos, and the torch bearers remain at the altar; the children and processional attendants join the guests.

The officiating priest/ess lights the fire at the altar and opens the ceremony, addressing the Blissful Gods as great friends of mortals and pillars of the world:

Come, O' Blissful Gods,

great friends of mortals,

mighty pillars of the world.

Come, Immortal Gods,

by the grace of our libations, freely grant to us mortals,

untainted virtue, great happiness, and well-being.

The priest/ess takes a twig of olive branch, lights it from the flame on the altar, and extinguishes it in the bowl of aromatic water, creating holy water. The paranymphos cuts a small lock of the bride's hair, and the bride offers it to Artemis by placing it on the altar. The groom does the same, or alternatively offers red roses to Apollon. Both the bride and groom then wash their foreheads with the holy water.

The bride invokes the Goddess Artemis and the Moirae, lighting the frankincense and adding a measure of perfume:

I call You, O' royal Artemis (AR-teh-mees),

illustrious daughter of Zeus (ZOOS), modest maid,

of the brave heart,

nurturer, protectress of young children,

and Moirae (MEE-rye), friendly children of Nyx (NEEKS),

come to me who prays,

You who are clad in purple,

You are the authorities of good order,

perfect eye of Zeus.

Lakhesis (lah-KHEH-sees), Klotho (KLO-thoh) and Atropos (AH-tro-pos),

only You see the whole of mortal life.

Hear my prayers, accept these holy libations,

grant a bright and thriving life,

full of merriment.

Libate red wine.

The groom's mother now offers honey and walnuts to the bride and says:

"These things are most important; to love and to embrace and to be friends."

The groom calls upon the God Apollon, with frankincense incense:

I remember and do not forget Apollon (ah-POL-lon) Hekateios (heh-kah-TAY-os).

Come, O' Blessed Paian (PY-an), bright Phoibos (FY-bos), Ie Ie (EE-eh EE-eh), bestower of bliss,

Thou who sees the expanse of the sky

and the happy earth from up high.

From ages past, it is a sacred tradition to invoke You.

Come to me who prays and disperse

dread disease and suffering.

Libate red wine.

The paranymphos gives a whole-grain loaf of bread to the bride and another loaf to the groom. The priest/ess lights the styrax and additional perfume incense and prays to Zeus and Hera, the gods of matrimony:

I invoke the Great, the Pure, the Imperishable, the Much-honoured,

Father of the Blessed gods and of men; Greatest Zeus (ZOOS),

and Hera (HEH-rah), Queen of all, Blessed wife of Zeus,

Mistress of Matrimony, well-disposed Mother.

Come to us and grant to the newly-weds, health and nurturing peace,

a life of mirth and well-being, a life of mutual dedication.

Libate red wine.

The bride and groom lay the breads they were given on the altar. The priest/ess now joins their wrists together with a gold ribbon and validates the marriage with this prayer:

The gods have assumed the protection of your life together;

a sweet life, of goodness and peace; a life of plenty

and whatsoever each may desire.

Together with the paranymphos, the newlyweds circle the altar three times in a clockwise direction. While they circle, the guests shower them with the rose petals and wheat. At the completion of each circle, the paranymphos stops and exclaims thrice:

ΕΥΔΑΙΜΟΝΕΣ, ΕΥΔΑΙΜΟΝΕΣ, ΕΥΔΑΙΜΟΝΕΣ!

(eff-DEH-mo-ness, eff-DEH-mo-ness, eff-DEH-mo-ness, prosper, prosper, prosper)

The guests repeat the cry each time.

When the third circle is complete, the couple, having reached the centre of the altar, recite the Hymn to Aphrodite and Eros together with musical accompaniment. The bride begins, and the bride and groom take turns line by line. Light the mixed perfume incense:

We praise the Golden-wreathed,

modest, matrimonial,

exceedingly beautiful Aphrodite (ah-fro-DEE-teh).

You who wears upon your immortal head

a well woven wreath of gold,

You rouse a sweet yearning

in Gods and men alike,

Khaire (KHY-reh), Cyprus-born Goddess,

who brings gentle holy gifts to mortals.

And You, sweet Eros (EH-ros),

winged, powerful archer,

who fills people with fire,

keen key-bearer of all,

filling our lives with joy.

Come O' Blessed Gods

who grants desired descendants,

friends of men and women,

nurturers of mortals.

Libate red wine.

The mother of the bride now offers sweet quince preserve to the groom and says:

"May you have a prosperous household and treat each other with affection and kindness."

The paranymphos pours libations of red wine on the altar and the couple pray together to Peitho (PAY-tho), with perfume incense:

Sweet renowned Peitho (PAY-tho),

Beneficence and grace to mortals,

Your word is of the greatest sanctity,

It is to You, O' Goddess, that we open our hearts.

I [bride's name]

and I [groom's name]

pray that our journey in life be prosperous,

with love and mutual commitment,

that we may not consider anything more important

or equal to our Love,

and as Love joins

and makes everything into one,

may it join our lives,

our minds and our souls.

Close the ceremony together:

Khairete (KHY-reh-teh) O' Blessed Gods,

Be with us always,

Never leave,

Cease all illness

and drive away sorrow.

Libate red wine and seal the working with ΓΕΝΟΙΤΟ (yeh-NEE-toh, it is done).

The young children of the wedding offer sweets to the guests, saying in a soft voice:

ΕΦΥΓΟΝ ΚΑΚΟΝ, ΕΥΡΟΝ ΑΜΕΙΝΟΝ

(EH-fee-gon KAH-kon, EFF-ron ah-MAY-non, depart evil, retain the good).

When the couple arrives at their home or at the reception, the guests shower them with a mixture of flowers, grains, raisins, and walnut kernels. The wedding cake is then offered to the couple. It is made of whole wheat flour, sesame seeds, and honey, all symbols of abundance and fruitfulness.

Notes

The paranymphos is the sponsor of the marriage and may be of any gender. In the procession, both children carrying the ceremonial baskets must have both parents still living, a traditional sign of fortunate auspice. The crowns of the bride and groom may be made of laurel leaves, olive leaves, or fresh seasonal flowers, and are decorated with red and white ribbons. The closing wedding cake of whole wheat, sesame, and honey is meant to symbolise abundance and fruitfulness for the new household.

Several of the Moirae's individual names (Klotho, Lakhesis, Atropos) are tagged through their parent collective.
