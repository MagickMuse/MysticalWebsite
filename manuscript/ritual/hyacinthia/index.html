Hyacinth

A two-day Spartan festival held in July honoring the death and renewal that the hyacinth carries. The first day is given to grief, with mourning rites at the tomb of Hyacinthus for the dying of vegetation and for every loss the year has brought. The second day turns to revelry, honoring Apollo with song, dance, and games, and celebrating the joy of the harvest. As a rite of personal practice, the Hyacinthia walks the same arc: a deliberate descent into sorrow, then a deliberate climb back into joy, with the hyacinth as the flower that ties the two halves together.

Quantities

A bundle of fresh hyacinths, ideally enough to lay several on a small altar on the first day and to wear or hold the rest on the second.

A candle in a dark or muted color for the first day, and a candle in gold, yellow, or sun-bright color for the second.

A small offering bowl, a libation of wine or fresh water, and bread or fruit for both days.

Music of your choosing for the second day, ideally something with a lyre, harp, or sung melody.

Directions

  1. On the first day, set the altar simply: a dark cloth, the muted candle, the offering bowl, and a few hyacinth blooms laid as if at a grave.
  2. Light the candle in silence and name aloud what you are grieving this year, whether a person, a relationship, a project, a season, or the general dying back of the green world as summer matures toward harvest.
  3. Pour the libation into the bowl, offer the bread or fruit at the foot of the candle, and stay with the grief as long as it needs. Do not sing, do not celebrate, do not rush.
  4. When you are ready, snuff the candle and leave the altar undisturbed overnight.
  5. On the second day, change the cloth to a bright color, replace the muted candle with the sun-bright one, and add the remaining hyacinths in a vase or laid in a circle around the candle.
  6. Light the new candle and speak Apollo's name aloud, thanking him for what has been planted, what has ripened, and what is to come.
  7. Play the music you have chosen, sing if you can, and let the rite turn into something close to a small feast: share the bread and pour fresh wine or juice into the bowl.
  8. Dance, even if only in place, and offer the joy of your body as devotion.
  9. When the candle has burned down or you are ready to close, take one of the hyacinths and press or dry it to keep until next year as a memory of the turn from grief to gladness.
