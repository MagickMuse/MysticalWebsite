The Wheel of Fortune

An ancient Mesopotamian-inspired five-part ritual to prevent evil omens, scary dreams, or inauspicious signs from manifesting as actual misfortune. A group ceremony performed by others upon the at-risk person, involving purification, tamarisk cleansing, incense, bells, drums, food offerings to river spirits, destruction of evil emblems, and a prescribed closing exit.

Quantities

Flour (to cast the enclosure circle)
Water for bathing
Tamarisk branches (for cleansing/asperging)
Incense
Bell (copper preferred)
Drum
Food and aromatics (offerings to spirits)
Candles or magick lamps
Protective amulets (to wear for at least seven days afterward)

Directions

  1. Create an enclosure (the Mesopotamians would have cast a magick circle with flour).
  2. Perform purification rites within the enclosure. The targeted person is bathed, then shaved or waxed and bathed again. Tamarisk branches are used in the cleansing process (for asperging, sweeping, or a barrida-style ritual). Cleanse the person with incense. Sweep the area clean. Light magick lamps or candles. Ring a bell (copper favored) and beat a drum.
  3. Offer food and aromatics to the spirits and the deified river or the deity in the river, because this is how the spell remnants will be carried away.
  4. Destroy any emblems of evil, if there are any. (This spell could also be used to remove an object-driven jinx.)
  5. Close the ritual: Spin the person around and tell them to leave without looking back or around. Face forward. A magickal prescription may also be given: the person should wear protective amulets for at least the following seven days.

About This Ritual

Evil omens, scary dreams, inauspicious signs, or any other portents of misfortune don't necessarily mean that bad luck is inevitable. Take enchanted steps to stop the Wheel of Fortune turning in the wrong direction.

The ancient Mesopotamians possessed complex rituals to prevent evil portents from translating into actual reality. Five thousand years later, the basic steps — if not the exact ritual — may be reproduced.

Typically, these rituals involved five components. Someone else performs the cleansing for the targeted person. Five thousand years ago, it would have been a specialist priestess, priest, or shaman. Today, several people can get involved.

Items Needed

  • Flour (to cast the enclosure circle)
  • Water for bathing
  • Tamarisk branches (for cleansing/asperging)
  • Incense
  • Bell (copper preferred)
  • Drum
  • Food and aromatics (offerings to spirits and the river deity)
  • Candles or magic lamps
  • Protective amulets (for the person to wear afterward, for at least seven days)

Five-Part Ritual

Step 1: Create the enclosure. Cast a magic circle with flour.

Step 2: Purification rites. The targeted person is bathed, then shaved or waxed and bathed again. Tamarisk branches are used in the cleansing process — whether for asperging or a barrida-style sweeping is unclear from historical sources. Cleanse the person with incense. Sweep the area clean. Light magic lamps or candles. Ring a bell (copper was favored by the Mesopotamians) and beat a drum.

Step 3: Offerings. Offer food and aromatics to the spirits and the deified river or the deity in the river. This is how the spell remnants will be carried away.

Step 4: Destroy emblems of evil. If any objects are driving the jinx, destroy them at this stage.

Step 5: Close the ritual. Spin the person around and tell them to leave without looking back or around. Face forward. Traditionally, finding a tavern was part of the close — the ancient Mesopotamians found spiritual significance in bars and their priestess-barmaids, a connection linked to devotion to Inanna-Ishtar, though full details are not yet understood.

Post-Ritual Prescription

The person should wear protective amulets for at least the following seven days.
