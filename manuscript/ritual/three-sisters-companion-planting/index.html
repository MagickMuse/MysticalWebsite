🌱Three Sisters Companion Planting

An Indigenous companion-planting method that grows corn, beans, and squash together as a living relationship of mutual support.

Quantities

Corn seed, at least six to eight kernels per mound, of a flour or dent variety that grows tall and sturdy
Pole bean seed, four to six per mound, ideally a climbing variety such as scarlet runner
Squash or pumpkin seed, two to three per mound, of a sprawling rather than bush type
A small offering of tobacco, cornmeal, or water for the soil before planting
A garden bed in full sun with well-drained soil, prepared into a low mound roughly a yard across

Directions

  1. Prepare the mound after the last frost, once the soil is warm to the touch.
  2. Stand at the edge of the mound, name each of the three plants aloud, and thank the land for receiving them.
  3. Leave the offering (tobacco, cornmeal, or water) at the center of the mound.
  4. Plant the corn first, in a small circle at the top of the mound, pushing each kernel about an inch down and covering with soil, then water gently.
  5. Wait two to three weeks until the corn reaches about six inches and stands on its own.
  6. Plant the beans in a ring around the corn, each about a hand's width from a stalk.
  7. Wait another week, then plant the squash or pumpkin seeds at the outer edge of the mound where the vines can sprawl outward.
  8. Let the plants grow undisturbed except for gentle weeding, watering as needed and keeping soil mounded around the corn.
  9. At harvest, gather the three together, dry and store them together, and ideally cook them into a single meal.
  10. Speak thanks again at harvest, naming each Sister.

A companion planting method developed by Indigenous nations of the Americas, in which corn, beans, and squash or pumpkin are planted together in a single mound. The three plants form a living relationship in which each one feeds the others: corn gives the bean a structure to climb, the bean fixes nitrogen into the soil for the corn, and the squash shades the roots of the other two while discouraging animals from grazing. Eaten together, the three form a complete protein.

⚠️ This is an Indigenous practice with deep cultural and spiritual roots in many nations of the Americas. Approach the method with respect, attribute it to its originating cultures when you teach it, and do not strip the names of the Sisters or claim the practice as your own invention.

Notes

The choice of corn variety matters. Sweet corn is too short and too short-lived to support pole beans well. Use a flour, dent, or flint corn if you can. The squash can be replaced with pumpkin without changing the relationship, and some traditions use a fourth plant, a sunflower or a sunflower-like helper, at the perimeter to attract pollinators and to mark the corners of the garden. Bean roots fix nitrogen in the soil even after the plants are gone, so the same bed in the following year is excellent for hungry plants like greens or brassicas. If you cannot plant in a mound, the method still works in a flat bed with the same staggered timing. Do not eat raw beans of any kind; cook them first, ideally with a pinch of wood ash in the cooking water, which neutralizes the phytohemagglutinin protein that raw beans contain.
