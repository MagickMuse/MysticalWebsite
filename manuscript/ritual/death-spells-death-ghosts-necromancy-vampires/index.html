Death Spells: Death, Ghosts, Necromancy & Vampires

Death magick is not about causing death, and there are no killing curses here. As a category, death spells concern the topic of death itself, its aftermath, its spiritual mechanics, and its effects on both the departed and those left behind. Though practitioners may be buried with their tools, the overwhelming majority of death spells are crafted for the living.

This is a spell category that walks the edge between religion, spirituality, and magick. Work these spells, or adapt them, according to your own spiritual framework.

What Death Spells Actually Do

Death spells typically fall into three categories:

  • Preparing and propitiating the dead so they move peacefully into the next existence. Correct funerary rites are essential. Incorrect or absent rites may produce ghosts, demons, vampires, or general spiritual unrest.
  • Protecting the living from the dead: shielding against lingering presences, hostile energies, or souls that could not properly cross over.
  • Accessing the dead's special powers: including necromancy, petitioning ancestor spirits, or working with the dead's accumulated wisdom, with or without their cooperation.

Death Magick & Modern Culture

Many of these workings are ancient and assume that the dead are prepared at home, by someone who either knew the deceased or was trained in proper magickal procedure. This stands in stark contrast to contemporary industrialized society, where death is outsourced to professionals and kept carefully out of sight.

Magickal traditions hold the preparation of the body and funerary rites to be critically important. The consequences of getting it wrong affect both the living and the dead:

  • Without correct funerary ritual, a soul becomes vulnerable, susceptible to capture by a sorcerer, who may put it to work as a magickal instrument or servitor. This belief is documented at least as far back as ancient Babylon and echoes in traditions like the duppies of the West Indies.
  • A spirit unable to transition properly has nowhere to go, and may take out that frustration on the living. This is especially true of those who died through violence or were never identified.
  • In many traditions, death leads to ancestorhood: the honored dead serve and protect their descendants. Without proper rites and ongoing offerings, that protective relationship cannot form, leaving the living undefended and the dead resentful.

Historical Context: Preparing to Die

This was not always solely a concern for the living. Ancient Egyptian and Tibetan traditions required that certain spells and rituals be learned in life so that they could be performed after death. The soul navigating its afterlife examinations the way a student prepares for a difficult entrance exam.

The Tibetan Book of the Dead (The Book of the Great Liberation) and the Egyptian Book of the Dead (The Book of Going Forth by Day) document these traditions. Over time in Egypt, the practice shifted: the wealthy were simply buried with Books of the Dead as amulets, essentially glorified hall passes for the afterlife.

📝
A note on organization: Spells to prevent the formation of ghosts and vampires, particularly those centered on funerary rites and immediate post-death rituals, belong with Death Spells. Spells dealing with already-formed, "mature" ghosts and vampires fall under their own categories.

Death Spirits

Death Spirits, spirits of death, carry an innate unease that is not simply modern squeamishness. Our ancestors felt it too. These spirits serve necessary functions, and that necessity makes them powerful allies when approached correctly, at the right moment, for the right purpose.

Death Spirits tend to fall into two types:

  1. Transition spirits: those involved in the dying process itself, easing the soul from one state to the next.
  2. Guardians: those who preside over the realms of the dead, rule cemeteries and cremation grounds, and keep the dead where they belong.

Many guardian spirits of death's threshold go unnamed deliberately. As with spirits of disease, there is a long tradition of avoiding their true names lest they be summoned unintentionally. Epithets and euphemisms serve instead. Hades literally means "the unseen one." When even that grew too familiar, he was called Polydegmon ("the hospitable one," because all are eventually welcome in his realm) or Pluton ("wealth," since ultimately he possesses everyone and everything).

Guardians of the Dead & the Cemetery Gates

  • Baron Samedi and La Grande Brigitte: leaders of the Vodoun Ghede spirits
  • Dongyue Dadi, Lord of Tai Shan (China)
  • Erishkigal / Lamashtu (Mesopotamia)
  • Hades and Persephone (Greece)
  • Hel (Norse)
  • Kali, Shiva, Yama (Hindu)
  • Mictlantecuhtli and Mictecacíhuatl: the Aztec Lord and Lady of the Dead
  • Oya (Yoruba)
  • Osiris (Egypt)
  • Yambe-Akka (Saami)

These guardians may be petitioned to protect the souls of the dead, to maintain order among the departed, and, when appropriate, to grant access to the spirits of the dead.

Psychopomps

Psychopomp comes from the Greek for "conductor of souls." These spirits specialize in guiding souls between the land of the living and the land of the dead. They also serve as sponsors and protectors for shamans who travel between realms.

You may recognize a psychopomp in dreams or visions by the objects they carry: a key (threshold crossing), a cutting instrument (severing ties with the living), and/or a torch (illuminating the path ahead). The Etruscan psychopomp Culso, for instance, arrives at death holding scissors in one hand, to sever the soul's connection to the living world, and a blazing torch in the other, to light the way into whatever comes next.

Psychopomps Across Traditions

  • Gabriel, Azrael, and the unnamed Angel of Death (Jewish)
  • Anubis, Hathor, Wepwawet (Egypt)
  • Baron Samedi, Baron La Croix, Baron Cimetière (Vodoun)
  • Culso (Etruscan)
  • The Valkyries and Freya (Norse)
  • Giltinė (Lithuanian)
  • Hekate and Hermes (Greece)
  • Jizō (Japan)
  • Mother Holle (German)

Animal Psychopomps

Animals serve as psychopomps as well. Dogs, jackals, and canines are the most widely attested, including Anubis (jackal), Kali (jackal), Hekate (dog), and Wepwawet (wolf). Other animal psychopomps include butterflies, snakes, and birds, particularly ravens, crows, hornbills, and frigatebirds.

If any of these animals appear spontaneously at a funeral or death rite, it is considered deeply auspicious: the soul's escort has arrived. Their presence is meant to comfort and reassure the living that the crossing is underway.

Psychopomps may be petitioned by either the dying person or by their loved ones, to ease the passage and ensure a safe transition.
