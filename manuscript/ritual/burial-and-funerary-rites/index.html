The full Hellenic funerary rite sequence, from preparing and burying the deceased through the ninth-day, thirtieth-day, and first-anniversary honors.

Quantities

Preparation of the Deceased:
Warm water or wine, for washing the body
Scented oil or myrrh, for anointing
Laurel or olive leaves
White linen or bright white cloth, to cover the body
Wreath of gold, silver, bronze, or seasonal flowers, for the head
White linen cloth (Kalyptra), to cover the face
Ribbons and jewellery
A coin (Obolos), for the mouth

Lying in Repose:
Bowl of water at the entrance, for hand washing
Honey cake (Melopita) or gingerbread, for visitors
Origanum, olive branches, and four vines wrapped with ribbon, for beneath the casket

Burial Ceremony:
Vessel of khoes (libations)
Pipers
Khoe of red wine, water, incense of styrax, mint, and dry laurel (Hymn to Hermes Khthonios)
Khoe of red wine, incense of styrax, seasonal fruits, narcissus, and pomegranate (Prayer to Plouton)
Khoe of red wine and water, incense of frankincense, honey cakes (Decomposition of Our Elements)
Khoe of red wine (Farewell to the Deceased)
Olive or parsley wreath, for the chest
Prized possessions of the deceased
Khoe of red wine, water, oil, honey, and milk, with incense of frankincense, myrrh, and dry laurel, per funeral speech
Olive branches, to cover the grave
Basin of perfumed water with myrrh, for hand washing

Perideipnon (Funeral Supper):
Fragrant oils or incenses
A bowl, for gathering fallen crumbs

Trita (3rd Day, if separate from burial):
Khoe of red wine, incense of styrax, seasonal fruits, narcissus, and pomegranate
Basin of scented water with myrrh

Enata (9th Day):
Flowers, boiled barley, and pomegranate
Khoes of red wine, water, and honey
Incense of laurel leaves, cedar, and pine
Libation of red wine and incense of saffron (Prayer to Hekate)
Basin of scented water with myrrh

Triakas (30th Day):
Flowers, honey cake (Melopita) or milk cake (Galatopita)
Khoes of red wine and hydromel
Incense of dry laurel leaves, cedar, and pine
Basin of scented water with myrrh

Eniausia (1st Anniversary):
Flowers, boiled barley, and pomegranate
Khoes of red wine, olive oil, milk, and honey
Khoe of red wine, dry fruits and nuts (excluding pulses), incense of dry laurel leaves

Directions

  1. Preparation of the Deceased:
  2. At the moment of death, close the eyes of the deceased.
  3. Wash the body with warm water or wine, then rub it with scented oil or myrrh.
  4. Place laurel or olive leaves on the deceased and cover the body with white linen or bright white cloth.
  5. Place a wreath of gold, silver, bronze, or seasonal flowers on the head.
  6. Cover the face with the white linen cloth known as the Kalyptra.
  7. Decorate the covered body with ribbons and jewellery.
  8. Place a coin (the Obolos) inside the mouth as the offering to Charon Peramataris.
    Lying in Repose:
  9. On the day after death, move the body to the chamber where it will lie in repose for twenty-four hours.
  10. Place a bowl of water at the entrance so visitors can wash their hands as they leave.
  11. Arrange the coffin with the feet facing the entrance.
  12. Place origanum, olive branches, and four ribbon-wrapped vines beneath the casket.
  13. Hold the lament in measured tones around the coffin and offer honey cake or gingerbread to those who arrive.
    The Funeral Procession:
  14. On the third day after death, transport the deceased to the burial or cremation site.
  15. Lead the procession with a woman holding the vessel of khoes, followed by the pipers, the deceased, the family, and then friends and acquaintances.
    The Burial:
  16. At the graveside, before burial or cremation, have a family member invoke Hermes Psychopompos.
  17. Recite the Hymn to Hermes Khthonios, offering a khoe of red wine, water, incense of styrax, mint, and dry laurel.
  18. Recite the Prayer to Plouton, offering a khoe of red wine, incense of styrax, seasonal fruits, narcissus, and pomegranate.
  19. Recite the Decomposition of Our Elements, offering a khoe of red wine and water and incense of frankincense with honey cakes.
  20. Say farewell to the deceased three times, offering a khoe of red wine each time.
  21. Place an olive or parsley wreath on the chest of the deceased and arrange their prized possessions around the body.
  22. Entomb the deceased face up, with the head facing east or west.
  23. Deliver funeral speeches; after each, pour a khoe of red wine, water, oil, honey, and milk with incense of frankincense, myrrh, and dry laurel, then break the khoe vessels on the grave mound.
  24. Cover the grave with olive branches.
  25. Wash hands in a basin of perfumed water and myrrh before leaving, then empty the basin into a small ditch west of the grave while saying the purification words.
    Perideipnon (Funeral Supper):
  26. Hold a dinner in honor of the deceased and purify the atmosphere by burning fragrant oils or incenses.
  27. After dinner, gather any fallen crumbs into a bowl and sprinkle them atop the grave.
    Trita (3rd Day, if not the burial day):
  28. Recite the Prayer to Plouton again, with the same offerings as the burial.
  29. Wash hands in a basin of scented water and myrrh, then empty it into the ditch west of the grave with the purification words.
    Enata (9th Day):
  30. Offer flowers, boiled barley, and pomegranate, with khoes of red wine, water, and honey.
  31. Burn incense of laurel leaves, cedar, and pine.
  32. Recite the Prayer to Hekate, offering a libation of red wine and incense of saffron.
  33. Wash hands in a basin of scented water and myrrh, then empty it as before.
    Triakas (30th Day):
  34. Offer flowers and honey cake or milk cake, with khoes of red wine and hydromel.
  35. Recite the Hymn to Hermes Khthonios again, burning incense of dry laurel leaves, cedar, and pine.
  36. Wash hands in a basin of scented water and myrrh, then empty it as before. This ceremony marks the end of mourning.
    The Tomb:
  37. Decorate the tomb with columns inscribed with images and sayings honoring the deceased.
  38. Plant flowers and place vases of flowers on the tombstone.
    Eniausia (1st Anniversary):
  39. Offer flowers, boiled barley, and pomegranate, with khoes of red wine, olive oil, milk, and honey.
  40. Recite the Prayer to Gaia, offering a khoe of red wine with dry fruits and nuts (excluding pulses) and incense of dry laurel leaves.
  41. From this day forward, honor the deceased yearly on their birthday and during community festivals for the dead, such as the day of Khoes during the Anthesteria.

The full Hellenic burial and funerary tradition, as preserved and adapted by the modern Labrys Devotional Community from sources documented on funerary pottery dating back roughly 2,800 years and from classical references in Thucydides, Plato, Aischylos, Pausanias, and Aeschines. The rites are considered Just, Lawful, and Sacred, and to deny the prescribed burial honours to the deceased is regarded as hubris.

The rites address the chthonic gods who govern the soul's passage and the earth that receives the body: Hermes Psychopompos as guide of souls, Plouton (Hades) as ruler of the Underworld, Persephone as queen of the dead, Hekate Khthonia as torch-bearer of the wayside, Charon Peramataris as ferryman across the rivers of the dead, and Gaia as the mother who receives the body home.

Hymn to Hermes Khthonios

Recited at the burial and again at the Triakas.

O' Hermes who dwells on Kokytos'
necessary path of no return,
who guides the souls of mortals to Hades.
Child of Dionysos, the Bacchic dancer,
and, the Kore from Paphos, Aphrodite
of the curving lashes.
You who serves in the holy house of Persephone,
as a guide to the ill-fated souls of the earth,
with whom you descend at the fateful hour.
Bearer of the sacred staff of sleep, who brings rest to everything,
and again, in turn, awakens those who slumber,
for, to you, the goddess Persephone has granted
the honour of guiding human souls
in their relentless and eternal march
into wide Tartarus.
O' God, may you guide with your staff, the soul of [name of deceased],
who is a child of Ouranos and Gaia,
Guide them to the Elysian Fields (at burial) or the Blissful Fields (at Triakas),
the pure place with wondrous light,
and filled with joyful voices and dance,
with hushed sacred sounds and holy images.

Prayer to Plouton

Recited at the burial and again at the Trita, if separate.

O' Plouton, who holds the keys to the earth,
Zeus Khthonios, wielder of the sceptre.
You who reigns over mortals through death.
You alone award deeds, revealed and unrevealed,
You who exults in the sacred venerations.
I call on You to benevolently receive [name of deceased] in your domain.

The Decomposition of our Elements

Recited at the burial.

Fire lives the death of Earth
and Air lives the death of Fire,
Water lives the death of Air
and Earth, the death of Water.
The death of Earth is to become Water,
the death of Water is to become Air
and that of Air to become Fire and of Fire to become Aether.

Saying Farewell to the Deceased

Recited three times at the burial.

Farewell, beloved [name of deceased],
you will be in our memories throughout this life.
We shall commemorate and honour you
as you deserve.
Mighty Persephone, receive [name of deceased]
all that is beautiful flows unto you.
Beloved [name of deceased], may your memory be everlasting.

Prayer to Hekate

Recited at the Enata (9th day).

I call upon Hekate Khthonia, the saffron-robed,
who cares for the souls of the dead.
Queen and key-bearer of the whole world.
Khaire Hekate Prothyraia (of the entrance), Almighty
Maiden Enodia (of the wayside),
O' Goddess, May you lead the soul of [name of the deceased]
on the illuminated paths of the Gods.

Prayer to Gaia

Recited at the Eniausia (1st anniversary).

Goddess Gaia, mother of all Gods and mortals.
All-nurturing, all-giving, all-fulfilling,
seat of the undying world.
Immortal, venerable, multi-formed maiden,
many-flowered Goddess of the happy fate.
O' Goddess Gaia, we have come to give thanks,
for the hospitality you extend to the body of [name of deceased].

Notes

All offerings and khoês for the dead are never consumed by the living.

These rites stem from the original traditional practices of Hellenic polytheism, with information drawn from funerary pottery dating back as far as 2,800 years and from classical sources including Thucydides (Histories 2.34.1), Plato (Laws 12.947b and 959), Aischylos (Seven against Thebes 1030), Pausanias (Messinians 13.3), and Aeschines (Against Ktesiphon 235). The version recorded here is the form practised by the modern Labrys Devotional Community.

The traditional burial location was a family or community plot; in the modern context, burial takes place at a public cemetery operating under the supervision of local authorities, regardless of religious affiliation.

Key terms:

  • Khoê / Khoês: a libation poured for the dead or chthonic gods, never consumed.
  • Obolos: the coin placed in the mouth of the deceased as fare for Charon.
  • Kalyptra: the white cowl that covers the face of the deceased.
  • Melopita: honey cake. Galatopita: milk cake.
  • Hydromel: a mixture of honey and water, mead-like.
  • Perideipnon / Necrodeipnon: the funeral supper held after the burial.

The four ceremonies of remembrance, Trita (3rd day), Enata (9th day), Triakas (30th day, end of mourning), and Eniausia (1st anniversary), structure the year of mourning. After the Eniausia, annual honours continue on the deceased's birthday and at community festivals for the dead, especially the day of Khoes within Anthesteria.
