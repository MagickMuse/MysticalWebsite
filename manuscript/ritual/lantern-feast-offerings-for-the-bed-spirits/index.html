🏮Lantern Feast Offerings for the Bed Spirits

A formal offering ritual to Lord Ch'uang-kung and Lady Ch'uang-mu, the Chinese presiding spirits of the bed, performed on the final day of the lunar year and/or the day following the full moon in the first lunar month (the Lantern Feast). The most auspicious candle is obtained from the dragon lantern processions. Offerings of cakes, fruits, tea, wine, fertility fruits, and red-dyed eggs are presented at or beside the bed.

Quantities

Cakes
Fruits (including fertility fruits such as pomegranate, dates, or longan)
Eggs dyed red
A cup of fine tea (for the Lady)
A glass of wine (for the Lord)
A candle (ideally a stub from the dragon lantern procession, otherwise any candle)
A tray (optional)

Directions

  1. On the final day of the lunar year or the day following the Lantern Feast full moon, arrange offerings directly on the bed, on a tray placed on the bed, or on a table beside the bed.
  2. Use the dragon lantern candle stub, or light another candle, at the edge of the bed.
  3. Present the offerings ceremoniously: cakes and fruits on the bed or tray, tea for the Lady, wine for the Lord, and red-dyed eggs and fertility fruits on the bedside table.
  4. Petition the Lord and Lady explicitly for what you desire.
  5. Couples traditionally perform this working together.

The Lantern Feast falls on the first full moon of the Chinese lunar new year, marking the official close of the new year festivities. The dragon lantern processions through the streets are a public expression of collective prayer for abundance and new life, and the candles used to illuminate the dragons carry that accumulated intention.

For those seeking fertility, procuring one of the ends of these candles is considered the most powerful material available for this working. It has been charged by communal celebration, handled by many hands in a spirit of joy and renewal, and associated with the serpentine body of the dragon, a creature whose energy in Chinese cosmology is synonymous with life-force and fertility.

The pairing of offerings reflects the gendered nature of the Lord and Lady: wine for the masculine, tea for the feminine. Red-dyed eggs appear across cultures as symbols of new life held in potential, and their placement at the bedside links the offering directly to the hope of conception.
