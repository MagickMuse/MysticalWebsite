What is Magick?

Ancestral Magick

Magick may feel exciting to some and controversial to others. Those new to the subject may find it strange or even a bit disorienting at first. Most religious institutions frame it as supernatural and dangerous; secular culture frames it as irrational and naive. And yet, magick has a timeless appeal, and records of its practice stretch back as far as records exist.

As Gordon White explains in Star.Ships, the first structures humans built as a species were not houses. They were temples, where hunter-gatherer ancestors very likely went to commune with spirits and engage in magickal practice. Whatever the living may have told you about magick, your ancestors practiced it, and they will help you practice it too.

Defining Magick

Magick is the pitter-patter in your heart when seeing someone that catches your fancy, or the butterflies in your stomach that give you a thrill when your loved ones are near. Magick is the hair standing on end, warning you that something is about to happen. These things can be explained away with biological reasoning, but that makes them no less magickal.

Fantasy author Arthur C. Clarke's Third Law states: "Any sufficiently advanced technology is indistinguishable from magick." Magick is simply the name for things science does not understand yet. That does not make them any less real. Whether journeying within oneself to follow the link between self and environment, or altering consciousness to reach other planes, the only difference is the destination. They both deal with the realm of the possible.

Place your hand on your heart. That pulse you feel under your hand? The lub-dub of the heart's chambers working in concert is one of the hallmarks of life. Life implies biological processes, and in humans, they start with electrical impulses. As much as science understands those biological processes and the human body, even surgeons admit they do not comprehend what makes the heart beat, or what causes this complex machine to stop working. Witches do. It is magick, plain and simple, while also being terrifyingly complex.

Art and Science

When asking a witch what magick is, one is likely to get several mixed answers. The most common is Aleister Crowley's definition: "Magick is the art and science of creating change in accordance with will."

The breadth of the definition is part of its appeal. There are as many ways to do magick as there are people practicing it. Some require deep study or training with an adept; others are entirely self-directed. Some rely primarily on visualization; others leverage physical materials. And the mundane actions that support a desired outcome are their own form of magick.

Magick can also be understood as prayer in 3D. Where ordinary prayer says "please make my life better," magick involves choosing a specific outcome and reinforcing it through corresponding actions. In the example of landing a dream job, the mundane work includes updating a résumé, activating a network connection, researching the company, and preparing for the interview. Magickal work identifies which additional actions and correspondences align with the desired outcome and layers them in.

Crowley did not put "art and science" in there by mistake. The art of magick is entirely separate from the science of magick. The art of magick is the difference between a painting by one of the masters and a paint-by-numbers copy. Creating magick has art involved, in the same way painting does. Anyone can paint and have fun with it, just as anyone can recite a rhyming couplet. What makes the difference between a rhyming couplet and magick is the spirit of the person reciting them.

Are the spells cast in popular witchcraft media real? Not yet, no. But they could be. Magick requires thought and intention, among other things. Saying the words is not enough. Thought and intent must be put into them.

Egregors and Thoughtforms

There is a concept known in magickal circles as an egregor (pronounced E-gr-g-Or). An egregor is a thoughtform that has been thought of by so many people, for so many years, in such a varied population, that it becomes its own autonomous entity. The idea is that figures like Santa Claus have been thought of for long enough that they became true spirits. They may not occupy a physical place on this plane, but they exist in spirit.

Magickal spells that exist in popular culture could be used in spells by witches, but just because something was on television does not make it real. The only thing real in pop culture magick is usually the dry ice.

The Role of Feeling

Magick should make you feel something. You should feel something before you start the spell, while creating it, and after you have finished the spell. If there is no feeling attached, there can be no magick.

This does not mean you have to experience extreme passion to work practical magick, but there needs to be some feeling to tap into. If you do not care enough about the issue, it is like a chocolate bunny in the spring: it looks good, but it is hollow. One can be passionate about a subject without being hysterical.

Making Magick

There is a disconnect between the brain and the heart when it comes to making magick happen. It comes from watching magick on TV and reading about it in books. New witches see fictional characters reciting "magick words" and something amazing results with sparkly special effects. In real life, there are no light cues or faint chimes to signal that a spell was effective.

What televised magick leaves out is the hair-raising knowing that comes with real magick. The tingle in your palms that says the energy was raised. You can have all of the aesthetics right and still not raise any energy. Ritual theater (the costumes, masks, robes, and the like) will only get you so far. The magick comes from you.

Psi-Magick

Psi-magick is magick worked solely with the mind, no candles, crystals, tools, or anything else. Many practitioners consider it the most advanced form of magick because it requires complete mastery of focus, visualization, and energy manipulation without physical aids. The practitioner becomes both the tool and the source.

What is a Magick Spell?

At its simplest, a spell is a deliberate, structured act of directing magickal force (heka) toward a specific outcome. The ingredient that elevates a sequence of actions from mere routine into genuine spellwork is the practitioner: your focused intention, your clearly defined goal, and the depth of feeling behind it. Precision matters. A sharply defined objective, a vivid mental picture of the result, and real emotional investment all increase the likelihood that the working will succeed.

You Are the Irreplaceable Component

Spells exist in an almost limitless variety. Some are elaborate and theatrical, some are startling, and some look so ordinary you would never guess magick was involved. Countless styles exist, each with its own preferred ingredients and methods, and if one approach does not resonate, another will. Uncomfortable with open flame? Build your spell around an enchanted bath. No bathtub? Brew a potion or work with knotted cord. If a particular ingredient is impossible to source, alternatives almost always exist. Many of the most potent magickal materials hide in plain sight among everyday kitchen staples.

Magickal energy is persistent and pervasive, and spells are the structured channels through which it is aimed. Yet out of every element in any spell you will ever cast, exactly one is truly irreplaceable: you. Your personal energy is the binding force, the animating spark that turns a collection of actions, words, and materials into a living magickal working.

Naturally Charged Objects

Certain substances found in nature arrive already saturated with magickal power. Iron, menstrual blood, salt, and specific botanicals all radiate their own energetic charge without any preparation or consecration. At the other end of the spectrum, some magickal tools, a wand, a ritual blade, a consecrated mirror, require painstaking spellwork over an extended period before they reach the level of power needed for serious use. The most heavily charged objects shoulder a significant portion of the work in a given spell, though no working is ever entirely effortless.

The Morality of Magick

Even practitioners who work with magick regularly sometimes hold limiting beliefs about how it operates. One common belief is that magick cannot be performed for personal gain, an idea likely popularized by the television show Charmed and not rooted in any serious magickal tradition. Another is the principle that magick must "harm none."

Harming none sounds straightforward but is nearly impossible in practice. Doing magick to obtain a job is simultaneously doing magick to prevent someone else from getting it. Other candidates may want or need it more. Whether intended or not, some degree of harm is always a possibility. And yet the world does not collapse from this. The person who does not get that particular job continues applying and may ultimately find something better suited to them.

The belief that magick must "harm none" is not rooted in rigorous moral philosophy. It tends to arise from a deeper wound: the belief that one cannot have what one wants because one does not deserve it. This wound has many sources, including organized religion, family dynamics, and cultural messaging. You can have what you want. You can have good things, you can be loved, and when that truth is genuinely internalized, the obsessive pull of desire begins to loosen.

For those still concerned about the ethics of working magick for personal gain, ancestral magick offers a natural moral framework. When petitioning the ancestors, one stands before the eldest elders and states desires aloud. This requires clarity and conviction. Whatever is being asked for must feel genuinely warranted. The ancestors' attention is not to be wasted on requests made in bad faith, repeated for problems of one's own making, or directed against others without real justification. The relationship itself cultivates discernment.
